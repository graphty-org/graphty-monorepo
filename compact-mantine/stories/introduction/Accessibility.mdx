# Accessibility

The library targets WCAG 2.2 AA, and the work is done inside the components rather than left as
props you must remember.

## What you get without asking

- **Every control is a real control.** Fields are inputs, toggles are checkboxes or switches,
  segmented options are radios, the tree is an ARIA tree, the advanced button is a button, so
  keyboard behaviour, focus order and disabled semantics come from the platform.
- **Keyboard focus is always visible, and pointer focus is not**, so a dense surface stays quiet
  under the mouse without giving up the focus indicator. The ring is Figma's 1px blue ring: inside
  a field, outside a button (see [Focus and motion](?path=/docs/foundations-focus-and-motion--docs)).
- **Composite widgets have one Tab stop and arrow keys inside.** The toolbar, the navigation
  rail, the shortcuts sheet's tabs, the tree, the page list, the segmented control and the
  alignment matrix all move with the arrow keys, Home and End.
- **State is announced, not only drawn.** Expanded, checked, pressed, selected, disabled, busy and
  current are all exposed as ARIA in addition to colour.
- **Colour is never the only signal.** An advanced button whose settings have changed says so in
  its accessible name; a section with non-default values does the same.
- **Charts are one named image with a hidden table behind them**, so a screen reader announces
  the chart and can then read every value, rather than a run of anonymous bars. Give every chart
  a `label`.
- **Results that arrive late are announced.** Give a row `busy` and it becomes a polite live
  region; while `busy` is true the announcement is held back, so the reader hears the finished
  result once rather than every frame of it. A row never given `busy` stays silent, because most
  rows hold a value the reader set themselves.
- **Hover-revealed controls also appear on focus**, stay in the document and stay in the tab
  order.

## Where this goes beyond Figma

Kept on purpose, where Figma's own editor does less: tooltips stay linked with
`aria-describedby`; the layer tree, find results and quick actions are focusable and announced;
the context menu also opens from the keyboard (Shift+F10 and the ContextMenu key); Escape closes
the shortcuts sheet; the resize handle takes Shift+arrow, Home and End; and the quick actions
palette is a named dialog with a combobox and a list box.

## Contrast: exact Figma, or AA

Figma's own contrast falls short of AA in a few places: secondary text, placeholder text, field
and checkbox edges, the focus ring on white, and white text on the blue, red and green fills.
The default theme is exact Figma. Create the theme with `highContrast` to raise exactly those:

```tsx
<MantineProvider theme={createCompactTheme({ highContrast: true })}>
```

It changes colour tokens only; nothing moves or resizes. Secondary text goes from 50% to 55%
black (4.7:1 on white), checkbox, switch and field edges reach 3:1 (fields gain a 1px inside
edge), the selected segment of a segmented control gets a 3:1 edge, and the placeholder, focus
ring, links and filled buttons each move to the next darker colour in Figma's palette. Dividers
stay as Figma draws them. The **Contrast** switch in the toolbar shows every story both ways.

## What is still yours to do

- **Name what has no visible label.** An icon-only button, a segmented control of pictures and a
  chart each need a `label` or an `aria-label`; the components take it and put it in the right
  place.
- **Name pairs a reader must hear together.** `DataRow` draws `value` verbatim and gives the pair
  no accessible name of its own.
- **Keep one `Tooltip.Group`.** Nesting a second one breaks the warm hand-off between tooltips,
  but not their accessibility.

## Checking your own pages

Storybook runs axe-core against every story in this Storybook; the **Accessibility** panel under
a story lists any violation. Every component also carries a root `data-testid` holding its own
name in kebab case (`advanced-button`, `metric-row`, `popout-panel`), and most are best found in
tests by role and name, as a screen reader would.
