# Focus group: the first session

Six simulated participants looked at the start screen of the clickable skeleton and then at
the Les Miserables sample opened from it, and discussed it over three rounds. Round 1 asked
what they saw and how the screen is organized. Round 2 asked them to respond to each other
and say what was missing. Round 3 asked what would make them quit, what would make them
switch, and the one thing they would change.

Participants (persona files in study/personas/):

- explorer-elena -- Elena, curious explorer with an accounts and integrations export
- class-project-student -- Dev, student following a tutorial, deadline Friday
- nonprofit-operations-analyst -- donor and organization export, reports to a director
- alert-reviewer -- Nadia, financial alert reviewer, one account number per alert
- data-journalist -- Ruth, unpublished names, must defend every number to an editor
- recipe-recipient -- Tom, receives finished files from a colleague, needs a slide

## Themes

Counts are participants out of six. "Unprompted" means raised in round 1, before anyone
heard the others. Rounds 2 and 3 were steered ("what is missing", "what would make you
quit") and participants answered each other, so agreement there is weaker evidence.
Severity is Nielsen 0-4. These are simulated participants: treat every finding as a
hypothesis for a real session, and treat single voices as leads, not findings.

### 1. The usage-data banner contradicts the privacy promise and is dismissed unread -- severity 3, 6 of 6

The start screen says "Files are read on this computer and never uploaded" and shows
"Local only", then a banner covering a third of the screen asks to share usage data.

- Dismissed without reading, unprompted: all six in round 1 ("No thanks" to make it go
  away). That alone means the banner collects no meaningful consent.
- The contradiction itself, unprompted: Ruth only ("whose usage, and sent where?"). Tom
  raised a related worry unprompted: the banner text mentions "Claude Code sessions", a term
  he read as something that might be reading his genes.
- Joined in round 2 after Ruth: nonprofit analyst, Nadia, Dev, Tom; Elena in round 3.
- What they asked for: one plain sentence stating what leaves the computer, and "nothing"
  if nothing does (Ruth, nonprofit analyst, Elena, Tom). Nadia: do not let it sit above
  the first action.
- Dissent: none.
- Discount: the contradiction framing is Ruth's, adopted by everyone else; the independent
  evidence is the unanimous dismissal plus two unprompted objections. The internal term in
  the banner copy is a defect on its own regardless of discussion.

### 2. Algorithm names are unreadable to every participant -- severity 3, 6 of 6

PageRank, Louvain, Betweenness, Density and Link prediction mean nothing to anyone here.

- Unprompted: Elena, Dev, Nadia, Ruth, Tom. Nonprofit analyst in round 2.
- Plain-word translations offered: "most connected" for PageRank, "groups who know each
  other" for Louvain (nonprofit analyst); "who matters" and "clusters" (Tom); "these are the
  ones most things connect to" (Elena).
- Nuance, not dissent: Dev wants the technical name kept beside the plain one ("this is
  called PageRank, a kind of centrality") so he can cite it; Ruth wants the method stated
  so she can defend it. So: plain words first, technical name kept, not replaced.
- Boundary note: an algorithm's plain-language name and one-line description are catalog
  data that every consumer needs, so they belong to graphty-element, not app chrome.

## Where they disagreed

- **Open a finished example, or start from your own data?**
  - Finished first: Dev (to see what "done" looks like), Elena (to judge if it is worth the
    afternoon), Tom (finished is the only thing he ever opens).
  - Own data first: Ruth (names in a table, counted), nonprofit analyst (her own export
    arriving), Nadia (one account, nothing pre-run on the transactions sample).
  - Common ground reached in round 3: a finished example is acceptable if it shows what was
    done to it. Dev wants the steps in order to copy them; Ruth wants them to defend the
    result; Elena explicitly does not want steps, only a plain statement of the outcome.
    So the steps should be available, not imposed.
- **Table or drag-and-drop?** Ruth wants a table to count; Elena would drag the file on and
  see. Both are first-step preferences, not conflicts in the product.
- **Who reads the methods?** Tom says the middle steps are his colleague's job; he wants
  only "which dots matter in English". The explanation needs a reader's-level summary as
  well as the method.

## Group-think to discount

- The banner contradiction (theme 1) was one participant's framing, repeated by five. Keep
  the unanimous unread dismissal and the internal-term defect as the hard evidence.
- "Just say what it means" became a chorus in round 3, with participants citing each other
  rather than the screen. Theme 2 stands on its five unprompted voices, not the chorus.
- The import count (theme 5) is quoted from Ruth's wording by Dev and echoed by the
  nonprofit analyst. Real need for at least Ruth, Dev and the nonprofit analyst; inflated
  for Elena and Tom, who adopted it.
- Mock fidelity: the Les Miserables sample opens heavily loaded, which inflates the left
  list and the "twenty things" reaction. Rerun the list finding on a freshly imported file
  before sizing it.

## What to carry forward

1. Replace the usage banner with one plain sentence about what leaves the computer; remove
   the internal term from its copy; do not let it cover the first actions.
2. Show plain-language algorithm names with the technical name beside them, sourced from
   graphty-element's algorithm catalog.
3. Add a one-sentence reading of the current coloring above or beside the legend, and a
   per-node "why" that states the measure and the count it was taken over.
4. Group the left list by kind (data, results, notes, folders) and fix the truncated and
   jargon labels.
5. Verify that the import states report counts and skipped rows; if they do, test them
   directly next round with the nonprofit analyst, Ruth and Dev.
6. Test search placement with Nadia and Ruth as a first-click task.
