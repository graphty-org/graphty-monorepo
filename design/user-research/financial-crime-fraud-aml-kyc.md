# Financial crime investigators: fraud rings, anti-money-laundering, beneficial ownership, crypto tracing and insurance fraud

## 1. Summary

This population covers the people who decide whether a customer, a claim, a provider or a set of transactions is criminal, and who prove where the money went. Inside banks, payment firms and insurers they are transaction-monitoring alert reviewers, anti-money-laundering (AML) investigators and suspicious activity report (SAR) writers, fraud-strategy analysts, know-your-customer (KYC), beneficial-ownership and sanctions analysts, receiving-bank fraud teams chasing scam proceeds, and insurance special investigation unit (SIU) investigators. Outside them are blockchain investigators at crypto exchanges and in police crypto units, analysts at government financial intelligence units (FIUs) and tax agencies who read many institutions' reports at once, forensic accountants who rebuild money flows from subpoenaed bank records, healthcare program-integrity analysts who look for fraudulent providers in claims data, and public procurement integrity analysts at competition authorities, audit institutions and anti-corruption research groups who screen public tenders for bid-rigging groups and corruption risk.

They use graph software for three jobs. **Linking**: finding everyone who shares a phone, device, card, address, account, garage or patient, then reading the connected group as a possible ring. **Flow**: following money through a directed, timestamped multigraph of transactions, hop by hop, often across institutions or blockchains, and, when a court or victim asks, allocating how much of a specific deposit reached a specific account under a legal tracing rule. **Structure**: following an ownership chain up to the people who own 25 percent or more of a company, or testing whether sanctioned owners hold 50 percent or more in aggregate. **Collusion**: linking firms that bid on the same public tenders, finding tight, exclusive groups of co-bidders year by year, and reading them against price screens and the firms' ownership ties. Their output is not a picture but a decision (clear, escalate, freeze, file, exit, refer) recorded in a case file under legal deadlines and audit, and often a filing or court exhibit whose numbers must be reproducible.

## 2. Population

**Job titles seen in postings and vendor material.** Transaction monitoring analyst (Level 1, "L1"), AML or financial crimes investigator (Level 2), SAR writer, FIU analyst, fraud or fraud-strategy analyst, KYC, enhanced due diligence (EDD) and KYB analyst, sanctions screening analyst, money laundering reporting officer (MLRO, UK) or BSA officer (US), SIU investigator, blockchain investigator, forensic accountant, certified fraud examiner, program-integrity analyst. Bank of America and Guidehouse postings describe investigator / SAR writer roles that work alerts end to end ([Bank of America](https://careers.bankofamerica.com/en-us/job-detail/24017594/global-financial-crimes-gfc-investigator-multiple-locations), [Guidehouse](https://themuse.com/jobs/guidehouse/financial-crimes-compliance-investigator-4f668c)). KYC postings ask analysts to "unpick ownership and control chains, including nominee, trust and multi-jurisdictional arrangements" ([Capitex](https://apply.workable.com/capitex/jobs/view/A1643B1944.md)). Crypto.com's blockchain investigator posting asks for familiarity with "movement of funds on UTXO and account-based networks" ([Crypto.com posting](https://jobs.lever.co/crypto/611acd9c-c98f-486b-8d13-984791d6a83d)); a Coinbase investigator posting includes responding to "legal orders compelling Coinbase to provide evidence" (read through search summary).

**Sectors.** Retail and commercial banks; payment processors and e-money firms; crypto exchanges and other virtual asset service providers (VASPs); blockchain-analytics vendors (Chainalysis, TRM Labs, Elliptic) that also run investigations; payment-scheme operators (Pay.UK with Vocalink); insurers (motor, property, workers' compensation, health); industry utilities (Cifas, the UK Insurance Fraud Bureau, NICB and Verisk ClaimSearch in the US); FIUs (FinCEN, UK NCA, and the many FIUs on UNODC's goAML system); tax agencies (HMRC's Connect team); Medicare and Medicaid program-integrity contractors and state Medicaid Fraud Control Units (MFCUs); forensic accounting and litigation-support firms; and the managed-service firms that staff alert backlogs.

**Size.** No credible headcount for the whole population was found. Indirect evidence: the UK FIU received over 57,000 defense-against-money-laundering requests in 2023/24 ([NCA UKFIU chapter 3](https://www.nationalcrimeagency.gov.uk/who-we-are/publications/776-ukfiu-chapter-3-understanding-damls-and-datfs/file)); UK firms offboarded 238,396 customers for suspected mule activity in 2025 ([FCA review](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/multi-firm-reviews/money-mules-activity-cashing-out-findings)); the Coalition Against Insurance Fraud's 2024 benchmarking of 35 carriers found SIU staffing grew 0.5 percent ([Insurance Business](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/us/news/technology/automated-tools-now-make-45-of-siu-referrals--investigators-havent-kept-pace-596528.aspx)); Chainalysis claims "1,500+ organizations" as Reactor customers and "2,000+ crypto investigators" in its community (vendor, [Reactor page](https://www.chainalysis.com/product/reactor/)); up to 4,000 HMRC users have access to Connect and about 250 analysts specialize in it (["European Labour Authority" fiche](https://www.ela.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2021-09/Good%20Practice%20Fiche%20-%20UK%20Data%20mining%20tools%20and%20methods%20to%20tackle%20the%20hidden%20economy%20in%20the%20UK.pdf)).

**Public procurement integrity.** Competition authorities screen tender data for bid rigging; Ireland's Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (CCPC) lists Brazil, Denmark, Portugal, Spain, Switzerland, the United States and South Korea as already screening public procurement data while it asks for the legal power to do the same ([CCPC, 2025](https://www.ccpc.ie/news-and-media/news/article/2025/05/14/cracking-down-on-bid-rigging--why-the-ccpc-wants-powers-to-screen-public-procurement-data)). The UK Competition and Markets Authority runs an in-house Bid Rigging Intelligence Tool, piloted with three government departments and "generating live enforcement leads"; the same paper says Korea's BRIAS screening system "is being upgraded to cover joint bidding and inter-firm networks" and that Brazil's Cerebro tool screens for superfluous losing bidders ([CMA, 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/procurement-in-the-national-interest/rigged-bids-real-costs-a-case-for-urgent-action-on-bid-rigging-in-uk-public-procurement)). In Austria the competition authority and eight regional courts of audit, which must examine state and municipal procurement, published a joint checklist for spotting cartel patterns in tender data in 2024 (read through search summary, [AFCA](https://www.bwb.gv.at/en/news/detail/afca-and-eight-regional-courts-of-audit-join-forces-to-uncover-cartels-in-public-sector-tendering-and-present-checklist-for-contracting-authorities-prevention-and-recognition-of-collusion)). Outside government, the Government Transparency Institute in Budapest publishes tender data for 30 European countries, 2011-2025, and tags its work with cartel screening ([GTI](https://www.govtransparency.eu/)), and the Open Contracting Partnership publishes a list of 73 procurement red flags mapped to the Open Contracting Data Standard (OCDS) ([OCP red flags](https://www.open-contracting.org/resources/red-flags-in-public-procurement-a-guide-to-using-data-to-detect-and-mitigate-risks/)) and Cardinal, an open-source library that computes them, including collusion flags such as identical bid prices ([OCP Cardinal](https://www.open-contracting.org/2024/06/12/cardinal-an-open-source-library-to-calculate-public-procurement-red-flags/)). No source counted how many authorities run network screens, as opposed to price and participation screens; only Korea's upgrade is described as network-based in the material read, and no source names the graph or visualization software a screening unit uses.

**How often they touch graph tools.** Daily for L2 investigators and SIU triage staff where a graph tool is deployed (Zurich investigators "switch from the rule-based tool to the graph application" for every escalated case, [Neo4j Zurich story](https://neo4j.com/customer-stories/zurich-insurance/)). Daily and centrally for blockchain investigators: the tracing tool's graph is their main screen. Less often for L1 reviewers (the case queue is their screen) and forensic accountants (spreadsheets first, charts at the end); in batch for fraud-strategy staff and for procurement screening units, which rerun screens over a year or a market of tenders.

**Skill profile.** Investigators: strong domain knowledge, moderate graph literacy ("linked", "hops", "cluster"), little coding; postings ask for Excel and red-flag spotting ([Teal summary](https://www.tealhq.com/job/financial-crime-investigator_7ea1aff6730272e3d301aed85f4a8ad542d60)). FinCEN's analyst description names i2 Analyst's Notebook and "link analysis charts, network diagrams, and timeline visualizations" ([FinCEN](https://fincen.gov/careers/core-career-descriptions/intelligence-research-specialist)). Fraud-strategy and detection engineers: SQL, Python, Cypher. Open Ownership found graph tools such as Linkurious and Neo4j Bloom liked but their "expense and steep learning curve" excluded many users ([Open Ownership](https://www.openownership.org/en/blog/what-graph-visualisation-teaches-us-about-beneficial-ownership/)).

**Team context.** In banks, alert generation, alert management (L1, L2, quality control) and the SAR decision (committee or MLRO) are separate; the FFIEC BSA/AML manual names these components ([FFIEC](https://bsaaml.ffiec.gov/manual/AssessingComplianceWithBSARegulatoryRequirements/04)).

## 3. Personas

### 3.1 Priya Raman -- AML investigator and SAR writer (Level 2), mid-size US bank

- **Tools.** Vendor case manager (Actimize, Oracle FCCM, Verafin); core-banking and wire screens; Excel pivots of 6-12 months of activity; LexisNexis and World-Check; an embedded graph view (Linkurious, Quantexa, a KeyLines panel) where licensed.
- **Data.** Alert payload; 90-365 days of transactions per subject account with amount, direction, channel, counterparty name and account, counterparty bank, timestamp; KYC profile; prior alerts and SARs. Only legs touching her bank's customers are visible (section 6.2).
- **Goals.** File inside 30 days of detection; explain why the activity is suspicious; investigate a network once, not five alerts five times.
- **Frustrations.** Most alerts are false positives (section 6.6); data gathering dominates; hundreds of parallel transactions form a hairball unless summed; the trail goes dark where funds leave the bank unless a 314(b) partner answers; nothing drawn in the graph flows into the narrative.

### 3.2 Marcus Lee -- fraud strategy analyst, digital bank or payments firm

- **Tools.** Neo4j Graph Data Science (weakly connected components, node similarity), notebooks, Bloom or Linkurious for single rings, the rule engine, BI dashboards.
- **Data.** Users, cards, devices, IPs, phones, emails, P2P transfers, chargeback labels; the Neo4j P2P walkthrough has about 33,700 users ([Neo4j part 2](https://neo4j.com/blog/developer/exploring-fraud-detection-neo4j-graph-data-science-part-2/)).
- **Goals.** Find synthetic and mule rings before bust-out; turn rings into rules; measure what a rule finds.
- **Frustrations.** Super-connected identifiers merge innocents into one giant component; investigators reject rings they cannot explain; no clean feedback of dispositions into labels.

### 3.3 Elena Novak -- corporate KYC, beneficial-ownership and sanctions analyst, international bank

- **Tools.** Orbis or Dun and Bradstreet ownership reports, Companies House PSC register, OpenCorporates, Open Ownership, Sayari at larger banks; PowerPoint or Visio when the vendor chart is wrong.
- **Data.** Ownership edges with percentages or bands, voting rights, control types, dates, jurisdictions; screening hits (PEP, sanctions, adverse media).
- **Goals.** Find every natural person owning or controlling 25 percent or more; decide separately whether the customer is blocked because sanctioned persons own 50 percent or more in aggregate; document evidence per edge; spot when a change alters either answer.
- **Frustrations.** Bands not percentages; duplicate person records; chains that stop at a foreign entity; circular ownership; vendors disagree; tools that give one percentage when the two rules need different arithmetic (section 5.6).

### 3.4 Gareth Owen -- SIU investigator, motor insurer (UK, with US differences)

- **Tools.** Claims system, automated fraud scoring that refers claims, Insurance Fraud Register and IFB hub checks, Cifas searches, a link-analysis tool (Linkurious on Neo4j at Zurich Switzerland; Quantexa at Zurich UK; SAS or Verisk elsewhere). A US counterpart adds ClaimSearch and NICB referrals.
- **Data.** Claims, policies, claimants, drivers, passengers, vehicles, addresses, phones, emails, payout accounts, garages, credit-hire firms, solicitors, medical experts, devices and IPs.
- **Goals.** Decide whether a referred claim is isolated or part of a staged-accident network; stop payment where justified; refer to the IFB, police or (US) the state fraud bureau.
- **Frustrations.** Link analysis runs only after referral, so clean-looking ring members are paid ([Xebia](https://xebia.com/articles/how-can-insurers-detect-fraud-rings-before-paying-claims/)); automated tools make 45 percent of referrals while staffing is flat; one shared garage links hundreds of innocent claims.

### 3.5 Tomas Herrera -- blockchain investigator, crypto exchange financial-crimes team (formerly a police crypto unit)

- **Tools.** Chainalysis Reactor or TRM Forensics (Elliptic Investigator and Crystal elsewhere), the exchange's own customer and deposit records, its transaction-monitoring alerts, i2 for law-enforcement partners (Reactor integrates with i2, Cellebrite and Siren, [Reactor page](https://www.chainalysis.com/product/reactor/)).
- **Data.** Public ledgers: Bitcoin-style transactions with many inputs and outputs, account-based chains such as Ethereum, swaps, bridges and mixers. Vendor clusters and attribution labels (Chainalysis claims "180K+ unique real-world counterparties", "27+ blockchains", "300+ bridges and DEXs", vendor). Internal KYC for the exchange's own users.
- **Goals.** Decide whether incoming funds come from a hack, scam, sanctioned party or darknet market; trace funds to the service where they were cashed out; answer law-enforcement orders; file SARs.
- **Frustrations.** Clusters are heuristic and can wrongly collapse (section 5.7); cross-chain hops break simple paths; an exchange's own deposit and sweep addresses add noise; allocation rules (taint) give very different answers.

### 3.6 Amina Diallo -- FIU or tax intelligence analyst

- **Tools.** goAML at many FIUs, a vendor graph tool or i2; at HMRC, Connect (built by BAE Systems on NetReveal, [Wikipedia](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connect_(computer_system)>)).
- **Data.** SARs, STRs, cash-threshold and cross-border reports from thousands of filers, each naming subjects in its own spelling; for tax, HMRC and third-party records (Companies House, Land Registry, electoral roll, card data). Connect holds "22 billion lines of data, and 500 million documents" (ELA fiche).
- **Goals.** Find which reports describe the same people and network; turn a cluster into a case proposal or dissemination to police or tax compliance; run strategic reviews by sector.
- **Frustrations.** Entity resolution across filers; records that may be the same person but cannot be proven so; no single bank's narrative covers the whole network.

### 3.7 Ruth Feldman -- forensic accountant and certified fraud examiner, litigation support

- **Tools.** PDF statement extraction (DocuClipper, Valid8), Excel, a database, i2 or a charting tool for exhibits.
- **Data.** Statements, check images, deposit tickets, wire records and signature cards from many banks, received under subpoena, often as PDFs.
- **Goals.** Build one reconciled transaction database; show sources and uses of funds; say how much of the victim's money reached each asset under the rule the court accepts; produce exhibits that survive cross-examination.
- **Frustrations.** Extraction and transfer matching across banks take most of the time; tracing rules change the answer; every number on an exhibit must tie back to a page of a statement.

### 3.8 Kevin Brooks -- healthcare program-integrity analyst (health insurer SIU or Medicaid program-integrity contractor)

- **Tools.** Claims warehouse, SQL, provider-risk scoring products (LexisNexis markets one that combines Medicaid claims with public records, read through search summary), occasionally a graph tool.
- **Data.** Claims linking patients, rendering and referring providers, pharmacies, diagnoses and procedures, over millions of beneficiaries.
- **Goals.** Find providers who share implausible patient populations, referral loops or pharmacy pairings; refer them to an MFCU, OIG or police with evidence.
- **Frustrations.** Patient-sharing networks are huge bipartite graphs; normal referral hubs look like rings; no public source was found that documents an MFCU running a prescriber-pharmacy-patient graph in practice.

### 3.9 Teodora Ilic -- public procurement integrity analyst (competition authority screening unit, audit institution or anti-corruption research group)

- **Tools.** The national tender portal or its OCDS feed, Python or R notebooks, the authority's own screening system where one exists (BRIAS in Korea, BRIT in the UK, Cerebro in Brazil, per the CMA paper), Orbis or a company register for ownership, Cardinal for standard red flags.
- **Data.** Tender-level records (buyer, product code, reserve price, procedure, date) and bid-level records (bidder, bid price, winner); Georgia's market alone gave nearly 150,000 contracts and nearly 15,000 firms over 2011-2016 ([Wachs and Kertesz 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6658564)), Sweden 170,442 bids by 6,909 bidders on 50,598 tenders over 2010-2015 ([Villamil, Kertesz and Fazekas 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10850496/)). Losing bids are often missing: in the UK they are "not routinely collected or available in a consistent, machine-readable format" (CMA).
- **Goals.** Turn a year of tenders into a short list of firm groups and buyers worth investigating or auditing; explain each flag; check the screen against cases already proven.
- **Frustrations.** Missing losing bids; firm identifiers that do not match the ownership register; screens that are "only suggestive" (Wachs and Kertesz); collusion spread over several buyers, each of whom sees "only part of the picture" (CMA); innocent reasons for ownership ties and co-bidding (Villamil et al.).
- **Why a persona of its own, not a variant of the FIU analyst (section 3.6).** Her data is public tender records rather than reports from regulated filers; her law is competition and procurement law, not anti-money-laundering; her graph is firms linked by shared tenders, not subjects linked by shared reports; and her output is an investigation or audit lead, not an intelligence dissemination.

## 5. Step-by-step workflows

### 5.1 From alert to SAR decision (AML, US-centric, with UK differences)

Sources: FFIEC manual; FinCEN SAR FAQs of October 2025 ([NCUA copy](https://ncua.gov/regulation-supervision/regulatory-compliance-resources/bank-secrecy-act-resources/frequently-asked-questions-regarding-suspicious-activity-reporting)); [FinCEN common errors](https://www.fincen.gov/resources/statutes-regulations/guidance/suggestions-addressing-common-errors-noted-suspicious); [31 CFR 1020.320](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-X/part-1020/subpart-C/section-1020.320).

1. **Alert generated.** A scenario fires (cash just under 10,000 dollars, rapid in-and-out wires). Linkurious alerts are scheduled graph queries ([Linkurious 3.0](https://linkurious.com/blog/linkurious-enterprise-3-0/)); its Watchtower module consolidates "alert results relating to the same entity into a single case" ([brochure](https://resources.linkurio.us/hubfs/On-demand%20Resources/PDFs/Brochure_Linkurious-Enterprise-Watchtower.pdf)).
2. **L1 triage.** Read the triggering transactions, compare with the KYC profile, close with a rationale or escalate. Vendor estimates of 15-22 minutes per alert ([Tookitaki](https://www.tookitaki.com/compliance-hub/reducing-false-positives-transaction-monitoring), [Sayari brief](https://sayari.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/Sayari_Alert_Adjudication_AMLA_Intelligence_Brief.pdf)) are not independent measurements.
3. **Link alerts and subjects.** The 1-2 hop neighborhood of the subject, styled by "has open alert" and "has prior SAR" ([Linkurious AML](https://linkurious.com/aml-investigation-solution)). Decide one case or several.
4. **Assemble the transaction picture (the longest step).** Pull 6-12 months for all subjects. Build accounts-as-nodes, transactions-as-edges. Before graphing, group the free-text counterparty names from wire and ACH records into probable counterparties (section 6.2), keeping external counterparties as their own node kind. Sum parallel edges per pair and direction (section 5.3). A vendor walkthrough puts L2 transaction analysis at about 17 minutes "often via manual SQL queries" and the whole case at about three hours ([SymphonyAI, vendor](https://www.symphonyai.com/resources/blog/financial-services/how-ai-agents-reduce-aml-investigation-time/)).
5. **Ask other institutions.** Where funds leave to another bank, send a 314(b) request to a registered peer. FinCEN's fact sheet of 12 June 2026 says suspected fraud alone qualifies, that sharing may be real time, and that alerts and indicators may be shared but SARs may not ([Morrison Foerster summary](https://www.mofo.com/resources/insights/260617-fincen-expands-section-314-b-information-sharing-guidance)). The other bank's legs are merged into the case graph with their source recorded; 314(a) requests run the other way, from law enforcement to banks. UK firms use the Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act 2023 sharing provisions (FCA review).
6. **Research subjects**: screening and registries; the Neo4j/GraphAware example adds Companies House officers and OpenSanctions matches ([Neo4j beyond the alert](https://neo4j.com/?p=435608)).
7. **Test typologies** on the summed graph: fan-in, fan-out, pass-through, cycles, sub-threshold cash (section 7.2).
8. **Recommend and decide.** A second reviewer or committee decides; examiners check that decisions are "supported and reasonable" (FFIEC). Since October 2025, documenting a no-file decision is encouraged but not required (FAQ Q4).
9. **Narrative and filing.** The narrative must give "the five essential elements" (who, what, when, where, why). Deadline 30 days from detection, up to 60 if no suspect is identified.
10. **Attach the transaction table.** FinCEN's current filing instructions (April 2026, version 1.0, section 6.1.6) allow "a single Microsoft Excel compatible comma separated values (CSV) file with no more than one megabyte of data"; "the contents of this file must be described in Part V", it is "most suitable for documenting transaction records that are too numerous to record in Part V", and no other supporting documentation may be attached; the narrative instructions add "Do not include tabular data in a FinCEN SAR narrative" ([FinCEN SAR Filing Instructions](https://bsaefiling.fincen.gov/resources/FinCENSARFilingInstructions.pdf)). Chart images cannot be attached, so the picture must still be put into words, but the per-transaction or per-pair table goes in the CSV. FinCEN prescribes no column list; the columns the case already holds (date, amount, direction, channel, originator and beneficiary name and account, counterparty bank, and per-pair count, total, minimum, maximum, first and last date) are the natural candidates, an inference from section 5.3 rather than documented practice.
11. **Retain.** Five years; supporting documentation is "deemed filed" and must be produced on request; no disclosure of the SAR ([FinCEN guidance](https://www.fincen.gov/index.php/resources/statutes-regulations/guidance/suspicious-activity-report-supporting-documentation)).
12. **Continuing activity.** Optional 90-day review with filing 120 days after the prior SAR (FAQ Q2-Q3); the graph task is comparing counterparties and flows across two date ranges.

**UK differences.** Reports go to the NCA's UKFIU with "XX" glossary codes ([Law Society of Scotland](https://www.lawscot.org.uk/media/4c4d3qxj/ukfiu-sar-glossary-codes-note-march-2022-v10.pdf)); a defense against money laundering (DAML) request starts a seven-working-day notice period and, if refused, a 31-day moratorium ([POCA s.335](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2002/29/section/335)).

### 5.2 Ring detection through shared identifiers (fraud strategy, Neo4j GDS)

Sources: [Neo4j part 2](https://neo4j.com/blog/developer/exploring-fraud-detection-neo4j-graph-data-science-part-2/), [part 3](https://neo4j.com/blog/developer/exploring-fraud-detection-neo4j-graph-data-science-part-3/), [synthetic identity guide](https://neo4j.com/developer/industry-use-cases/finserv/retail-banking/synthetic-identity-fraud/).

1. **Load a two-mode graph**: users and identifiers (Card, Device, IP, Phone, Email), plus P2P edges; about 241 chargeback-flagged users.
2. **Inspect identifier degree**; very high-degree identifiers are noise.
3. **Project to one mode by rules**: shared card plus a P2P transfer (6,240 edges); shared card or device used by 10 or fewer accounts plus two more shared identifiers (5,316 edges).
4. **Run weakly connected components**: 28,203 components, median 1, largest 175. Chosen because "deterministic, scales well, and is easy to explain".
5. **Propagate labels**: 452 risk accounts, 211 new; under 1 percent of users but 12.7 percent of P2P value.
6. **Profile components by size.**
7. **Rank further candidates** by fraud-risk-weighted degree and Jaccard node similarity with inverse-degree weights; one top user linked two fraud communities through shared IPs.
8. **Hand off** ranked lists; confirmed labels feed the next run. IP data is inexact and devices can be used without consent, so rules need review.

LexisNexis calls the synthetic-identity signal "contact field crowding" ([white paper](https://risk.lexisnexis.com/-/media/files/financial%20services/white-paper/lnrs-uncovering-synthetic-identity-fraud-white-paper-nxr14904-02-0923-en-us.pdf)).

### 5.3 Following the money through parallel transactions (i2, Linkurious, KeyLines)

1. **Import** a statement extract, one row per transaction; i2 maps columns to entity and link types with an import specification ([i2 import](https://www.ibm.com/support/knowledgecenter/SSJSV9_latest/com.ibm.i2.anb.doc/import_data.html)).
2. **Collapse parallel edges.** i2: occurrences on the label and "Sum Numeric Links" ([IBM](https://www.ibm.com/support/pages/node/716175)). Linkurious edge grouping shows count, Sum, Average, Min, Max and first and last date per group of same type and direction ([manual 4.2](https://doc.linkurious.com/user-manual/4.2/edgegrouping/)). KeyLines `aggregateLinks` with `aggregateByDirection`; graph functions ignore aggregate links ([docs](https://developers.cambridge-intelligence.com/docs/keylines/aggregate-links)).
3. **Show net flow** paired with a timeline ([Cambridge Intelligence AML showcase](https://developers.cambridge-intelligence.com/showcases/anti-money-laundering)).
4. **Filter by time and amount**; the XRP example filters over a 3,000 dollar threshold and plays the time bar ([blog](https://cambridge-intelligence.com/blog/identifying-high-risk-transactions-in-xrp-ripple-data/)); i2 histograms show peaks ([i2](https://docs.i2group.com/anb/10.0.0/showing_data_distribution.html)).
5. **Trace paths** with direction ([i2 Find Path](https://docs.i2group.com/anb/9.4.0/general_find_path_criteria.html)); query templates such as "all transactions within 24 hours of this transaction" ([Linkurious 2.10](https://linkurious.com/blog/linkurious-enterprise-2-10-more-expressive-visualizations-powerful-query-templates-and-improved-indexing-strategies/)).
6. **Hand off** an image plus sums and dates.

**Limit.** Paths and summed edges show that money moved, not how much of a particular deposit arrived. That needs section 5.4.

**Data-model note.** Neo4j's transaction-ring model makes the transaction a node (Account -PERFORMS-> Transaction -BENEFITS_TO-> Account) so a query can find loops of 3-6 hops where each amount is 80-100 percent of the last and dates increase ([Neo4j](https://neo4j.com/developer/industry-use-cases/finserv/retail-banking/transaction-ring/)). Tools must draw it as if transactions were edges.

### 5.4 Allocating traced funds under a tracing rule (forensic accounting, asset recovery, crypto)

When money is commingled, "the goal of 'tracing' is not to trace anything at all in many cases, but rather [to] serve ... as an equitable substitute"; the commonly accepted methods are the lowest intermediate balance rule (LIBR), pro rata, first in first out (FIFO) and last in first out ([Carriglio, Robinson and Wright, ABI Journal 2018, via Cozen O'Connor](https://cozen.com/news-resources/publications/2018/a-taxonomy-of-tracing-rules-one-size-does-not-fit-all)). Under LIBR the account holder is presumed to spend its own money first, and the claim is capped at the lowest balance after the deposit; later deposits do not restore it (Fifth Circuit summary, read through search summary of [ca5 opinion](https://www.ca5.uscourts.gov/Opinions/unpub/00/00-50358.0.wpd.pdf)). For crypto, Anderson et al. contrast "poison" (all outputs of a tainted transaction are fully tainted), "haircut" (taint is fractional: a wallet with 3 stolen and 7 clean coins spends 30 percent stolen coins) and FIFO from Clayton's case; on the 2012 Linode theft haircut tainted 93 percent of all addresses and FIFO 1.3 percent, and FIFO is reversible while haircut is not ([Bitcoin Redux, WEIS 2018](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~rja14/Papers/bitcoin-redux-weis2018.pdf)).

1. **Fix the seed and the question**: forward (where did the victim's 50,000 dollars go) or backward (where did the money in account X come from).
2. **Choose the rule** the jurisdiction or engagement accepts; often run two for sensitivity.
3. **Walk accounts in time order**, keeping a running balance and the traced portion at every transaction; record a balance checkpoint wherever the traced amount is capped.
4. **Split onward payments** by the rule into traced and untraced parts and continue into the next account.
5. **Summarize** as a flow-of-funds diagram. Valid8 draws Sankey diagrams "with relative size accuracy" and exports the transactions behind any flow ([LawNext](https://www.lawnext.com/2023/01/this-new-category-of-legal-software-lets-you-follow-the-money-through-financial-evidence-data.html)); DocuClipper's Sankey shows sources, accounts and destinations, pins a link to list its transactions, and exports a workbook with transactions, between-account pairs and account totals ([DocuClipper docs](https://www.docuclipper.com/docs/flow-of-funds-analysis/)). Neither documents LIBR or FIFO in the material read.
6. **Export the allocation table** (each step, rule, balance, traced amount, source document) for the exhibit.

### 5.5 Tracing scam proceeds onward from a first-hop mule (receiving bank, UK APP regime)

Since 7 October 2024 UK payment firms must reimburse authorized push payment (APP) scam victims up to 85,000 pounds, with the cost split 50:50 between sending and receiving firms ([PSR](https://www.psr.org.uk/news-and-updates/latest-news/news/psr-continues-to-take-bold-action-on-app-fraud-as-it-publishes-final-reimbursement-details-ahead-of-2024-implementation/); cap and split read through search summaries of [ICAEW](https://icaew.com/technical/financial-services/2025/the-fight-against-app-scams) and [Freshfields](https://www.freshfields.com/our-thinking/briefings/2024/09/authorised-push-payment-fraud-a-new-mandatory-reimbursement-regime-for-uk-psps)). The receiving side now pays for mules it banks. In Lloyds data on known mules, nearly 28 percent of value left within 15 minutes, a further 25 percent within an hour, and under 15 percent remained after 24 hours; 57 percent left by Faster Payments and nearly 20 percent by debit card ([RUSI, Westmore and Owen, July 2025](https://static.rusi.org/following-the-fraud-the-role-of-money-mules.pdf)). The FCA found "some firms focus on outbound transaction monitoring" without adequate inbound monitoring, and that receiving firms were "not responding swiftly enough" to notifications ([FCA 2023](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/multi-firm-reviews/proceeds-fraud-detecting-preventing-money-mules)); cash-out concentrated between the second and fifth mule ([FCA 2025](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/multi-firm-reviews/money-mules-activity-cashing-out-findings)). "No individual institution necessarily has visibility of the entire chain" ([FinTech Global](https://fintech.global/2026/10/07/banks-risk-missing-mule-networks-beyond-the-first-hop/)).

1. **Victim report** arrives from the sending bank naming the payment into a first-hop account.
2. **Seed the graph** with that payment, not with the account: only payments out after it arrived, and only up to the amount received, can carry the victim's money.
3. **Expand forward hop by hop**, inside the bank and to named receiving banks, as new payments arrive.
4. **Decide per hop**: hold, freeze, or notify the next bank; mark each node with its decision.
5. **Repatriate and report**: return what is held, file the SAR, and record the receiving-bank contribution.

Only the payment scheme sees the whole system: Pay.UK's mule proof of concept covered nearly 87 million accounts (section 6.3).

### 5.6 Beneficial ownership tracing, sanctions ownership and change detection

Sources: [FinCEN CDD FAQs](https://www.fincen.gov/resources/statutes-and-regulations/cdd-rule-faqs); EU AMLR Article 52 ([text](https://www.springlex.eu/en/packages/aml/amlr-regulation/article-52/), [Strise](https://strise.ai/guides/ubo-mapping-amlr)); [Neo4j KYC change](https://neo4j.com/blog/financial-services/corporate-kyc-can-you-see-when-the-owners-change/); [OFAC FAQ 401](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/401) and [FAQs 398-402](https://ofac.treasury.gov/faqs/topic/1521).

1. **Collect** declarations and registry data: UK PSC daily JSON ([data.gov.uk](https://www.data.gov.uk/dataset/people-with-significant-control-psc)); Open Ownership BODS JSON ([reference](https://standard.openownership.org/en/main/standard/reference.html)), which allows exact shares or min-max ranges ([schema](https://standard.openownership.org/en/0.0.3/schema.html)); Orbis, D&B, OpenCorporates.
2. **Build** owner-to-owned edges with share, votes, control type, dates and source filing; PSC gives bands such as `ownership-of-shares-25-to-50-percent`.
3. **Resolve duplicates**: deduplication cut PSC person records 5 percent and entities 10 percent ([Open Ownership](https://www.openownership.org/en/publications/insights-from-the-united-kingdoms-people-with-significant-control-register/6-challenges-and-best-practices-in-data-analysis/)).
4. **Traverse upward** (unbounded paths over 16 million nodes in the [Neo4j UBO demo](https://neo4j.com/blog/graph-database/exploring-ultimate-beneficial-ownership-with-graph-technology); Sayari advertises 10+ degrees, [Sayari](https://sayari.com/enterprise/kyc-beneficial-ownership/)).
5. **Compute effective ownership (CDD, AMLR).** Multiply along each path, add across paths: 80 x 60 x 60 percent = 28.8 percent (Neo4j); 24 plus 40 = 64 percent (Strise). Threshold 25 percent (AMLR allows lowering to 15 for higher-risk categories). Bands need interval arithmetic; D&B notes inferred totals can exceed 100 percent ([D&B](https://www.dnb.co.uk/content/dam/english/business-trends/db-4981-beneficial-ownership-whitepaper.pdf)). No standard cycle formula was found.
6. **Compute sanctions ownership (a different rule).** Under OFAC's 50 Percent Rule an entity is blocked if owned "50 percent or more in the aggregate" by blocked persons, directly or indirectly. Once an entity is blocked, its own 50-percent-or-more stakes count in full downstream: in OFAC's example, a blocked person owning 50 percent of A, which owns 50 percent of B, blocks B, although the product is 25 percent. A stake passes through only an intermediary that is itself blocked (FAQ 401, example 4), and the rule tests ownership, not control (FAQ 398). The UK's ownership limb needs "more than 50 percent", adds a separate control limb, and per law-firm and vendor summaries does not generally aggregate separate designated owners; EU treatment of aggregation is described inconsistently in the summaries read (read through search summaries of [DLA Piper on the July 2026 OFAC-OFSI guidance](https://www.dlapiper.com/en-at/insights/publications/2026/07/ofac-and-ofsi-publish-joint-comparative-guidance-on-us-and-uk-sanctions) and [Kharon](https://www.kharon.com/resources/article/sanctions/sanctions-50-percent-rules-and-beyond-complex-ownership-structures-and-hidden-compliance-risks)). A tool that only multiplies along chains gives the wrong sanctions answer.
7. **Screen people** and attach hits.
8. **Detect change** on a proposed snapshot, alerting when the set of qualifying owners or the blocked status changes, not the count (Neo4j: one owner rose 24 to 32 percent while another fell 40 to 20).
9. **Hand off** a structure chart, qualifying owners with evidence per edge, and which rule produced each flag.

US context: since March 2025 US-created companies are exempt from Corporate Transparency Act reporting, so banks rely on declarations and commercial data ([FinCEN](https://www.fincen.gov/news/news-releases/fincen-removes-beneficial-ownership-reporting-requirements-us-companies-and-us); [Troutman](https://www.troutman.com/insights/beneficial-ownership-gets-a-refresh-fincen-recalibrates-cdd-rule/)). Sanctions-evasion and export-control work over trade and supply-chain networks (Sayari's market) is related but not researched here; see supply-chain-and-physical-infrastructure-networks.md in this folder.

### 5.7 Crypto tracing from an address or transaction to a cash-out service

Sources: [Chainalysis Reactor page](https://www.chainalysis.com/product/reactor/) (vendor); Chainalysis Academy course pages (Reactor Essentials ends with modules on exporting from Reactor and OSINT; separate courses cover automated change detection in "peel chains" and indirect exposure; read through search summary of [Reactor Essentials](https://chainalysis-academy-clone.skilljar.com/reactor-essentials) and [change detection](https://chainalysis-academy-clone.skilljar.com/automated-change-detection-peelchains)); the Chainalysis expert declaration in United States v. Sterlingov ([Bisbee declaration](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.dcd.232431/gov.uscourts.dcd.232431.149.1.pdf)); [Moser and Narayanan, FC 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.05749) ([full text](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2107.05749)); TRM release notes. The Council of Europe's tracing training deck was not accessible.

1. **Seed** an address or transaction hash from an alert, a victim report or a seized device.
2. **Read the transaction structure.** On Bitcoin-style ledgers a transaction spends several inputs and creates several outputs; the natural graph is transaction-as-node with input and output edges, and one output is usually change returned to the sender.
3. **Apply clustering.** The co-spend (common-input) heuristic assumes inputs spent together belong to one owner; Chainalysis says it skips detected CoinJoin co-spends. Change heuristics mark the change output. Exchange deposit addresses are clustered because they sweep into a consolidation address (Bisbee declaration). Moser and Narayanan show the multi-input heuristic is "vulnerable to false positives from techniques like CoinJoin and PayJoin", and that naively merging predicted change produced "one large supercluster" (a 1596 percent jump in addresses); a single wrong change link between two exchanges can merge both.
4. **Overlay attribution.** Vendor labels name clusters (exchange, mixer, darknet market, sanctioned entity). Chainalysis says exchanges confirm or deny attributions when they receive subpoenas (Bisbee declaration).
5. **Expand hop by hop** in the direction of the funds, noting the amount carried at each hop; collapse swaps and bridges into single steps (Reactor "auto-interprets" swaps, bridges and mixers; TRM reports real-time swap matching across 36 EVM chains and 21 bridges, [TRM October 2026](https://www.trmlabs.com/forensics/whats-new-2026-10)).
6. **Allocate** where funds were commingled (section 5.4): poison, haircut or FIFO.
7. **Find the cash-out**: a cluster attributed to a regulated exchange.
8. **Watch** addresses still holding funds; TRM lets an investigator add a graph address to its Detect tool for near real-time alerts on movement ([TRM January 2026](https://www.trmlabs.com/resources/blog/january-product-highlights-accelerating-investigations-with-intuitive-workflows)).
9. **Export** the graph, transaction list and report; TRM records each export in an audit log (August 2026 notes, read through search summary).
10. **Request records** from the exchange: law enforcement by subpoena or warrant, an exchange by 314(b) or a SAR; the KYC return names the account holder.

The police and federal-agent side of this work (seeds from ransom notes, joining exchange KYC identities to the case chart, the court flow graph and the Sterlingov admissibility ruling) is in law-enforcement-and-intelligence-analysts.md, section 5.9.

### 5.8 FIU and tax analysis across many filers' reports

Sources: UNODC goAML services page (read through search summary, [goAML](https://goaml.unodc.org/goaml/en/services.html)); [Linkurious FIU blog](https://linkurious.com/blog/fiu-intelligence-aml-investigations-graph/) (vendor); ELA fiche on HMRC Connect.

1. **Ingest reports** (STRs, SARs, cash-threshold reports) from many filers into one store of people, companies, accounts and transactions.
2. **Resolve subjects across filers.** goAML's subject unification merges or splits records by rules "without altering original data" and is logged; where identity cannot be confirmed, an analyst creates a "Virtual Object", a temporary link between possibly identical records that keeps both.
3. **Find report clusters**: subjects and accounts named in several reports; centrality to find facilitators; paths across jurisdictions (Linkurious).
4. **Run strategic pivots** by sector, occupation or geography (HMRC plots undeclared work street by street, Wikipedia).
5. **Disseminate** a case proposal to police, prosecutors, foreign FIUs or (tax) compliance teams, with a chart. No primary source was found confirming goAML exports to i2.

### 5.9 Rebuilding money flows from subpoenaed bank records (forensic accountants, law-enforcement financial investigators)

Source: Marilyn Peterson, "A Guide to the Financial Analysis of Personal and Corporate Bank Records", National White Collar Crime Center, 2nd ed. 1998 ([NCJRS](https://www.ojp.gov/pdffiles1/Digitization/174809NCJRS.pdf)), whose 14 steps run from planning to presenting; current tools from DocuClipper and Valid8.

1. **Plan and obtain** records by subpoena; **inventory** them with an index and status log (Peterson).
2. **Extract** statements, checks, deposit items and wires into rows; today from PDF by OCR tools (DocuClipper; Valid8 claims 30+ source system types).
3. **Normalize** into one template: date, account, counterparty, amount, instrument, check number, memo, source page.
4. **Review for leads**: payees, endorsements, signatories, where checks were negotiated.
5. **Match transfers between the subject's own accounts** across banks, so they are not counted as income: DocuClipper pairs debits and credits of the same amount within a few days.
6. **Analyze** by date, payee and source; then "analyze flow among multiple accounts" with a flow matrix (Peterson).
7. **Chart**: Peterson lists event, activity and commodity flow charts and link charts; today a Sankey (section 5.4).
8. **Trace under a rule** when restitution or forfeiture depends on it (section 5.4).
9. **Summarize, conclude and present** an exhibit tied to source documents.

### 5.10 Insurance claim triage through link analysis (SIU)

Sources: Neo4j Zurich story; [Linkurious insurance](https://linkurious.com/blog/insurance-fraud-investigation/); [Oskarsdottir et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.08313).

1. **Score and refer**: a rules engine refers claims above a threshold (Zurich).
2. **Open the claim network**: claim, policies, customers, vehicles, payments, national databases and blacklists, about 20 million nodes and 35 million relationships at Zurich Switzerland.
3. **Expand to parties and their other claims**; look for shared addresses, phones, payout accounts (fan-in of payouts), garages, credit-hire firms, solicitors and doctors; Linkurious warns each detail "looks harmless alone". For property claims it suggests layering geography; for medical claims, seeing whether providers "share many common patients".
4. **Discount hubs**: a garage with hundreds of claims needs its own check.
5. **Check industry data.** Cifas National Fraud Database ([Cifas NFD](https://www.cifas.org.uk/fraud-prevention-community/member-benefits/data/nfd)): in a 2015 test, 196 of 618 proven Hill Dickinson motor fraud cases (about one third) matched details in Cifas cases ([Cifas release, 14 October 2015](https://cifas.org.uk/newsroom/cross-sector-fraud-data-unlock-millions-additional-insurance-savings)). IFB Insurance Fraud Register and IFi Hub ([Insurance Times](https://insurancetimes.co.uk/news/ifb-launches-new-industry-scale-fraud-prevention-platform/1429269.article)); Zurich UK links claims to Companies House, Equifax and the IFR with Quantexa ([Insurance Business UK](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/uk/news/claims/zurich-uncovers-record-109-million-of-fraud-as-gangs-turn-to-ai-596232.aspx)). US: ClaimSearch, which Verisk says holds over 1.9 billion claims ([Verisk](https://verisk.com/products/network-analysis)); no public source was found on how SIUs read ClaimSearch match reports.
6. **Decide**: settle or special fraud processing.
7. **Refer (US).** California requires SIUs to refer suspected fraud to the Department of Insurance Fraud Division and, where needed, district attorneys, stating whether the investigation is complete; New York requires fraud prevention plans and an annual report to its Insurance Frauds Bureau; NICB's Fraud Bureau Reporting Program sends a member's referral to the state insurance department and NICB at once (read through search summaries of [CDI SIU page](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/0300-fraud/0100-fraud-division-overview/12-siu), [NY DFS](https://www.dfs.ny.gov/apps_and_licensing/insurance_companies/fraud_reporting_and_information) and [NICB slides](https://www.dwd.wisconsin.gov/misclass-taskforce/pdf/meetings/210114/worsham/fraud-bureau-reporting.pdf)).
8. **Network sweeps**: the IFB linked about 170,000 of 2.7 million motor claims to suspected crash-for-cash networks ([Which?](https://www.which.co.uk/news/article/insurance-fraud-do-you-live-in-a-crash-for-cash-hotspot-aD76p9P9jfWm)); academically, claims-parties bipartite graphs scored with BiRank seeded by known fraud (Oskarsdottir et al.).

No public source was found that records a click-by-click SIU session in a graph tool.

### 5.11 Healthcare program integrity: provider networks in claims data

Sources: [O'Malley, Bubolz and Skinner, Social Science and Medicine 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10290506/); [Paudel, Eberle and Talbert, FLAIRS 2017](https://cdn.aaai.org/ocs/15455/15455-68704-1-PB.pdf); MFCU rules ([HHS-OIG data mining](https://oig.hhs.gov/fraud/medicaid-fraud-control-units-mfcu/data-mining.asp), [KFF](https://www.kff.org/medicaid/understanding-the-role-of-medicaid-fraud-control-units-mfcus/)).

1. **Extract claims** for a provider type and period (O'Malley et al. used all fee-for-service Medicare home health claims, 2002-2016, across 306 regions).
2. **Build the bipartite graph**: patients to providers (agencies, doctors, pharmacies), one edge per patient-provider-year.
3. **Project** to a weighted provider-provider graph: two agencies are linked when they share a patient, weighted by shared patients.
4. **Measure sharing structure**: their bipartite mixture index (0 to 1) rose with billing; one standard deviation was associated with about 173 dollars more billing per enrollee and an 8.6 percent higher chance of a DOJ strike force. They caution it may not predict newer schemes such as telemedicine fraud.
5. **Search for anomalous substructures**: Paudel et al. ran graph-based anomaly detection over patient, provider, claim, diagnosis and procedure graphs from CMS data, citing Sparrow's split between "hit and run" schemes and "steal a little all the time".
6. **Rank providers** and **refer** to an MFCU, OIG or police. MFCU data mining has required OIG approval under 42 CFR 1007.20; KFF reports OIG waived prior approval in August 2026 (the two sources conflict; read through search summary).

### 5.12 Screening public tenders for bid-rigging groups (competition authorities, auditors, anti-corruption researchers)

Sources: [Wachs and Kertesz, Scientific Reports 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6658564) (preprint at https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.08667); [Villamil, Kertesz and Fazekas, Scientific Reports 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10850496/); [Baranek, Musolff and Titl 2021](https://www.uu.nl/sites/default/files/LEG_USE_WP_21-11.pdf); [Fazekas, Toth, Wachs and Abdou, International Journal of Industrial Organization 2025](https://unipub.lib.uni-corvinus.hu/12023/1/1-s2.0-S0167718725000943-main.pdf) (read through search summary); CMA paper; OCP Cardinal.

1. **Get the tender records**: an OCDS feed or a national portal export, with the buyer, product code (CPV in the EU), reserve price, date, every bidder and every bid price. Ukraine's ProZorro platform, compulsory for all public entities since 2016, is one such source (Baranek et al.). Collusion flags need individual bid values (OCP Cardinal), and losing bids are often the missing part (CMA).
2. **Flatten to one row per bid** and check quality. Cardinal's prepare step warns about quality issues it can fix and ones that block indicator calculation (OCP Cardinal). Where ownership is added, firm identifiers are matched between the procurement data and the ownership database (Villamil et al. matched GTI tender data with Orbis).
3. **Build the co-bidding network**: firms are nodes; two firms are linked when they bid on the same contracts, weighted by the Jaccard similarity of their contract sets, counting only contracts with at least one other bidder (Wachs and Kertesz). Villamil et al. instead weight by the count of shared tenders and analyze each market year by year.
4. **Find groups** with an overlapping, greedy community method, and score each by **coherence** (geometric over arithmetic mean of its internal edge weights) and **exclusivity** (its internal strength over its total strength). A group is suspicious when both exceed the 80th percentile of groups found in null networks built by shuffling bids within 2-digit CPV codes for the same year; in Georgia a group counted only if its firms bid exclusively on at least 30 contracts in a year (Wachs and Kertesz).
5. **Read the screens beside each group**: average price relative to the reserve price, price and bid coefficients of variation, in-group bid protests (Wachs and Kertesz); single bidding, missing bidders, stable market structure, winning probability and cut-point position in the co-bidding network (Villamil et al.); superfluous losing bidders (Brazil's Cerebro, per the CMA); identical bid prices (OCP Cardinal).
6. **Add the ownership layer**: firms linked when any ownership path connects them, beside the co-bidding layer, per year. Markets with more ownership links showed more single bidding and firms with ownership ties more often held cut-point positions; the authors make no causal claim and name innocent explanations (Villamil et al.).
7. **On auction platforms with bid updates, test pairs**: a pair of firms that never undercut each other in the live auction is a likely collusive link; links between the 25 percent of pairs with the lowest pairwise score are drawn per product category, with already-prosecuted links styled apart from the rest (Baranek et al.).
8. **Validate against proven cases**: Baranek et al. checked their pairs against 863 firms the Ukrainian anti-monopoly agency banned for collusion, which took part in 23,515 tenders (sentenced pairs scored lower, t-statistic 17.06); Fazekas et al. trained screens on 73 proven cartels from 7 European countries, 2004-2021, and report 70-84 percent accuracy across countries and markets against 97 percent when trained and tested on one cartel (read through search summary).
9. **Hand over a short list**, not a verdict: the method "cannot prove collusion" and serves to narrow the space "into a shorter list of candidates for investigation" (Wachs and Kertesz); the CMA's tool feeds enforcement leads (CMA).

No public source was found that records a screening analyst's session in a graph tool, or how long a screening run takes.

## 6. Data

### 6.1 Sources and formats

- **Transactions**: core banking, wire (SWIFT MT, ISO 20022), ACH, card, P2P, cash; CSV or Excel to investigators ([Altman et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.16424v1)).
- **Statements under subpoena**: PDFs, check images, deposit tickets, wire records (section 5.9).
- **Blockchain**: public ledgers through vendor tools; clusters and attribution labels are vendor data.
- **Identity**: customer master, devices, IPs, phones, emails, cards, addresses.
- **Corporate**: PSC JSON, BODS JSON, OpenCorporates, Orbis, D&B, Sayari; sanctions lists.
- **Reports**: SARs and STRs aggregated at FIUs; tax and third-party records at HMRC.
- **Insurance and health claims**: claims, policies, parties, providers, payout accounts; Cifas, IFR, ClaimSearch.
- **Public tenders**: OCDS releases (nested JSON of tender, bids, awards, buyer) or national portal exports; GTI's TED-based export for 30 European countries; ownership from Orbis or company registers (section 5.12).

### 6.2 Graph properties and data reality

- **Directed temporal multigraph** for transactions (Altman et al.).
- **One-sided visibility.** A bank sees only legs touching its own customers. External counterparties arrive as free-text names and partial account numbers from wire and ACH records, the same counterparty under many spellings, so the subject's graph is a star with external leaves until names are grouped and other banks answer 314(b) requests. Graph edges therefore need a source: own records, a 314(b) response, a statement page, a vendor.
- **Transaction-as-node** for UTXO blockchains (many inputs and outputs) and for time-constrained queries (Neo4j).
- **Heuristic entity clusters** over addresses (crypto) and over report subjects (FIUs), some confirmed, some tentative.
- **Two-mode** graphs for identifiers, claims-parties and patients-providers, projected for components or sharing measures.
- **Ownership hierarchy with cycles**, edges weighted by percentage or band, with dates.
- **Tender-firm two-mode graph** projected to a weighted co-bidding graph per year or market, often stacked with an ownership layer as a temporal multiplex graph (section 5.12).

### 6.4 Identifier problems

- People lack unique identifiers across registries (Open Ownership) and across FIU filers (goAML).
- Shared identifiers are not proof; super-nodes must be capped (Neo4j used 10 or fewer accounts per identifier).
- Crypto clusters can collapse through one wrong merge (Moser and Narayanan).
- Fuzzy name matching; Quantexa claims "up to 99%" entity resolution accuracy (vendor, [FAQ](https://quantexa.com/faqs)).

### 6.5 Sensitivity and access

SAR confidentiality and UK tipping-off offenses apply, and 314(b) does not allow sharing SARs. Cifas keeps records up to six years ([Cifas](https://www.cifas.org.uk/secure/contentport/uploads/documents/Cifas%20Documents/Information%20for%20consumers%20-%20The%20National%20Fraud%20Database.pdf)); the IFR was set up with the UK ICO engaged ([Pinsent Masons](https://www.pinsentmasons.com/out-law/news/insurance-fraud-register-launched-to-aid-crackdown-on-fake-claims)). Data rarely leaves the institution, so a browser tool must work on local data with no upload.

### 6.6 Alert volumes and durations: sourced vs unsourced

- **Working paper**: false-positive rates about 70-95 percent, alert-to-STR conversion 2-10 percent (Redhead, SWIFT Institute/RUSI 2021, via [SWIFT](https://swift.com/swift-resource/252235/download), read through search summary).
- **Consultancy**: one or two alerts per hundred acted upon ([McKinsey](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/risk-and-resilience/our-insights/the-investigator-centered-approach-to-financial-crime-doing-what-matters)).
- **Vendor-only**: 30 alerts per analyst per day, 15-22 minutes per alert. Unverified.
- **Unsourced in Graphty's current W06**: "50+ alerts daily", "40-60% fewer false escalations", "under 30 minutes per alert".

## 7. Analysis methods

### 7.1 Algorithms actually used

- **Weakly connected components** on projected identifier graphs, first because explainable (Neo4j).
- **Degree, weighted degree, Jaccard similarity, seeded ranking** (Neo4j part 3; BiRank); i2 "most connected items" ([IBM](https://www.ibm.com/support/pages/ibm-i2-analysts-notebook-refining-list-most-connected-items-functionality)).
- **Directed and time-ordered path and cycle search** with amount ratios (Neo4j transaction ring).
- **Typology motifs** (Altman et al.).
- **Aggregation** per pair: count, sum, min, max, first and last date, net flow.
- **Flow allocation over running balances**: LIBR, FIFO, pro rata; crypto poison, haircut, FIFO (section 5.4).
- **Address clustering**: co-spend and change heuristics, with merge constraints (section 5.7).
- **Ownership arithmetic**: product-and-sum for beneficial ownership; aggregate-and-propagate for OFAC (section 5.6).
- **Bipartite projection, sharing indices, anomaly detection** for provider networks.
- **Co-bidding groups**: Jaccard-weighted co-bidding projection, overlapping greedy community detection, coherence and exclusivity against a bid-shuffling null model; cut points; pairwise no-undercut tests (section 5.12).
- **Entity resolution** first, with confirmed merges and tentative links. Community detection is secondary in sources read.

### 7.2 Typologies and the views they need

| Typology                                           | Graph signature                                                                        | View needed                                                        |
| -------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |

### 7.3 Validation habits

Every link must be explainable (which identifier, transaction, filing or heuristic); investigators "must judge significance, weigh legitimate explanations, and document their reasoning" (Neo4j beyond the alert); a second person reviews before filing; crypto attributions are checked against exchange subpoena returns (Bisbee declaration); rules are tuned by new hits and value moved.

## 8. Outputs and collaboration

Case files with status history, comments, saved graph state and linked alerts (Linkurious presents case history as an audit trail); SARs and STRs with five-element narratives, UK glossary codes and DAML requests; 314(b) requests, freeze and repatriation notices between banks; ownership charts with evidence per edge and the rule behind each flag; flow-of-funds exhibits (Sankey or flow chart, allocation table, transactions tied to source pages); crypto trace reports with CSVs and exchange requests; FIU disseminations; SIU, MFCU and OIG referrals; ring labels fed back to detection; backlog and aging reports.

Every one of these people starts from an alert, a case list or a victim report, never from an unfamiliar graph file, and the sources read use components and shared-identifier similarity rather than community detection or domain-free anomaly scores, so the generic first-exploration, community-analysis and anomaly-detection workflows do not fit them. W06 omits two-mode identifier graphs, multi-edges with amounts, one-sided visibility and provenance; leads with community detection where practice uses components then similarity; treats "follow the money" as paths where courts need allocation under a rule; and has no regulatory output and unsourced metrics ("50+ alerts daily").
