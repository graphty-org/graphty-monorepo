# Network Scientists and the Validity of Core Analysis Methods

**Summary.** Network scientists (trained in physics, computer science, applied mathematics or a quantitative social science) study real and synthetic networks mostly in code -- igraph, NetworkX, graph-tool -- and open Gephi to explore and draw figures. A larger group of applied analysts runs the same core methods (centrality, communities, paths, hubs, outliers) inside digital humanities, computational social science, social-media and business projects, leaning on Gephi tutorials and short Python recipes. Both need to turn a network into a claim a reviewer or decision-maker will accept, usually through a figure. The literature has shown that the default ways of making those claims are often invalid: Louvain can return disconnected communities, modularity has a resolution limit and many near-equal optima, most "scale-free" claims fail proper tests, motif z-scores depend on the null, path measures silently read weights as distances, and clusters seen in a force-directed drawing need not match any computed partition. Beyond structure, network scientists also simulate processes on networks (spreading, cascades, attacks) and analyze multilayer, two-mode and group (hypergraph) data directly, without flattening it to pairs. A product that serves this population has to make the valid method the easy one.

Scope note: this document deepens Graphty's records `expert-emma` and `analyst-alex` and workflows W03 (Iterative Analysis Cycle), W04 (Community Analysis), W05 (Path Investigation), W12 (Anomaly Detection) and W17 (Hub Investigation) in `design/designloom`, saying where each is confirmed, wrong or incomplete. Related documents in this folder: `academic-social-network-research.md` (ERGM, SAOM, QAP), `temporal-and-dynamic-networks.md`, `ecological-interaction-networks.md` and `bibliometrics-and-science-mapping.md` (two-mode data), `code-first-data-scientists-and-graph-ml.md`, `digital-humanities-and-historical-networks.md`. This document holds the cross-domain account of group (hypergraph) data (Workflow M).

## 1. Population

**Who they are.**

- **Academic network scientists**: postdocs, faculty and PhD students in network science, complex systems, statistical physics, computational social science, network neuroscience and systems biology. Postings ask for complex networks, computational modeling, percolation and spreading ([Northwestern CND](https://cnd.northwestern.edu/?p=673)) and even contributing code to igraph ([Network Science Institute](https://www.networkscienceinstitute.org/news/postdoctoral-research-associate-the-commlab)).
- **Industry graph data scientists**: covered in `code-first-data-scientists-and-graph-ml.md`; they share this document's validity problems (seeds, weight conventions, null comparisons) but work toward production pipelines, not papers.
- **Applied analysts who use network methods** (the `analyst-alex` population): digital humanists, communication and political-science researchers, social-media analysts, librarians teaching Gephi, policy and market researchers. In Gephi's own user survey (December 2015, 285 respondents from 46 countries, explicitly not representative), academia was the largest sector and "researcher" the most common job title; 70% typically analyze social networks, 46% social-media networks and 43% semantic networks, and more than half had never used Gephi on dynamic networks ([Gephi blog, 6 Feb 2016](https://gephi.wordpress.com/2016/02/06/a-close-look-at-the-gephi-user-community/)). Gephi has more than 9,800 citing publications ([van Geenen et al. 2023, Frontiers in Big Data](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fdata.2022.510310/full)).

**Size.** No source counts network scientists. Proxies: Netzschleuder holds more than 160,000 network datasets ([IT:U project page](https://it-u.at/en/research/research-groups/complex-systems-and-network-science/projects/netzschleuder/)); the Broido and Clauset corpus drew 928 datasets from ICON, the Colorado Index of Complex Networks ([Broido and Clauset 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6399239/)).

**How often they touch graph tools.** Daily for academic network scientists. For applied analysts, project-based: weekly during a study, then idle; about a third of Gephi survey respondents used it at least weekly (Gephi blog, above).

**Skill profile.**

| Trait          | Network scientist (`expert-emma`)                                    | Applied analyst (`analyst-alex`)                                        |
| -------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Graph literacy | Null models, modularity's flaws, SBMs, percolation, spreading models | Degree, betweenness, "modularity class" from Gephi                      |
| Coding         | Python or Julia daily; C/C++ for speed; reads library source         | Copies notebook recipes (NetworkX, R igraph); some use only Gephi's GUI |
| Statistics     | Maximum likelihood, ensembles, Bayesian model selection              | Descriptive; often treats one run's output as the answer                |
| Typical team   | Solo or a 2-5 person lab; code on GitHub                             | Solo inside a non-network team; hands figures to non-experts            |

**Correction to `expert-emma`.** Her motivations (rigor, parameterization, scripting, export to R or Python) are right, but her only sources are Nielsen Norman Group articles and her goals are UI conveniences (shortcuts, command palette). What she most needs from a GUI tool is **method validity and provenance**: correct algorithm variants, explicit weight semantics, nulls, seeds, run-to-run stability and a record of every parameter.

## 2. Personas

### 2.1 Dr. Emma Larsen -- computational network scientist (`expert-emma`, corrected)

- **Role:** assistant professor in physics or complex systems with three PhD students; publishes on community structure, spreading and group interactions.
- **Tools today:** graph-tool for SBM inference and ensembles (`minimize_nested_blockmodel_dl`, `random_rewire`, `motif_significance`); python-igraph for Leiden; NetworkX for prototyping; XGI for hypergraph data; the `powerlaw` package for tail fitting ([Alstott et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/3906378/)); EoN or NDlib for epidemic simulation ([EoN, Miller and Ting](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.02436); [NDlib](https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.05854)); Gephi ForceAtlas2 for figures, finished in Inkscape.
- **Data:** Netzschleuder and ICON datasets fetched by name ([graph-tool collection](https://graph-tool.skewed.de/doc/collection.html)), SNAP, XGI-DATA hypergraphs, LFR and SBM benchmarks; 10^3 to 10^9 edges.
- **Goals:** characterize a network against the right null model; report communities that are statistically supported; know how a process spreads on it and how fragile it is; produce a figure that does not mislead; make the analysis rerunnable by a referee.
- **Frustrations (evidenced):** Louvain partitions that change every run and can be internally disconnected ([Traag et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6435756/)). Modularity's exponential number of near-optimal partitions ([Good et al. 2010](https://arxiv.org/abs/0910.0165)). Tools that give one number with no ensemble. Inconsistent weight conventions ([igraph-help thread](https://lists.gnu.org/archive/html/igraph-help/2019-10/msg00007.html); [Opsahl's tnet notes](https://toreopsahl.com/tnet/weighted-networks/node-centrality)). Gephi cannot record what was done; the Fieldnotes plugin exists for that ([van Geenen et al. 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fdata.2022.510310/full)).

### 2.2 Alex Moreno -- applied network analyst (`analyst-alex`, given a domain, data and scale)

- **Role:** research data analyst in a digital-scholarship or computational social science lab (also stands for policy and market analysts); self-taught Python from the Programming Historian, Ognyanova's R igraph tutorials and library Gephi guides ([Programming Historian](https://programminghistorian.org/en/lessons/exploring-and-analyzing-network-data-with-python); [kateto.net](https://kateto.net/networks-r-igraph); [Brown Gephi guide](https://libguides.brown.edu/gephi)).
- **Tools today:** Gephi (0.10/0.11) plus NetworkX in a notebook, handing off with `write_gexf` (Programming Historian). Some use NodeXL in Excel for social-media pulls, with older clustering (CNM, Wakita-Tsurumi, Newman-Girvan; [Pew methods note, archived](https://web.archive.org/web/2020/https://legacy.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/9/2014/02/How-we-analyzed-Twitter-social-media-networks.pdf)).
- **Data and scale:** correspondence networks (the Quaker lesson: 119 nodes, 174 edges), co-authorship and citation exports, retweet and hashtag networks (10^3 to 10^5 nodes), person-organization tables, as CSV. Gephi suggests 2 GB of RAM for about 100,000 nodes plus edges and more than 8 GB for about a million ([Gephi requirements](https://gephi.org/users/requirements)).
- **Goals:** find "the groups" and "the important people" and explain them to non-network colleagues in a figure and a paragraph.
- **Frustrations (evidenced):** Modularity gives a different number of communities on each run, and results "can vary with the initial order" of input rows ([Gephi bug 727701](https://bugs.launchpad.net/gephi/+bug/727701)). Tutorials say to pick "a resolution between 0.1 and 2" with no rule for choosing (Grandjean). `nx.diameter` fails on a disconnected graph (Programming Historian). Readers take a sample map as the whole population (van Geenen et al. 2023).

### 2.3 Dr. Priya Raman -- network neuroscience researcher (new: `network-neuroscience-researcher`)

- **Role:** postdoc in a cognitive or clinical neuroscience lab building brain graphs from fMRI or diffusion MRI. Uses the MATLAB Brain Connectivity Toolbox and GenLouvain (custom nulls, resolution, multilayer modularity), Python (bctpy, NetworkX) and consensus clustering scripts ([Betzel 2020 review](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2011.06723)).
- **Data:** per-subject weighted, signed correlation matrices (about 100-400 regions, fully dense before thresholding), often in patient-control cohorts, sometimes as time windows stacked into a multilayer network.
- **Goals:** compare network organization between groups without threshold artifacts; find modules and connector hubs; track module change across time windows.
- **Frustrations (evidenced):** Proportional thresholding can turn overall connectivity differences into spurious topology differences ([van den Heuvel et al. 2017](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/proportional-thresholding-in-resting-state-fmri-functional-connec/)). Degree-preserving nulls inflate modularity in spatially embedded networks, and negative weights need signed nulls (Betzel 2020).
- **Why she matters:** she stands for every user whose graph is **derived** from a similarity matrix (brain, genes, stocks, keywords), where threshold and weight meaning decide the result; some now also model co-fluctuations of three or more regions as higher-order interactions (Workflow M). See `network-neuroscience-connectomics.md`.

## 4. Step-by-step workflows

### Workflow A -- Characterizing a new network (network scientist)

Sources: Clauset, Shalizi and Newman 2009 ([arXiv 0706.1062](https://arxiv.org/abs/0706.1062)); Broido and Clauset 2019 (above); `powerlaw` ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/powerlaw/)); Newman on assortativity ([arXiv cond-mat/0205405](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cond-mat/0205405)); Fosdick et al. 2018 ([arXiv 1608.00607](https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.00607v3)); [NetworkX small-world module](https://networkx.org/documentation/latest/reference/algorithms/smallworld.html); Colizza et al. 2006 ([arXiv physics/0602134](https://arxiv.org/abs/physics/0602134)); motif tools ([Weizmann motif software](https://weizmann.ac.il/mcb/UriAlon/download/network-motif-software); [graph-tool motif_significance](https://graph-tool.skewed.de/static/docs/stable/autosummary/graph_tool.clustering.motif_significance.html)).

1. **Acquire and record provenance.** Load by name from Netzschleuder or SNAP, or a collaborator's edge list; record what an edge means, its type (directed, weighted, multi, bipartite, multilayer, temporal, group) and how it was sampled.
2. **Decide the representation (an explicit decision).** Broido and Clauset turn every non-simple property into one or more simple graphs by a stated transformation (in- and out-degree separately, thresholded weights, one layer at a time, projections). GUI tools hide this step: Gephi merges parallel edges on import and by default "increases the weight of the edge" ([Gephi release notes, v0.7.0](https://github.com/gephi/gephi/releases)). Two-mode, multilayer and group data can also be analyzed without flattening (Workflows J, K and M).
3. **Basic descriptives.** n, m, density, components, giant component; diameter and average path on the giant component only (NetworkX raises an error otherwise); global transitivity and average local clustering, two numbers often confused.
4. **Degree distribution, fitted correctly.** Plot the CCDF on log-log axes. Fit only the upper tail k >= k_min, with k_min chosen by minimizing the Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance and alpha by discrete maximum likelihood; never least-squares on a log-log histogram, which Clauset et al. show is biased. A bootstrap KS p >= 0.1 means only that a power law is _plausible_. Then run likelihood-ratio tests against log-normal, exponential and cutoff alternatives (`powerlaw.Fit(degrees, discrete=True).distribution_compare('power_law', 'lognormal')`).
    - Across 928 datasets, 49% were "not scale free", only 4% met the strongest evidence, and a log-normal fit at least as well as a power law for 88%. (Broido and Clauset).
    - Decision: report "heavy-tailed" with the fitted alternatives, not "scale-free", unless the strong criteria hold.
5. **Mixing.** Degree assortativity r (Newman) and attribute assortativity; r misleads with very high-degree nodes ([arXiv 0910.3377](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0910.3377)).
6. **Build the null ensemble (minutes to hours).** Generate 100-1000 degree-preserving randomizations by double-edge swaps (keeping each node's degree, or in- and out-degree for directed graphs; [Maslov and Sneppen 2002](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0205380)). Fosdick et al.: labeling is "inconsequential" for simple graphs but matters for multigraphs and self-loops, where the user must choose vertex- or stub-labeled sampling and record it. Tools: graph-tool `random_rewire`; NetworkX `random_reference`, `double_edge_swap`, and `sigma`/`omega` for small-worldness. Different randomization methods "can lead to divergent conclusions" ([De Clerck et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.05400)).
7. **Compare each statistic to the ensemble.** Clustering, path length, assortativity, modularity Q, and the rich-club coefficient normalized by the null (rho(k) = phi(k)/phi_rand(k)); an unnormalized rich-club curve rises even in Erdos-Renyi graphs, so only rho > 1 is evidence (Colizza et al.).
8. **Motifs.** Count 3- and 4-node subgraphs (FANMOD/ESU, mfinder, graph-tool `motifs`) and compute z = (N_real - mean N_rand) / sd N_rand. A spatial toy network with no motif-selecting rule produced the same "significant" motifs ([Artzy-Randrup et al. 2004](https://www.marcottelab.org/users/CH391L/Handouts/Networkmotifcomment1.pdf)), so the null must preserve whatever structure is not the hypothesis (degree, space, layers).
9. **Hand off.** A table of observed value, null mean, null sd and z for each statistic; a CCDF with fitted alternatives; a figure (Workflow G); code and seeds in the paper's repository.

### Workflow B -- Community detection that survives review

Sources: Traag, Waltman and van Eck 2019 (above); Fortunato and Barthelemy 2007 ([arXiv physics/0607100](https://arxiv.org/abs/physics/0607100)); Good, de Montjoye and Clauset 2010 (above); Lancichinetti and Fortunato 2012 ([Sci Rep 2:336](https://www.nature.com/articles/srep00336)); Fortunato and Hric 2016 ([arXiv 1608.00163](https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.00163)); Betzel 2020 (above); Peixoto 2023 ([arXiv 2112.00183](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.00183v7)).

**What current literature says (it corrects W04).**

- **Louvain is no longer the right default.** It can produce badly connected and even internally _disconnected_ communities: up to 25% badly connected and up to 16% disconnected in Traag et al.'s experiments, worse when iterated. Leiden guarantees connected communities, is faster and finds better partitions.
- **Modularity has a resolution limit.** Modules with about sqrt(2L) internal links or fewer may be merged with neighbors (Fortunato and Barthelemy). The Constant Potts Model (CPM) avoids this ([igraph cluster_leiden](https://r.igraph.org/reference/cluster_leiden.html)).
- **Modularity is degenerate.** It has exponentially many high-scoring, structurally different partitions and usually no clear maximum; its output "should be interpreted cautiously in scientific contexts" (Good et al.). So W04's success criterion "modularity > 0.3 means real communities" has no basis: Q is size-dependent and random graphs can reach high Q.
- **Descriptive vs inferential.** Peixoto argues modularity-style methods are descriptive; using them to infer latent groups is "full of pitfalls" and finds communities in fully random graphs. Fitting a stochastic block model (SBM) by minimum description length is the inferential alternative, and consensus clustering does not remove overfitting.
- **Girvan-Newman "for quality"** (W04) is a 2002 method superseded by Leiden, SBMs and Infomap.

**What the tools teach, and where defaults trap users.**

- **Gephi Modularity** is Louvain, with source defaults `isRandomized=false`, `useWeight=true`, `resolution=1.0` ([Gephi source](https://github.com/gephi/gephi/blob/master/modules/StatisticsPlugin/src/main/java/org/gephi/statistics/plugin/Modularity.java)). Tutorials say to choose a resolution "between 0.1 and 2" and color by Modularity Class ([Grandjean](https://www.martingrandjean.ch/introduction-to-network-visualization-gephi/); [Gephi quick start](https://www.bu.edu/networks/files/2012/08/gephi-tutorial-quick_start.pdf)); none found teaches reruns or stability. Gephi's SBM-based Statistical Inference (0.9.3) has no tutorial coverage found.
- **python-igraph `community_leiden`** defaults to `objective_function='CPM', resolution=1.0` ([API](https://python.igraph.org/en/stable/api/igraph.Graph.html)), and NetworkX 3.7's native `leiden_communities` defaults to CPM too ([source](https://github.com/networkx/networkx/blob/networkx-3.7/networkx/algorithms/community/leiden.py)): on an unweighted graph CPM at resolution 1 returns near-singletons. NetworkX `louvain_communities` has `seed=None`.

**The defensible sequence.**

1. **Prepare.** Work on the giant component or state how isolates are handled. Community methods treat weights as strengths, the opposite of path methods (Workflow C). For signed or correlation networks use a signed null or drop negative weights first; Infomap requires positive weights (Betzel).
2. **Pick the method by goal.** For "these groups exist in the generating process", fit an SBM by minimum description length (graph-tool, Gephi Statistical Inference). For description or a figure, Leiden (modularity or CPM). Louvain only to reproduce older work.
3. **Run many times with recorded seeds.** Betzel shows 100 Louvain runs giving several distinct solutions. Do not keep the single highest-Q partition; many near-degenerate partitions have almost equal Q.
4. **Sweep resolution.** gamma = 1 "is not special". Betzel's example: 0.5, 0.75 and 1.0 give 2, 3 and 4 communities. Pick the scales where repeated runs agree most, or keep several; Lambiotte's multi-scale modularity selects resolutions by "complementary measures of robustness" ([arXiv 1004.4268](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1004.4268)).
5. **Consensus.** Build a co-assignment matrix D (D_ij = fraction of runs in which i and j share a community), subtract a permutation null, recluster, repeat until stable (Betzel; Lancichinetti and Fortunato). For SBMs, sample the posterior and report node marginals.
6. **Compare partitions.** NMI, adjusted Rand or variation of information between runs, resolutions and known metadata. No tool tutorial reviewed teaches them; no source found showing Gephi computes them.
7. **Test against a null.** Compare observed Q with Q on degree-preserving randomizations; add distance to the null for spatially embedded networks (Betzel). Or fit the SBM, whose description length already penalizes structure a random graph would show.
8. **Characterize.** Sizes, internal density, attribute profiles, within-module degree z-score and participation coefficient ([Guimera and Amaral 2005](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2175124)). Check clusters against outside evidence; van Geenen et al. checked modularity clusters against profile bios, PageRank and cluster density "rather than treating the clusters as final".
9. **Hand off.** Partition at the chosen scales, a stability statistic, the null comparison, seeds and software versions, and a figure colored by community with uncertain nodes shown as uncertain.

### Workflow C -- Weighted path measures: deciding what a weight means

Sources: [igraph betweenness](https://r.igraph.org/reference/betweenness.html); igraph-help 2019 thread (above); [NetworkX betweenness](https://networkx.org/documentation/networkx-2.8/reference/algorithms/generated/networkx.algorithms.centrality.betweenness_centrality.html); [networkx-discuss thread](https://groups.google.com/g/networkx-discuss/c/-xLBfmpPkRg); [Opsahl, Agneessens and Skvoretz 2010](https://toreopsahl.com/wp-content/uploads/2010/04/node_centrality_in_weighted_networks1.pdf); Gephi [GraphDistance.java](https://github.com/gephi/gephi/blob/master/modules/StatisticsPlugin/src/main/java/org/gephi/statistics/plugin/GraphDistance.java), [ShortestPath.java](https://github.com/gephi/gephi/blob/master/modules/ToolsPlugin/src/main/java/org/gephi/tools/plugin/ShortestPath.java) and [issue 1816](https://github.com/gephi/gephi/issues/1816).

1. **Read what the weight is.** Interaction counts, co-authorships, trade volume and correlations are _strengths_. Kilometers, travel time and cost are _distances_.
2. **Know each tool's convention.** igraph: all path-based functions "interpret weights as distances" (maintainer, igraph-help). NetworkX betweenness: weights "are interpreted as distances"; tnet: "tnet considers a higher tie value to be stronger" and inverts internally. Gephi reads one weight column three ways in one session: Modularity and ForceAtlas2 read it as a strength (a heavier edge is a stronger tie, [gephi/gephi issue 1816](https://github.com/gephi/gephi/issues/1816)); the Network Diameter statistic (betweenness, closeness, eccentricity) runs unweighted BFS, since `GraphDistance.java` has no weight handling; and the interactive Shortest Path tool reads it as a distance, running Bellman-Ford on directed graphs and Dijkstra otherwise, adding `edgeWeight(edge)` to the path length ([ShortestPath.java](https://github.com/gephi/gephi/blob/master/modules/ToolsPlugin/src/main/java/org/gephi/tools/plugin/ShortestPath.java), [DijkstraShortestPathAlgorithm.java](https://github.com/gephi/gephi/blob/master/modules/AlgorithmsPlugin/src/main/java/org/gephi/algorithms/shortestpath/DijkstraShortestPathAlgorithm.java)). On strength data the drawn path therefore follows the weakest ties; a user on issue 1816 reports that the tool "chooses the one with lowest weight" although "higher the weight, stronger the nodes connection". No Gephi tutorial found warns about this. Neo4j Cypher's `SHORTEST` ignores weights and counts hops; the manual sends weighted paths to GDS ([Cypher shortest paths](https://neo4j.com/docs/cypher-manual/current/patterns/shortest-paths/)).
3. **Convert explicitly.** d = 1/w (Newman's convention, per Opsahl) or -log(w) for probabilities; results depend "on the specific monotonic transformation you chose" (igraph maintainer). Opsahl et al.'s alpha blends hop count (alpha = 0) and weight (alpha = 1).
4. **Or model counts as multi-edges**, which gives a different betweenness (igraph maintainer).
5. **Report the convention**, for example "betweenness on distances 1/w".

**Correction to W05.** "Should edge weights be considered?" is not enough: the decision is _what the weight means_ and _which conversion to use_.

### Workflow D -- Path queries beyond one shortest path

Sources: [NetworkX shortest_simple_paths](https://networkx.org/documentation/stable/reference/algorithms/generated/networkx.algorithms.simple_paths.shortest_simple_paths.html); [Neo4j Cypher shortest paths](https://neo4j.com/docs/cypher-manual/current/patterns/shortest-paths/); [Holme and Saramaki 2012](https://arxiv.org/abs/1108.1780).

| Query                            | Where it exists today                                                                            | Notes                                                                                                                   |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| One shortest path                | Everywhere; Gephi's click-to-path tool reads weights as distances, Cypher `SHORTEST` counts hops | The first query in most tutorials                                                                                       |
| All shortest paths (ties)        | NetworkX `all_shortest_paths`; Cypher `ALL SHORTEST`                                             | Showing one tie hides alternatives                                                                                      |
| k shortest loopless paths        | NetworkX `shortest_simple_paths` (Yen); Cypher `SHORTEST k`; Neo4j GDS Yen's                     | Cypher: when k cuts through a tie, the choice is arbitrary                                                              |
| Constrained by type or direction | Cypher quantified path patterns; NetworkX weight functions returning `None` to hide edges        | Investigators restrict to, say, money-transfer edges                                                                    |
| Time-respecting journeys         | pathpy, Raphtory, Reticula                                                                       | Not transitive; static aggregation overstates reachability (Holme and Saramaki); see `temporal-and-dynamic-networks.md` |

No time-respecting path function was found in the NetworkX, igraph or Gephi documentation reviewed.

**Correction to W05.** Add typed, directed and time-ordered paths; all-shortest and k-shortest with tie handling; the "no path" result with its reason (disconnected, or blocked by direction, type or time); and the weight convention used.

### Workflow E -- Hub interpretation with domain-aware normalization

Sources: Guimera and Amaral 2005 (above); [Gillis, Ballouz and Pavlidis 2014](https://repository.cshl.edu/id/eprint/29493/); [Schaefer et al. 2015](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/genetics/articles/10.3389/fgene.2015.00260/full); [Lucchetta et al. 2024, eLife](https://elifesciences.org/articles/99951.pdf); [Borgatti, Carley and Krackhardt 2006](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/on-the-robustness-of-centrality-measures-under-conditions-of-impe/); [Linkurious expand docs](https://doc.linkurious.com/user-manual/3.1/expand).

1. **Rank on several centralities** (degree, betweenness on distances, PageRank); high-betweenness, low-degree brokers are missed by degree alone (Programming Historian).
2. **Normalize against modules.** Within-module degree z_i and participation coefficient P_i: z >= 2.5 marks module hubs; provincial hubs (P <= 0.30) stay inside their module, connector hubs link many. About 80% of nodes link only within their own module (Guimera and Amaral).
3. **Normalize against collection effort.** A protein's degree largely reflects "the number of times it has been screened" (Schaefer et al.; Gillis et al.); study bias alone can produce the power law (Lucchetta et al.). In investigations, supernodes (a shared address, a bank) are usually artifacts; Linkurious flags nodes above a `superNodeThreshold`. Burt's constraint mostly tracks inverse network size ([Strategy Science 2024](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/stsc.2024.0297)).
4. **Normalize against a null.** Hub-to-hub connectivity (rich club) means something only relative to a degree-preserving null (Colizza et al.).
5. **State uncertainty.** Centrality accuracy declines smoothly as data error rises (Borgatti et al. 2006).
6. **Hand off.** A role label per hub (provincial or connector; artifact, infrastructure or genuine) with its evidence.

**Correction to W17.** "What degree threshold defines a hub?" has concrete answers: z >= 2.5 within a module, comparison with a null, normalization by collection effort, a supernode cap.

### Workflow F -- Measurement robustness before reporting

Sources: Borgatti et al. 2006 (above); [Martin and Niemeyer 2019](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/network-science/article/influence-of-measurement-errors-on-networks-estimating-the-robustness-of-centrality-measures/80DEBC5D57537B2244C4DBBB5AE0030E); van den Heuvel et al. 2017; [Serrano, Boguna and Vespignani 2009](https://arxiv.org/abs/0904.2389); [Atlas for the Aspiring Network Scientist](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2101.00863).

1. **Subsample.** Delete 5-50% of edges or nodes at random, recompute, and correlate rankings with the originals.
2. **Sweep thresholds for derived graphs.** Report results across densities. Proportional thresholds give more stable metrics but confound group differences in overall connectivity (van den Heuvel et al.). The disparity filter keeps edges significant locally at each node (alpha, often 0.05), though it can produce high centralization and weak communities (Atlas). It is built for weighted one-mode graphs; projections of two-mode data need a bipartite null (Workflow K).
3. **Rerun every stochastic method** (community detection, layouts, SBM fits, simulations) and report the spread.
4. **Alternative representations**: in- and out-degree, weighted and unweighted, each layer.
5. **Alternative nulls**: degree-preserving plus spatial or layer-preserving where relevant.

None of the GUI tutorials reviewed includes these steps.

**Correction to W03.** Its "Test" phase ("comparison to null models") names no method. It should name the ensembles of Workflow A, the statistical network models of `academic-social-network-research.md`, and the checks above.

### Workflow G -- Laying out, styling and reading a network figure

Sources: Grandjean tutorial and Gephi quick start (above); Venturini, Jacomy and Jensen, "What do we see when we look at networks" ([arXiv 1905.02202](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.02202)); [NetworkX spring_layout](https://networkx.org/documentation/stable/reference/generated/networkx.drawing.layout.spring_layout.html); [Gephi Lite differences page](https://docs.gephi.org/lite/user-manual/differences-with-gephi/).

For the applied analyst the drawing is the analysis: Venturini et al. call the practice visual network analysis and treat a force-directed layout as a method for reading structure, not decoration.

**The steps tutorials teach (Grandjean, Gephi 0.9-0.11).**

1. **Size nodes** by a ranking (degree, range 10-150).
2. **Lay out:** Fruchterman-Reingold (area 10000, gravity 10) "until the graph stabilizes", then ForceAtlas2 with Prevent Overlap and Scaling 10. Venturini et al. add LinLog and gravity 0 for clearer clusters; no "catch-all setting" exists.
3. **Color** by a partition (Modularity Class) or a category; brightness for continuous variables, hue for categories (Venturini et al.).
4. **Label** by node size above a threshold, trying more than one sizing variable.
5. **Export** SVG or PDF for Inkscape; PNG gives "a poor resolution" (Grandjean).

**The reading protocol (Venturini et al.).** Read groups and empty space, not individual positions: "It is not the node positions that count, but the density of nodes and its variation." Non-circular shapes indicate polarization, dense regions surrounded by sparse ones are clusters (Burt's structural holes), and bridges sit between regions. Then see which attributes align with the poles and clusters. To name clusters, add "qualifying nodes" (genres, labels) after freezing the main layout ("double spatialisation").

**Decisions.** Algorithm and parameters; when to stop; seed; size and color variables; label threshold; filters; export format.

**How a layout misleads.**

- **No meaningful distances or axes.** In the authors' jazz network, layout distance correlated poorly with shortest-path distance (R^2 0.167) and almost not at all with random-walk commute distance (R^2 0.0025). Force layouts "do not produce coherent axes".
- **Stochastic.** Layouts start from random coordinates unless seeded (NetworkX `spring_layout` takes `seed`), so two runs differ.
- **Visual clusters vs computed communities.** k-means cuts of the layout agreed with Louvain more for the karate club than for the jazz network; a reader can see a cluster the partition splits, or miss one it finds.
- **Scale.** Keeping every node visible "precludes the use of VNA on graphs of millions of nodes and edges"; aggregation is needed there (Workflow K).

**Where tools stop.** Gephi records none of it; Gephi Lite exports images only, "not SVG or PDF".

### Workflow H -- Simulating spreading on a network

Sources: NDlib ([Rossetti et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.05854); [model reference](https://ndlib.readthedocs.io/en/latest/reference/reference.html)); EoN ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2001.02436); [fast_SIR](https://epidemicsonnetworks.readthedocs.io/en/latest/functions/EoN.fast_SIR.html)); Pastor-Satorras et al., "Epidemic processes in complex networks", Reviews of Modern Physics 2015 ([arXiv 1408.2701](https://arxiv.org/abs/1408.2701)); [muxViz](https://www.github.com/manlius/muxViz).

1. **Choose the process.** NDlib ships 22 epidemic models (SI to SEIR, threshold, cascades) and 14 opinion models; EoN has "over 100 methods".
2. **Set parameters.** Transmission and recovery rates (EoN `fast_SIR(G, tau, gamma, ...)`), thresholds, per-edge or per-node weights.
3. **Choose seeds.** A random fraction (`rho`), named nodes (`initial_infecteds`), or a strategy such as highest degree.
4. **Run many realizations** with recorded random states. Papers report the mean and spread of prevalence or final size.
5. **Plot.** Prevalence curves across parameter settings, and final size against transmission rate to locate the epidemic threshold, which depends on degree heterogeneity (Pastor-Satorras et al.).
6. **Compare against a null.** Rerun on degree-preserving randomizations to see whether clustering or communities, not degree alone, change the outcome.
7. **Watch one run.** EoN's `return_full_data=True` keeps every node's status history to draw or animate a run; muxViz animates multilayer dynamics; NDlib has a web front end for non-programmers.

### Workflow I -- Percolation and robustness to failure and attack

Sources: Albert, Jeong and Barabasi 2000 ([arXiv cond-mat/0008064](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0008064)); Holme, Kim, Yoon and Han 2002 ([arXiv cond-mat/0202410](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0202410)); Schneider, Moreira, Andrade, Havlin and Herrmann, "Mitigation of malicious attacks on networks", PNAS 108(10), 2011 ([PMC3053993](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3053993), [arXiv 1103.1741](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/1103.1741)); Zeng and Liu, "Enhancing network robustness against malicious attacks", Phys. Rev. E 85, 066130, 2012 ([arXiv 1203.2982](https://arxiv.org/abs/1203.2982)); Wandelt, Sun, Feng, Zanin and Havlin, "A comparative analysis of approaches to network-dismantling", Scientific Reports 8, 13513, 2018 ([PMC6131543](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6131543)).

Why a single number matters: the critical fraction at which the giant component vanishes is the classic summary, but Schneider et al. argue it "may not be useful in many realistic cases". They define R, the average over every removal step of the largest component's share: R = (1/N) times the sum, for Q = 1 to N removed nodes, of s(Q), the fraction of nodes in the largest component after Q removals. R runs from 1/N (a star) to 0.5 (a fully connected graph). After rewiring that keeps every node's degree, their networks' percolation threshold "remains practically the same", while R rises -- by 45% for the European power grid with about 5% of its links changed. Two networks, or one network before and after a change, can therefore share a critical fraction and still differ sharply in robustness; R tells them apart and the critical fraction does not.

1. **Pick removal strategies:** random failure, and targeted attack in decreasing order of degree or betweenness.
2. **Decide whether to recompute** the ranking after each removal. Holme et al. find recalculated attacks often more harmful. Wandelt et al. benchmarked 13 dismantling methods by R and found the static betweenness attack "much worse" than degree attacks, because betweenness changes sharply after a few removals and needs "an iterative computation"; recomputed betweenness gave the lowest R in about 70-80% of their networks. Recomputing therefore changes R, so the choice is part of the result and is recorded with it. Its cost is high: they report recomputed betweenness scales "with the cube of the number of nodes" and recommend a sampled approximation for large networks.
3. **Remove step by step** and record the giant component's share of nodes S against the fraction removed f.
4. **Repeat random removal** many times with recorded seeds and plot the mean curve with its spread.
5. **Compute R per strategy.** For targeted attacks one R per strategy and recompute setting; for random failure the mean R over the runs and its spread (standard deviation or range), since each run gives its own R.
6. **Read the curves.** Albert et al. showed that scale-free networks keep a giant component under random failure but break quickly under attack on hubs, while random graphs fall apart beyond a threshold (about f = 0.28 in their exponential network). Rank or compare networks, or the same network before and after a change, by R.
7. **Edge removal, if failures hit links** (power lines, flight routes). Zeng and Liu define a link-robustness index on the same pattern, averaged over the number of links, under removal of the link with the highest edge betweenness at each step. Report it separately: they find that raising node robustness "cannot guarantee" better link robustness.
8. **Hand off.** S(f) curves per strategy, R per strategy (with its spread for random runs), the critical fraction, the recompute setting, the seeds, and the order of removed nodes.

Tools that run the removal sequence today:

- graph-tool's [`vertex_percolation(g, vertices)`](https://graph-tool.skewed.de/static/docs/stable/autosummary/graph_tool.topology.vertex_percolation.html) takes a removal order and returns the largest component's size before each removal, by the Newman-Ziff method in O(V + E) time; its documented example compares a degree-ordered attack with a shuffled (random) order. The order is fixed in advance, so it covers static attacks only; R is the mean of the returned sizes divided by N. `edge_percolation` is the link counterpart.
- [networkx-robustness](https://pypi.org/project/networkx-robustness/) (0.0.7, 2023) offers `simulate_random_attack`, `simulate_degree_attack`, `simulate_betweenness_attack`, `simulate_closeness_attack` and an eigenvector attack, each taking `attack_fraction` and returning the giant component's initial share, its share after each removal, and the average path length. Its [source](https://github.com/tsantanam/networkx-robustness) ranks nodes once before removal (no recomputation), and the random attack takes no seed. It does not compute R.
- [NetworkDismantling](https://github.com/hubsw/NetworkDismantling), the code behind Wandelt et al.'s benchmark (free for academic use), implements the 13 static and iterative strategies they compare by R.

None of these computes R, its spread over random runs, and both static and recomputed rankings in one call; analysts write that loop themselves. That loop is what `attack-robustness-curves` should own.

### Workflow J -- Multilayer networks analyzed as multilayer

Sources: Mucha et al. 2010 ([arXiv 0911.1824](https://arxiv.org/abs/0911.1824)); [GenLouvain](https://github.com/GenLouvain/GenLouvain); Bazzi et al. 2016 ([arXiv 1501.00040](https://arxiv.org/abs/1501.00040)); De Domenico, Porter and Arenas 2015 ([arXiv 1405.0843](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1405.0843)); [muxViz](https://www.github.com/manlius/muxViz); [emln README](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/emln/readme/README.html).

1. **Represent layers plus interlayer links.** Layers are relation types (multiplex), time windows or sites; interlayer links connect each node to itself in other layers (Mucha et al.).
2. **Choose the coupling omega and resolution gamma.** Multilayer modularity (Mucha et al.; GenLouvain) trades "static community structure within layers" against "persistence across layers" (Bazzi et al.). Sweep omega together with gamma.
3. **Read communities per layer.** Each node gets one community per layer; compute persistence (GenLouvain helper functions) and look for nodes that switch.
4. **Other analyses:** multilayer centrality, reducibility (can layers merge?), motifs (muxViz).
5. **Show it** as stacked planes (muxViz, `emln::plot_multilayer()`) or one graph with edges colored by layer.

### Workflow K -- Two-mode data and very large graphs

**Two-mode (bipartite) data.** Person-organization, author-paper, user-hashtag and species-site data are everyday input. Sources: [NetworkX collaboration_weighted_projected_graph](https://networkx.org/documentation/latest/reference/algorithms/generated/networkx.algorithms.bipartite.projection.collaboration_weighted_projected_graph.html); [backbone package](https://cran.r-project.org/package=backbone) and its paper ([Neal et al., arXiv 2203.11055](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.11055)); [graph-tool mailing list](https://archives.skewed.de/hyperkitty/list/graph-tool@skewed.de/thread/WQXERTSY54S4FIZUH7GOD6DYBQX46O4B).

1. **Keep the two-mode graph** as the source of truth and record which side is which.
2. **If projecting, choose the weighting:** simple shared-neighbor count, or Newman's collaboration weighting, where each shared paper adds 1/(n_p - 1) for its n_p authors (NetworkX).
3. **Extract a backbone against a bipartite null,** not a global threshold. The `backbone` package's `backbone_from_projection()` defaults to the stochastic degree sequence model (SDSM), which approximately preserves both sides' degrees; the fixed degree sequence model (FDSM) preserves them exactly. Its input "must be the bipartite network ... and not the weighted projection".
4. **Or analyze directly:** a bipartite SBM in graph-tool (Peixoto: the algorithm "will always learn that you have a bipartite network"), the ecology document's bipartite modularity and nestedness, or, when the second mode is a group (a paper, an email), Workflow M.

**Graphs of 10^8 to 10^9 edges.** SNAP's com-Friendster has 65.6 million nodes and 1.8 billion edges ([SNAP](https://snap.stanford.edu/data/com-Friendster.html)). Nobody draws that node by node (Venturini et al., above). Analysts reduce first: sample, take a k-core, extract a backbone, or coarse-grain. graph-tool's nested SBM gives "different levels of coarse-graining" (297 nodes into 16 groups into 5 in the demo), and `draw()` renders the hierarchy interactively ([inference demo](https://graph-tool.skewed.de/static/doc/demos/inference/inference.html)).

### Workflow L -- Benchmarking a community method against planted partitions

Sources: Lancichinetti, Fortunato and Radicchi 2008 ([arXiv 0805.4770](https://arxiv.org/html/0805.4770v4)); Fortunato and Hric 2016 (above).

1. **Generate graphs with known communities.** LFR takes n, degree and community-size exponents (tau1 2-3, tau2 1-2), size bounds and the mixing parameter mu (each node's share of links leaving its community); planted-partition SBMs are simpler.
2. **Sweep mu** from 0 past 0.5, where communities stop being defined in the strong sense.
3. **Run every method** on each graph, with seeds.
4. **Score against the planted labels** with normalized mutual information (or adjusted Rand).
5. **Average over realizations** (100 graphs per point in the original).
6. **Plot NMI against mu,** one curve per method, with error bars.

### Workflow M -- Group interactions kept as groups (hypergraphs and simplicial complexes)

Sources: Battiston et al., Physics Reports 2020 ([arXiv 2006.01764](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.01764)); Bick et al., SIAM Review 2023 ([arXiv 2104.11329](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.11329)); XGI ([Landry et al. 2023, JOSS](https://joss.theoj.org/papers/10.21105/joss.05162); [repository](https://github.com/xgi-org/xgi); [read/write API](https://xgi.readthedocs.io/en/stable/api/readwrite.html)); HyperNetX ([Praggastis et al., JOSS 2024](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2310.11626); [overview](https://hypernetx.readthedocs.io/en/latest/overview/)); Hypergraphx ([Lotito et al. 2023, Journal of Complex Networks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.15356)); others inline.

**The data and who analyzes it.** Much relational data records groups, not pairs: an email's recipients, a paper's authors, a classroom contact logged by proximity sensors, a drug's substances, a question's tags, a bill's cosponsors. These are the 17 timestamped "sequence[s] of simplices" of Benson et al.'s simplicial-closure study ([PNAS 2018, arXiv 1802.06916](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.06916); [data page](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/~arb/data/)); XGI-DATA holds 50+ curated datasets of face-to-face contacts, emails, co-authorships, legislation and ecology. Battiston et al. name face-to-face communication, chemical reactions and ecological systems as interactions that "cannot be simply described just in terms of simple dyads". The analysts are network physicists and applied mathematicians (the authors above), the PNNL group behind HyperNetX, and network neuroscientists: Santoro, Battiston, Petri and Amico build higher-order signals from products of z-scored fMRI time series ([arXiv 2203.10702](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.10702)) and applied them to 100 Human Connectome Project subjects ([Nature Communications 2024 record](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/higher-order-connectomics-of-human-brain-function-reveals-local-t/)). No source found for step-by-step higher-order practice in team science, chemistry or contact tracing beyond these datasets.

**The steps, as the libraries teach them.**

1. **Load.** `xgi.load_xgi_data(...)` fetches an XGI-DATA set in one line; XGI also reads the Hypergraphx and AHORN repositories, edge lists, bipartite edge lists, incidence matrices and HIF. HyperNetX 2.x builds on pandas data frames with attributes on nodes, hyperedges and incidences (a node's membership in a hyperedge, such as author position).
2. **Choose the representation, explicitly.** A hypergraph (any group), a simplicial complex (every subset of a group is also present; needed for homology and simplicial contagion), a directed hypergraph (reaction inputs to outputs), or the bipartite node-to-group graph. Battiston et al. compare hypergraphs, simplicial complexes and bipartite graphs as related frameworks. The bipartite graph keeps the full incidence information; the clique projection (every pair inside a group becomes an edge) does not: three separate two-person emails among A, B and C and one three-person email give the same projected triangle.
3. **Describe.** Hyperedge-size distribution, node degree (XGI `H.edges.size`, `H.nodes.degree`), components, assortativity and clustering.
4. **Paths and centrality with a width.** HyperNetX's s-walks require consecutive hyperedges to share at least s nodes, so a walk has a length and a width; s-components, s-closeness and s-betweenness follow, and every ordinary graph walk is a 1-walk ([Aksoy et al. 2020, EPJ Data Science](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.11295)). XGI offers H-eigenvector, Z-eigenvector (uniform hypergraphs only), clique-eigenvector, Katz, line-vector and node-edge centralities ([XGI centrality API](https://xgi.readthedocs.io/en/stable/api/algorithms/xgi.algorithms.centrality.html)).
5. **Communities.** HyperNetX's `hypergraph_modularity` scores a partition with a chosen hyperedge weighting (strict: credit only when every member is in one part; majority: more than half; linear), `kumar` runs the IRMM algorithm, and `last_step` refines a start, typically Louvain on the two-section (projected) graph ([HNX modularity docs](https://hypernetx.readthedocs.io/en/latest/modularity.html)). Chodrow, Veldt and Benson's degree-corrected hypergraph SBM yields a modularity with resolution and edge-size parameters ([Science Advances 2021, arXiv 2101.09611](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.09611)). A simulation comparison (at most 1,000 nodes, for compute cost) found the all-or-nothing hypergraph Louvain (Julia HyperModularity) best at recovering planted clusters, with one `last_step` run over 15,000 seconds ([Poda and Matias 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.14028)).
6. **Topology.** HyperNetX's simplicial homology finds holes of different dimensions in simplicial complexes built from "modestly sized" hypergraphs (HNX JOSS paper).
7. **Dynamics.** In simplicial contagion, a filled triangle infects at its own rate on top of pairwise infection; this yields a discontinuous transition with a bistable region, an account of why social change needs a critical mass ([Iacopini et al. 2019, Nature Communications](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6554271)). Hypergraphx and XGI simulate dynamics; HNX has a contagion tutorial.
8. **Draw.** XGI draws hyperedges as filled hulls over a seeded layout (`pos = xgi.barycenter_spring_layout(H, seed=1)`, then `xgi.draw(H, pos=pos)`). HNX-Widget in Jupyter lays out the bipartite form by force and draws hyperedges as convex hulls (Euler-style); users move and pin nodes and map size and color to metadata. PAOHvis draws nodes as rows and each hyperedge as a vertical line with dots at its members, per time slot and without overlaps, for medium-sized dynamic hypergraphs ([Valdivia et al., IEEE TVCG](https://aviz.fr/Research/paohvis)). Fischer et al.'s survey reports that Euler- and Venn-style drawings tell hyperedges apart by color, which severely limits their scale, and that interactive hypergraph visualization is rarely studied ([IEEE VIS 2021, arXiv 2107.13936](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.13936)). No source found giving a measured hyperedge count at which hull drawings fail.

**When a projection is acceptable, and when it gives wrong answers.**

- **Acceptable:** storing the bipartite graph (it is the incidence matrix); linear dynamics, which on a hypergraph reduce to a static weighted pairwise network, so a weighted projection gives the same answer; multibody effects appear only with nonlinear group interactions ([Neuhauser, Mellor and Lambiotte 2020, arXiv 1910.09226](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.09226)).
- **Wrong:** when the group is the unit of analysis. Clique expansion loses which pairs met in the same group; hypergraph clustering finds planted clusters in regimes where graph methods on the projection provably fail (Chodrow et al.); the discontinuous contagion transition does not exist in the pairwise model (Iacopini et al.); and pairwise statistics on a projection need a bipartite null (Workflow K).
- **Exchange:** each library had its own representation; HIF, a JSON schema for hypergraphs, directed hypergraphs and simplicial complexes with attributes on nodes, edges and incidences, was published in 2025 to fix that ([arXiv 2507.11520](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.11520)), XGI reads and writes it and HyperNetX's developers are its main contributors.

### Notes on anomalies (W12)

- **Structural, in research:** OddBall fits power-law relations between egonet features and scores each node by distance from the fit ([OddBall](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/user/lakoglu/pubs/oddball_TR.pdf); [Akoglu, Tong and Koutra 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1404.4679)). It assumes most nodes follow the pattern.
- **Fraud, cyber and biology** anomaly workflows are in `financial-crime-fraud-aml-kyc.md`, `cybersecurity-graph-work.md` and Workflow E's study-bias check.

**Correction to W12.** "How many standard deviations makes something anomalous?" assumes roughly normal metrics. Degree and betweenness are heavy-tailed, so z-scores on raw centrality flag the tail by construction. Use log or rank scores, deviation from a fitted pattern, or within-module z, and define "normal" per node type.
