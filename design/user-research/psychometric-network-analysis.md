# Psychological Network Analysts: Symptom, Attitude and Personality Networks Estimated from Survey Items

## 1. Summary

Psychological network analysts are clinical, personality, social and health psychologists, psychiatric epidemiologists and psychometricians who model a questionnaire as a network: each item (a symptom, a belief, a mood rating) is a node, and each edge is a statistical relation estimated from a respondent-by-item data matrix, usually a regularized partial correlation. Nobody observes these edges. They are estimated, so they are signed, weighted, sparse by design and uncertain, and the field's reporting standards require bootstrapped intervals on edges and a stability check before any node ranking is reported. The work is done almost entirely in R (qgraph, bootnet, mgm, IsingFit, NetworkComparisonTest, psychonetrics, mlVAR, graphicalVAR, EGAnet, bnlearn, ResIN) or in JASP's point-and-click Network module, which wraps the same packages. Graphs are small (typically 5 to 50 nodes), but the questions are large: is this edge real, is this ranking stable, did the network change between groups or over time, and does a figure invite a reading the data cannot support?

A second audience reads these networks without estimating them: therapists, and their patients, who get one patient's network from experience-sampling data as a printout or in a browser dashboard. Their needs (lay-readable encoding, values on the node, a one-page sheet, explanation text) differ sharply from the researchers', and they are the audience an embeddable web component serves most directly.

## 2. Population

**Who they are.**

- **Clinical psychology and psychiatry researchers.** PhD students, postdocs and faculty studying depression, PTSD, anxiety, psychosis, eating disorders, addiction and comorbidity; the core of the field. A systematic review of network psychopathology from 2008 to 2018 found 363 articles: 98 theoretical, 61 methodological and 204 empirical. Of 170 empirical papers using network psychometrics, 141 analyzed cross-sectional data and 32 time-series data. Depression appeared in 69 articles and PTSD in 31 (Robinaugh et al. 2020, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7334828).
- **Intensive longitudinal (experience sampling, EMA) researchers.** They prompt participants several times a day on a phone and estimate temporal and contemporaneous networks for a group or one patient. A scoping review of 43 temporal network articles found 98% used R, mostly multilevel VAR (https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/s13428-022-01839-y.pdf).
- **Therapists who receive per-patient network feedback, and their patients.** In trials and some clinics, a patient's EMA data are turned into a personal network and handed to the therapist (Section 3, Persona E; Workflow 7). They do not run R.
- **Personality psychologists.** They model trait items, for example the Big Five Inventory items in the `bfi` dataset, as networks instead of latent factors (https://arxiv.org/pdf/1609.04156v2).
- **Social and political psychologists and sociologists (belief network analysts).** Two strands. The Causal Attitude Network work models beliefs and feelings as item-level nodes; Dalege et al. used American National Election Studies (ANES) data from 1980 to 2012 (n = 18,795), six belief and four feeling items per election (https://arxiv.org/pdf/1705.00193.pdf). Response Item Network (ResIN) analysis instead makes every response option its own node and reads the layout's x axis as the latent ideological dimension (Workflow 10). This strand treats node position as a measurement, unlike the rest of the field.
- **Psychometricians doing dimensionality and scale development.** They use Exploratory Graph Analysis (EGAnet) to find how many dimensions a scale has and which items are unstable or redundant (Workflow 8). EGAnet had more 2025 CRAN downloads than bootnet (table below).
- **Causal-discovery users.** A smaller strand learns directed acyclic graphs (Bayesian networks) from cross-sectional symptom data with bnlearn, following a Psychological Methods tutorial by Briganti, Scutari and McNally (Workflow 9).
- **Applied health researchers.** Nurses, public-health and education researchers and psychiatrists publish "network analysis" papers of symptom scales, many during COVID-19, reporting strength, bridge centrality and NCT comparisons (for example https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10341738). No source counts them. Two bootnet issues show their scale: 2,000 respondents with 60 to 70 nodes (https://github.com/SachaEpskamp/bootnet/issues/139), and segments of 2,000 to 10,000+ respondents with 50+ nodes (https://github.com/SachaEpskamp/bootnet/issues/113).
- **Methodologists.** Concentrated at the University of Amsterdam (Psychosystems), the National University of Singapore (Sacha Epskamp: https://fass.nus.edu.sg/psy/people/sacha-epskamp/), Leiden, Utrecht and Groningen. They write the packages, tutorials and reporting standards and teach the workshops.

**Adjacent population: semantic network researchers (verbal fluency and free association).** Cognitive and clinical psychologists build semantic memory networks from word lists, not Likert items. No other research document in this folder covers them. The SemNA pipeline (Christensen and Kenett, Psychological Methods, https://neuroaesthetics.med.upenn.edu/assets/user-content/documents/publications/2022-16674-001.pdf; supplement doi 10.1037/met0000463.supp) runs:

1. Collect verbal fluency data: participants name category members (for example animals) for about 60 seconds. The tutorial's dataset has 516 participants split into low and high openness-to-experience groups.
2. Preprocess with SemNetDictionaries and SemNetCleaner: `textcleaner(data, miss = 99, partBY = "row", dictionary = "animals")` spell-checks interactively, converts "monikers" and plurals, removes inappropriate and duplicate responses, and returns `responses$clean` (responses in typed order) and `responses$binary` (participants by unique words, 1 if given). The manual spell-check decisions are saved for reviewers (vignette: https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/SemNetCleaner/vignettes/Preprocessing.html).
3. Estimate one network per group in SemNeT's Shiny app: community network, naive random walk, pathfinder, or correlation-based networks filtered with TMFG. For correlation-based networks, responses are "equated" so every group keeps only responses all groups gave, giving the same node set in each group.
4. Compare groups: global measures (average shortest path length, clustering coefficient, modularity Q) against random networks; case-wise bootstrap (resample participants, usually 1,000 times) or node-wise bootstrap (estimate on random subsets of nodes, for example 50%); random walks; spreading activation (spreadr). Effect sizes are partial eta squared.
5. Export with `convert2igraph`, `convert2cytoscape` (a CSV for Cytoscape), or `convert2snafu` for the Python SNAFU library's GUI.

Nodes are words (often 100+), shared across groups only after equating, and drawn in Cytoscape or R. The needs overlap with this document (two networks on one node set, bootstrap comparison) but the data type and import path differ, so it warrants its own document.

Caveats: CRAN counts include automated installs and dependency pulls. qgraph is inflated by 58 reverse imports, including `semPlot`, which SEM users install (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/qgraph/index.html). bootnet and EGAnet are the better proxies for people who estimate networks. No JASP Network module count, and no count of clinicians receiving network feedback, was found.

**How often they touch graph tools.** In bursts, one per paper: one dataset, one to four networks, a few days of R work, then revisions when reviewers ask for stability checks. Methodologists and ESM labs work continuously. Therapists see a network once per patient, at the start of treatment.

**Skill profile.** Strong in statistics and in the field's vocabulary (EBIC, gamma, strength, expected influence, CS-coefficient); low to moderate generic graph literacy. Bringmann et al. had to explain that betweenness and closeness assume flow along shortest paths, which does not fit psychological variables (https://research-collection.ethz.ch/bitstream/20.500.11850/383309.1/1/Bringmannetal.2019JAP.pdf). Most copy tutorial code; a substantial minority use only JASP. Recent bootnet issue titles are mostly "how do I" and error questions (https://github.com/SachaEpskamp/bootnet/issues?q=is%3Aissue). Therapists have no network training beyond a session on reading the feedback.

**Team context.** Small labs around one PI. Data come from the lab's survey or ESM study, cohorts (NESDA, ANES) or open repositories. A methods-savvy student often runs the analysis for clinical co-authors. Code and data increasingly go on OSF.

## 3. Personas

### Persona A: Symptom network researcher (clinical PhD student or postdoc)

- **Background.** Third-year clinical PhD student studying PTSD and depression comorbidity in a trauma clinic sample (N about 400, 20 to 30 items). Learned R from a methods course and the bootnet tutorial; attended one workshop of the kind Isvoranu and Tio teach (https://uol.de/p56840c31130).
- **Tools.** RStudio with bootnet (`estimateNetwork`, `bootnet`, `corStability`, `differenceTest`), qgraph, networktools (bridge centrality, expected influence), NetworkComparisonTest. SPSS for cleaning.
- **Data.** `.sav` or CSV of respondents by items, ordinal 0-3 or 1-5, a few percent missing, grouping variables.
- **Goals.** Find bridge and central symptoms in a form reviewers accept under the reporting standards (https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/184239260/ContentServer_2_.pdf).
- **Frustrations.** Case-dropping bootstraps that run for hours. Betweenness fails the stability check, matching the tutorial's betweenness CS = 0.05 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5809547). Two group networks get different spring layouts and must be forced into one. Labels overlap in the interval plot (https://github.com/SachaEpskamp/bootnet/issues/108). "Not positive definite" errors with ordinal data (https://github.com/SachaEpskamp/bootnet/issues/129).

### Persona B: Point-and-click network analyst (applied health researcher in JASP)

- **Background.** A nursing or public-health researcher, or MSc student, who uses JASP because it is free and needs no code.
- **Tools.** JASP's Network module: estimators EBICglasso, correlation, partial correlation, huge, IsingFit, IsingSampler, mgm; "Split by" for groups; Bootstrap Network; graphical options (https://raw.githubusercontent.com/jasp-stats/jaspNetwork/master/inst/help/NetworkAnalysis.md). Exports PNG into Word.
- **Data.** 8 to 30 scale items from 200 to 3,000 respondents.
- **Goals.** A publishable network figure, centrality plot and stability plot in an afternoon.
- **Frustrations.** A JASP 0.19.3 user wrote that when classifying groups with colors "the variables get the wrong color", that bootstraps gave "peculiar graphs, which do not seem to be correct", and later that the bootstrap plot "is different from what I am used to. I don't understand how to interpret it" (https://forum.cogsci.nl/discussion/9830). Another reported that mgm and IsingFit terminated unexpectedly (via search snippet, https://forum.cogsci.nl/discussion/comment/26597/). Real gaps, checked against the module's R source (https://github.com/jasp-stats/jaspNetwork/blob/master/R/networkanalysis.R): no NetworkComparisonTest call, no edge or centrality difference matrix across groups, and no temporal estimators. JASP does offer expected influence (a centrality-table column and a centrality-plot toggle alongside betweenness, closeness and strength) and computes one shared layout across Split-by groups with `qgraph::averageLayout(networks, layout, repulsion)`; the help file does not mention either.

### Persona C: Experience sampling dynamics researcher

- **Background.** A postdoc in a psychiatry department running an ESM study: 60 to 120 participants, 5 prompts a day for 14 to 30 days, 6 to 12 mood and symptom items. Produces the per-patient networks Persona E reads.
- **Tools.** mlVAR (group temporal, contemporaneous, between-subjects networks), graphicalVAR (single patient), psychonetrics `panelgvar`, qgraph. PECAN-style questionnaires where patients rate which symptom causes which (https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/303680451/How_perceived_causal_networks_can_complement_case_conceptualization_diagnostic_classification_and_data-based_networks.pdf).
- **Data.** Long table, one row per prompt (person, day, beep, timestamp, items 0-100 or 1-7). Median study: 66 participants, about 60 scheduled time points, 7 nodes (scoping review).
- **Goals.** Show what predicts what over time (directed, lagged), what co-occurs (undirected), and how people differ.
- **Frustrations.** Three networks on different scales need one layout but separate maxima: the mlVAR paper drew its temporal network "with a different maximum value" (arXiv 1609.04156). Self-loops and reciprocal edges clutter small figures. Idiographic models "varied widely across participants and differed markedly from the client's own predictions" (pilot via search snippet, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32838671/).

### Persona D: Network psychometrics methodologist and workshop teacher

- **Background.** Faculty who develop estimators and teach one-day courses. The IMPS 2026 short course (Epskamp and Isvoranu) covered cross-sectional estimation in bootnet and JASP, then longitudinal, multi-group and latent-variable models in psychonetrics (https://www.psychometricsociety.org/post/psychometric-network-modeling).
- **Tools.** psychonetrics (`ggm`, `prune`, `modelsearch`, `panelgvar`), BGGM and easybgm, `netSimulator`, EGAnet, qgraph themes.
- **Goals.** Move the field from exploratory pictures toward confirmatory tests and honest uncertainty.
- **Frustrations.** Applied users read node distance in spring layouts as meaning and read intervals that include zero as "non-significant"; the standards reject both. Nothing in drawing tools enforces the standards.

### Persona E: Therapist receiving per-patient network feedback (with the patient as co-reader)

- **Background.** A licensed or trainee psychotherapist in an outpatient clinic. Before therapy, the patient answers EMA prompts for two weeks; the therapist then receives the patient's network. In the TheraNet project (Hall, Lappenbusch, Wiegmann and Rubel 2024, Administration and Policy in Mental Health, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11703985), 34 therapists were invited, 24 had been trained, and 18 took part in five focus groups (October 2022 to January 2023).
- **Tools.** A printout produced by researchers (TheraNet), or a platform dashboard. m-Path lists "Network visualisations" ("Explore relations between variables using network graphs to study co-occurrence and interdependencies") and describes its dashboard as a "visual workspace" for "therapists and SCED researchers", with interactive graphs stored in the dashboard or exported as PDFs; the page does not say which graph types the clinical dashboard includes (https://m-path.io/visual-feedback/). Other platforms are RoQua, PsyMate and PETRA; the Therap-i module gives "descriptive and network graphs" in feedback sessions at weeks 2, 4 and 8 (Bartels et al. 2023, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10559801/).
- **Data.** One patient: about 56 prompts (four a day for 14 days), 0-100 sliders, up to three personalized items, open-text event descriptions.
- **Goals.** Understand the patient faster, support case formulation, choose and explain interventions. Three TheraNet therapists "based parts of their therapy plan or particular interventions on information from the networks"; others used it "for their own understanding of the patient (i.e., as a way to support their case formulation)"; some discussed it "directly with their patients in-session".
- **Frustrations (TheraNet, verbatim).** Wished "it to be a bit more intuitive, that you didn't have to look at the [influence] table on the side". "The confusion in the interpretations may have come through the colors used in the network" (red and green edges). A patient found it "too overwhelming". Four therapists said "the interpretation was generally not clear to them anymore" after training; one, with a later patient, that "the interpretation of the network was no longer clear to her". Two presumed it "too complex or too time-consuming to explain in-session". They asked for "one to two sentences printed directly underneath the network models", and the authors call for "more continuous training or more readily available interpretation aides" and "digitalized options that integrate into existing software solutions". A 2025 thesis summarizes the wider literature: practitioners "report uncertainty in interpreting complex outputs, such as network graphs, and prefer more conventional representations like time series, bar charts, or pie charts" (https://repository.tudelft.nl/record/uuid:1073ad9f-2785-4ba8-8156-401643447df6). In an experiment with 40 practitioners and clinical master's students, adding textual descriptions of effect size and confidence to ESM charts raised both accuracy and confidence (Piot et al. 2025, Internet Interventions 42:100873, https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/evaluating-visual-feedback-of-experience-sampling-data-for-mental/); that study did not test network graphs.

## 5. Step-by-step workflows

### Workflow 1: Cross-sectional symptom network to published figure (bootnet and qgraph)

Sources: Epskamp, Borsboom and Fried 2018 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5809547); Epskamp and Fried 2018 (https://arxiv.org/pdf/1607.01367); Burger et al. 2023 reporting standards.

1. **Clean data (hours to days).** Export the respondent-by-item matrix as `.sav` or CSV. Drop skip-structure items and items that are deterministic functions of others; report missingness.
2. **Check redundancy (30 minutes).** Two items measuring the same thing make one large edge and inflate centrality. Burger et al. merged CES-D items with overlapping content and r >= .50, going from 15 to 12 nodes. Tools: `networktools::goldbricker` and `net_reduce` (https://rdrr.io/cran/networktools/src/R/goldbricker.R), or `EGAnet::UVA`. Only 11 of 43 temporal articles checked redundancy.
3. **Choose correlation and estimator.** Ordinal items use `cor_auto` (polychoric), Spearman or nonparanormal. Default `estimateNetwork(data, default = "EBICglasso")` runs the graphical lasso over 100 lambda values and picks the EBIC minimum. Alternatives: `ggmModSelect`, `IsingFit`, `mgm`, `pcor`, `huge`, `TMFG`, `graphicalVAR` (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/bootnet/bootnet.pdf). There is no consensus on the best estimator (Isvoranu and Epskamp, https://dare.uva.nl/id/be1bbf96-06ac-4e31-ad85-c0d06407af2f).
4. **Set gamma.** "Typically set between 0 and 0.5"; one example gave 105 edges at 0, 95 at 0.25 and 87 at 0.5.
5. **Estimate and draw.** `plot(Network, layout = "spring")`. qgraph's spring layout is Fruchterman-Reingold, 500 iterations (https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/qgraph/html/qgraph.html). Width and saturation scale with absolute weight relative to `maximum`; `minimum` hides weak edges without moving the layout; `cut` splits the scaling. The manual calls the "colorblind" theme (blue positive, red negative) "the de facto standard", while "classic" green and red is still the default (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/qgraph/qgraph.pdf). Burger et al. drew negative edges dashed for print. Nodes get short labels with a legend of full item text (`nodeNames`) and colors by scale (`groups`).
6. **Describe.** Report density and mean absolute weight, because "one cannot get a clear notion of the average edge weight from visualization alone".
7. **Centrality.** `centralityPlot(Network, include = c("Strength", "ExpectedInfluence"))`. The standards want raw scores, noting older qgraph plotted z-scores that "may inflate dissimilarity". Bridge strength and bridge expected influence need a community per node (https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/networktools/html/bridge.html).
8. **Run Workflow 2 before any centrality claim.**
9. **Hand off.** Network figure, centrality plot, edge-interval plot, stability and difference plots; R and package versions with the analysis date; code and data on OSF.

### Workflow 2: Edge accuracy, centrality stability and difference tests

1. **Edge bootstrap.** `bootnet(Network, nBoots = 2500, nCores = 8)` (default 1,000). A user with N = 2,000 and 60 to 70 nodes under ggmModSelect saw it stall after "10+ hours" (bootnet issue 139).
2. **Edge interval plot.** `plot(boot1, labels = FALSE, order = "sample")`: one row per edge ordered by estimate, with sample value, bootstrap mean and a gray 95% band. With 136 possible edges this is a tall strip chart. The standards say these intervals "should never be used to assess for 'significance' of edges" ("double thresholding").
3. **Case-dropping bootstrap.** `bootnet(Network, type = "case")` drops 5% to 75% of cases at 10 levels. `plot(boot2)` shows correlation with the full-sample ranking against share of cases kept. `corStability` gives the CS-coefficient: "should not be below 0.25, and preferably above 0.5". In the PTSD example (N = 359, 17 symptoms) strength scored 0.44, betweenness and closeness 0.05.
4. **Difference tests.** `differenceTest(boot1, 3, 17, "strength")`; `plot(boot1, "edge", plot = "difference")` draws a triangular matrix, black for significant differences (alpha 0.05, uncorrected), gray otherwise.
5. **Report** CS-coefficients, put plots in the supplement, soften wording. Bootstraps take minutes to hours.

Only the network itself is a node-link drawing; the other three standard figures are charts keyed to node and edge identifiers.

### Workflow 3: Comparing networks across groups or time points

Sources: NCT manual (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/NetworkComparisonTest/refman/NetworkComparisonTest.html); van Borkulo et al. 2023 (https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/225456052/00060744-202312000-00004.pdf); averageLayout (https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/qgraph/html/averageLayout.html); Jones, Mair and McNally 2018 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6156459/).

1. **Estimate one network per group** with the same estimator, gamma and items. Burger's example: widowed (290) and separated (434) networks, 12 CES-D items plus a life-event node.
2. **Test.** `NCT(net1, net2, it = 1000, test.edges = TRUE, test.centrality = TRUE, p.adjust.methods = "holm")`; defaults `it = 100`, `paired = FALSE`. It tests network structure (maximum edge difference), global strength, and optionally each edge and centrality. Users cut iterations to 10 to finish big networks (bootnet issue 113). Output plots are permutation histograms, not graphs.
3. **Correlate weight matrices** as a similarity measure; a four-site PTSD study reported 0.62 to 0.74 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5974702).
4. **Draw comparably.** `L <- averageLayout(net1, net2)`, then `layout = L` and the same `maximum` in both plots. The standards list both rules, and warn that "equal layouts might suggest that network structures are more similar than they actually are".
5. **Interpretable distances.** MDS on absolute weights (`smacof::sim2diss`, `mds`), checking stress-1 (0.189 in the adult example), then `Procrustes` to align two groups (congruence 0.930). "The positioning of nodes in a force-directed algorithm cannot be interpreted", while MDS distances can.
6. **Figure.** Side-by-side panels with identical maxima; the caption gives each network's minimum and maximum edge.

### Workflow 4: The same analysis in JASP (no code)

Sources: JASP help (link in Persona B) and the module's R source.

1. Open CSV or `.sav`; Network > Network Analysis; drag items into "Variables" and optionally a group into "Split by".
2. Pick estimator, correlation method, gamma, missing-value handling, and (for correlation networks) a threshold such as Bonferroni or Holm.
3. Outputs: summary table, network plot (blue and red by default), centrality table and plot (betweenness, closeness, strength, expected influence; z-scores when "Normalized" is ticked), clustering plot, weights matrix.
4. "Bootstrap Network" (nonparametric, case, node, parametric, person, jackknife) adds edge and centrality stability plots.
5. Layout: "spring", "circle" or "data" (x and y coordinate columns). With Split by, one averaged layout is computed for all groups; the computed layout can be saved back into the data as columns. Node colors by group, node shape by mgm variable type.
6. Export PNG or PDF; copy citations.

**Time.** About 30 minutes. **Missing.** NCT, a cross-group difference matrix, temporal networks. A Bayesian network GUI exists but its project report calls it "basic" (via search snippet, https://cordis.europa.eu/project/id/101040876/reporting).

### Workflow 5: Intensive longitudinal (ESM) networks

Sources: Epskamp et al. 2018 (https://arxiv.org/pdf/1609.04156v2); mlVAR manual (https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/mlVAR/html/mlVAR.html); graphicalVAR vignette (https://ftp.fau.de/cran/web/packages/idiographic/vignettes/graphical-var.html); scoping review.

1. **Collect (weeks).** Fixed (33%) or pseudo-random (44%) schedules, mostly on phones; median 61 possible and 47.5 completed time points per person.
2. **Preprocess.** Long format with id, day, beep. Exclude low compliance (23% used a 30% cutoff). Check stationarity and detrend. Only 12% reported missing-data handling.
3. **Group model.** `mlVAR(data, vars, idvar, dayvar, beepvar, lags = 1)`. Three networks: temporal (directed lag-1, with self-loops), contemporaneous (undirected) and between-subjects (undirected); random-effect SD networks show individual differences. All reviewed articles reported a temporal network; 51% added contemporaneous and 36% between-subjects.
4. **Draw.** `plot(fit, "temporal", layout = "circle", nonsig = "hide")` and likewise for the others; panels share node positions, the temporal panel has its own maximum, reciprocal pairs are curved.
5. **Single patient.** `graphicalVAR(data, vars, beepvar, dayvar, gamma = 0.5)` returns a temporal (PDC) and contemporaneous (PCC) network; `plot(fit, mixed = TRUE)` overlays them.
6. **Panel data.** `psychonetrics::panelgvar` with `CIplot()` (https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s11336-020-09697-3).
7. **Centrality.** In- and out-strength or expected influence. None of the 43 articles was a replication.

### Workflow 6: Confirmatory models, Bayesian edge evidence and predictability

1. **Confirmatory GGM.** `ggm(data) %>% runmodel %>% prune(alpha = 0.01, adjust = "fdr") %>% modelsearch`, then fit the structure to a holdout sample and report SEM-style fit; multi-group models constrain edges equal (https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/psychonetrics/html/prune.html).
2. **Bayesian edge evidence (easybgm or JASP).** The edge evidence plot colors every possible edge by its inclusion Bayes factor: evidence for inclusion, for exclusion, or inconclusive. Colors and the default threshold differ between the README and the function reference (https://cran.rstudio.com/web/packages/easybgm/readme/README.html; https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/easybgm/html/edgeevidence.html). This separates "no edge" from "not enough data" (Huth et al. 2023, https://pure.amsterdamumc.nl/ws/files/136904501/Bayesian-analysis-of-cross-sectional-networks.pdf).
3. **Predictability.** `mgm` then `predict` gives R^2 or normalized accuracy per node; qgraph `pie` draws it as a ring (https://arxiv.org/pdf/1610.09108).

### Workflow 7: Per-patient network feedback for therapy (TheraNet and platforms)

Source: Hall et al. 2024 (Persona E), unless noted.

1. **Collect EMA (two weeks, before therapy).** During the probationary period, the patient answers four times a day for 14 days, each item prefaced "Since the last questionnaire...". Most items are 0-100 sliders: sleep, behaviors to work on, pleasant activities, intrusive thoughts, positive and negative affect, tension, emotion regulation. A yes/no item asks about difficult experiences, with open text. The patient adds up to three personalized items, always phrased negatively. Platforms used for this kind of collection include m-Path, RoQua, PsyMate and PETRA (Bartels et al. 2023).
2. **Estimate (researcher or platform).** graphicalVAR estimates a regularized partial correlation network. An "influence" value, similar to strength but accounting for item valence, is computed per node.
3. **Produce the sheet.** A printout with three parts: (a) the network, with triangles for resource items and circles for problem items, green positive and red negative edges, thickness for strength, and a legend mapping P1-P3 to the personal items; the influence values sit in a table beside it; (b) a sleep chart of daily ratings by date with a dashed mean line; (c) the patient's difficult experiences, verbatim, by day.
4. **Train the therapist (once).** One one-to-one session using the therapist's first TheraNet patient; no refreshers, even for later patients.
5. **Use in therapy.** The therapist reads it alone for case formulation, uses it to choose or justify interventions, or discusses it with the patient in session. Some skip it under time pressure or because explaining it seems too complex.
6. **What goes wrong.** The side table forces look-ups; red and green confuse; the full network overwhelms patients; understanding fades after training. Therapists ask for one or two explanatory sentences under the network. Researchers interviewed by Bartels et al. warn that patients misread "especially network graphs" and recommend verbal explanation and avoiding causal claims.

**Platform variant.** m-Path's dashboard lets therapists combine graphs on a "moodboard" and export PDFs, with network visualisations among the offered graph types (https://m-path.io/visual-feedback/). ESMvis, a Shiny app for one person's ESM data, deliberately shows raw data (line and circle figures, weekly "movie" views) instead of VAR networks, which its authors consider hard for patients and therapists to interpret (Bringmann et al. 2021, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8528801/).

### Workflow 8: Exploratory Graph Analysis in scale development

Sources: EGA and bootEGA reference pages (https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/EGAnet/html/EGA.html; https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/EGAnet/html/bootEGA.html); Christensen and Golino 2021 (https://www.mdpi.com/2624-8611/3/3/32); a worked course chapter (https://rafavsbastos.quarto.pub/psychometrics/07-EGA.html).

1. **Item matrix.** Respondents by items, for example the 25 `bfi` items.
2. **Redundancy first.** `UVA(data, key = item_text)` flags locally dependent items by weighted topological overlap; "values greater than 0.25" need action. For a pair it keeps one item and drops the other (the chapter removed "Get angry easily." at wTO 0.431). Continue with `reduced_data`.
3. **Estimate dimensions.** `EGA(data)`. The argument text gives defaults `corr = "auto"`, `model = "glasso"`, `algorithm = "walktrap"`, `uni.method = "louvain"`, though the usage line lists other options first, so defaults should be checked per version. It plots by default, with nodes colored by detected dimension; the summary reports the number of communities and TEFI, a fit index for comparing structures (-24.989 for five dimensions in the chapter).
4. **Bootstrap.** `bootEGA(data, iter = 500, type = "parametric")` re-estimates on 500 replicate samples and by default plots item stability. It reports how often each number of dimensions appeared (5 in 94.2% of samples in the chapter) and, with `typicalStructure = TRUE`, the median ("typical") network and its community assignment.
5. **Stability.** `itemStability` gives, per item, the proportion of replicates in which it lands in its empirical dimension (a 0-1 value per node, plotted as a dot chart); `dimensionStability` gives structural consistency per dimension. The bootEGA paper's guideline is item stability of at least 0.70 (via search snippet of the abstract); applied papers often use 0.75.
6. **Revise and re-run.** Remove or reassign unstable or redundant items and repeat steps 3 to 5 until the structure is stable. No source found prescribes this loop step by step; it is how applied papers report using the outputs.
7. **Report.** The network colored by dimension, the item stability plot, dimension frequencies, and TEFI comparisons. Variants: `hierEGA` (hierarchical), `riEGA` (random intercept), dynamic EGA for time series.

### Workflow 9: Bayesian network (DAG) from cross-sectional symptoms (bnlearn)

Sources: Briganti, Scutari and McNally, "A Tutorial on Bayesian Networks for Psychopathology Researchers", Psychological Methods 2022, doi 10.1037/met0000479 (https://dipot.ulb.ac.be/dspace/bitstream/2013/340429/3/tutorial.pdf); McNally, Heeren and Robinaugh 2017 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5632780/).

1. **Learn a structure.** Constraint-based `pc.stable(data)`, score-based hill-climbing `hc(data)`, or a hybrid. The tutorial's example is a depressive-symptom dataset.
2. **Bootstrap.** `boot.strength(data, R = 200, algorithm = "pc.stable")` returns, for each possible arc, a strength (share of replicates in which the arc appears) and a direction (share in which it points this way).
3. **Filter and average.** Keep arcs with `strength > 0.85` and `direction > 0.5`; `averaged.network(BST, threshold = 0.85)`.
4. **Weight.** `arc.strength(avgnet, data, "bic-g")` gives each arc's BIC change if removed.
5. **Draw.** `qgraph(avgnet, layout = "circle")`; edge thickness and color saturation denote the weight.
6. **Applied variant.** McNally et al. used hill-climbing with BIC on 17 PTSD symptoms (165 of 179 adults with complete data), 10,000 bootstraps, and showed a direction when at least 51% of networks agreed. One figure used edge thickness for direction probability; a companion figure used thickness for BIC importance. The DAG suggested physiological reactivity to reminders as a possible driver.

Each edge therefore carries two uncertainty values, and a direction near 0.5 means the arrowhead is barely better than a coin toss.

### Workflow 10: Response Item Network (ResIN) for belief systems and polarization

Sources: ResIN vignette (https://www.stats.bris.ac.uk/R/web/packages/ResIN/vignettes/ResIN-VIGNETTE.html); Chen et al. 2025, Network Science 13:e20 (https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/7E8EA3929B3ABF3F10E7E989FACDBADE/S2050124225100167a.pdf/a-broken-egg-of-us-political-beliefs-using-response-item-networks-resin-to-measure-ideological-polarization.pdf); package page (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/ResIN/index.html).

1. **Recode responses.** Each item's options become symbols ("Strongly Agree" to `++`, ..., "Strongly Disagree" to `--`). The vignette uses 8 attitude items, giving 40 nodes.
2. **Build nodes and edges.** Each response option becomes a binary variable and a node ("guar_jobs:1" to "guar_jobs:7"). A positive phi correlation between options of two different issues gives a weighted edge; only co-endorsed options attract.
3. **Lay out.** Fruchterman-Reingold force-directed layout, then a rotation that aligns the main dimension of node coordinates with the x axis. `left_anchor = "legal_abort_++"` flips the axis so a chosen node sits left; `seed` fixes the run.
4. **Read positions as measurements.** `ResIN_nodeframe$x` is each option's position on the latent axis; the vignette correlates it with mean partisanship of endorsers (0.87). Each respondent gets the mean position of the options they endorsed (`ResIN_scores$raw_x` major axis, `raw_y` minor axis).
5. **Clusters and statistics.** `detect_clusters = TRUE` (leading eigenvector by default); node strength, closeness, betweenness and expected influence; `node_covars` attaches mean covariates per node.
6. **Compare over time or groups.** Chen et al. built one ResIN per ANES wave from 2000 to 2020 on five issues and measured linearization (x spread over y spread) and link density, with 200 resamples of 80% of respondents for uncertainty. `ResIN_boots_prepare`, `ResIN_boots_execute` and `ResIN_boots_extract` do this in the package.
7. **Export.** `as.igraph`, `as.qgraph`, `as.gephi` (CSV for Gephi).

## 7. Analysis methods and how they are interpreted

- **Estimators.** EBICglasso (default), ggmModSelect, IsingFit, mgm, huge, TMFG, Bayesian (BGGM, BDgraph, easybgm), confirmatory psychonetrics; ESM: mlVAR, graphicalVAR, GIMME; causal: bnlearn's pc.stable, hill-climbing and hybrids; dimensionality: EGA.
- **Node metrics they trust.** Strength, expected influence and bridge variants. Bringmann et al. call betweenness and closeness "especially unsuitable". Spiller et al. found endorsement frequency predicted change better than most centrality indices, expected influence excepted (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7549218/). Dablander and Hinne found centrality correlates weakly with causal influence (https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s41598-019-43033-9).
- **Global metrics.** Density, mean absolute weight, global strength, weight-matrix correlation; ResIN linearization and link density.
- **Mesoscale.** Walktrap and Louvain inside EGA, with bootstrapped item stability; clique percolation for overlapping communities.
- **Validation habits.** Edge bootstraps, CS-coefficients, difference tests, NCT permutations, split-sample cross-validation (5% of temporal articles), multisite replication, simulation-based power, rare preregistration (1 of 43).
- **Interpretation norms.** Edges are conditional associations and possible, unproven causal paths. An interval that includes zero is not evidence an edge is absent. Centrality is a hypothesis generator, not a treatment target. In qgraph-style GGM work, node placement in a spring layout means nothing. The exceptions are deliberate: MDS layouts (distances interpretable) and ResIN (the rotated x axis is the measured latent dimension).
- **Critics.** Forbes et al. (2017) found only 13-21% of edges consistently estimated across six networks (https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/evidence-that-psychopathology-symptom-networks-have-limited-repli/); Borsboom et al. replied that the networks were "highly similar" (https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/false-alarm-a-comprehensive-reanalysis-of-evidence-that-psychopat/). Hallquist, Wright and Molenaar showed strength can be redundant with a factor loading (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7012663). The implication for a tool: draw these graphs as estimates, show uncertainty beside the edge, flag rankings that failed the stability check, and never imply that position means something unless the layout makes it so.

## 8. Outputs and collaboration

- **Researcher deliverables.** Journal figures (network, centrality, stability, intervals, difference matrix, NCT histograms, EGA item stability), supplementary tables (weights matrix, raw centralities, CS-coefficients), the reporting-standards checklist, and OSF code and data.
- **Clinical deliverables.** A one-patient sheet combining the network, a time series and dated events (TheraNet printout), or an interactive dashboard with PDF export (m-Path). The reader is a therapist with one training session, sometimes with the patient beside them.
- **Reproducibility.** Package versions, analysis date, bootstrap seeds and layout settings ("whether a minimum, maximum, or cut value were used") are reported. Spring layouts drift across versions, so authors save the layout matrix; JASP can save it into the data.
- **Sharing.** Researchers share PDF or PNG inside manuscripts. Clinical feedback is shared as paper or as a platform's web dashboard; TheraNet's authors recommend "digitalized options that integrate into existing software".
