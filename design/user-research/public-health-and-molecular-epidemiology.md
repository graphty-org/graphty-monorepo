# Public Health Outbreak Investigators and Molecular Epidemiologists

## 1. Summary

Public health epidemiologists, public health laboratory scientists, hospital infection preventionists and veterinary epidemiologists all draw graphs of who (or which farm, ward or isolate) is connected to whom, in order to stop an outbreak. Four kinds of graph recur:

- A **contact network** from case interviews or contact tracing: who named whom as a sex, needle-sharing, household or workplace contact, and which people shared a place or event.
- A **genetic distance network**: two people are linked when the genetic distance between their pathogens falls under a threshold (HIV-1 _pol_: usually 0.5% or 1.5% TN93 distance; tuberculosis: about 5 to 12 SNPs; enteric bacteria: a count of core-genome allele differences). Connected components are read as "clusters".
- A **phylogenetic tree**, often time-scaled, read alongside a map and a timeline (Nextstrain, Microreact, Taxonium), or a **minimum spanning tree** of bacterial allele profiles (GrapeTree, PHYLOViZ, Ridom SeqSphere+).
- A **contact network derived from time intervals**: patients linked because they were on the same ward on the same day, farms linked by dated animal movements, hospitals linked by shared patients.
- A **provider network projected from insurance claims**: physicians or facilities linked by the patients they both treated, used by health services researchers to study how care is actually organized (section 5.15). The same construction serves outbreak work (which facilities to alert) and fraud work, but its own field builds it from millions of claims and reads the communities as care teams.

They use these graphs to find fast-growing transmission, to decide which clusters get a response, and to point testing, partner services, environmental sampling or movement controls at the right places. Several of their tools are free browser tools built by public health agencies (CDC's MicrobeTrace and Secure HIV-TRACE, WHO's Go.Data, Nextstrain's Auspice). The work runs under strict confidentiality rules, an explicit CDC instruction to avoid molecular network diagrams when communicating with partners, and a published ethics debate in which networks of people living with HIV demanded a moratorium on molecular HIV surveillance (section 2, "Ethics and status").

## 2. Population

**Job titles seen in sources.** HIV surveillance epidemiologist; molecular HIV epidemiologist; cluster detection and response program specialist; TB genotyping or cluster epidemiologist; genomic epidemiologist and public health laboratory bioinformatician; PulseNet laboratory scientist and foodborne outbreak epidemiologist; hospital infection preventionist and healthcare epidemiologist; CDC Epidemic Intelligence Service officer; disease intervention specialist and supervisor; contact tracing supervisor in an emergency response; veterinary epidemiologist at a national veterinary institute.

**Organizations.** CDC (HIV, TB, viral hepatitis and foodborne divisions, Office of Advanced Molecular Detection); state and big-city health departments and their public health laboratories; PulseNet, "over 80 state, local, and federal public health and regulatory laboratories" ([Leeper et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12195096/)); hospital infection prevention teams; academic partners embedded with health departments (the Rhode Island HIV pipeline, [news-medical](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20230315/An-automated-bioinformatics-pipeline-to-monitor-HIV-data-in-real-time.aspx)); and international equivalents ("BC Centre for Excellence in HIV/AIDS", [PMC4853759](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4853759); WHO Go.Data users in more than 60 countries, [WHO](https://www.who.int/tools/godata/about); Uganda's Ministry of Health, [Akunzirwe et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12011214/); Sweden's national veterinary institute, [Noremark and Widgren 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3974595/)).

**Rough size (evidenced).** Secure HIV-TRACE, released August 2017, had 148 users from 54 health departments and 792,360 uploaded sequences by April 2024, across 59 CDC-funded HIV jurisdictions ([EID 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12078553/)). MicrobeTrace had 4,119 unique users from March 2018 to November 2020 (22% CDC, 78% outside), with visits averaging 8.7 minutes ([PLOS Comp Bio 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8491948/)), and more than 2,500 in the first nine months of 2021, driven by COVID-19 contact tracing ([CDC AMD](https://cdc.gov/advanced-molecular-detection/php/success-stories/microbetrace-connections.html)). PulseNet has more than 80 member laboratories. No source found gives the number of hospital infection preventionists, genomic epidemiologists or disease intervention specialists.

**How often they touch graph tools.** HIV: health departments at least monthly, CDC quarterly ([CDC guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/hiv/programresources/guidance/hiv-cluster-detection-and-response-guidance/cluster-detection.html)). Enteric bacteria: continuously, over a rolling 60-day window ([Leeper et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12195096/)). Outbreaks of TB, COVID-19, hepatitis, sexually transmitted infections, Ebola-family viruses, hospital organisms or livestock disease: episodic but intense, daily during a response ([PMC11304816](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11304816); [Akunzirwe et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12011214/)).

**Skill profile.** Graph literacy is practical: thresholds, clusters as connected components, degree, "who links whom", and reading trees; centrality belongs to research and to some TB, sexually transmitted infection and veterinary work, not to routine HIV cluster response. SAS is the HIV surveillance staple because CDC ships a SAS program for eHARS exports; R appears in TB tools (LITT and LATTE ship R code plus a Shiny interface for "users without R programming expertise", [LITT](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2021.667337/full)), in outbreak packages (epicontacts, outbreaker2, TransPhylo) and in veterinary tracing (EpiContactTrace). Command-line bioinformatics is concentrated in public health laboratories (Augur, chewBBACA, UShER); CDC built Secure HIV-TRACE because local installation "was technically complex and not feasible for most" health departments ([EID 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12078553/)).

**Team context.** HIV clusters are reviewed by a leadership and coordination group that must include "HIV prevention, surveillance, and partner services leadership and staff", with care, sexually transmitted infection, hepatitis, harm reduction, housing and informatics staff as appropriate ([CDC prioritization guidance](https://www.Cdc.gov/hiv/programresources/guidance/hiv-cluster-detection-and-response-guidance/cluster-review-and-prioritization.html)). In foodborne work the laboratory detects the cluster and hands it to epidemiologists for interviews ([Inter-EURL guidance 2024](https://zenodo.org/records/14536255)). In hospitals, infection prevention, microbiology and a genomics partner work together ([Wan et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11272044/)).

**Ethics.** Molldrem and Smith argue that molecular HIV surveillance re-uses sequences from clinical drug-resistance tests without consent, and question its evidence base, its risk-benefit ratio and the claim that direction of transmission can be inferred, proposing "HIV data justice" ([American Journal of Bioethics 20(10):10-23, 2020](https://www.hivjustice.net/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/Reassessing-the-Ethics-of-Molecular-HIV-Surveillance-in-the-Era-of-Cluster-Detection-and-Response-Toward-HIV-Data-Justice.pdf)). A 2022 draft resolution of the Presidential Advisory Council on HIV/AIDS records that "networks of people living with HIV, many other people living with HIV, and HIV advocates have demanded an immediate moratorium on all molecular HIV surveillance activities until adequate safeguards protecting the privacy and autonomy of people living with HIV are implemented", says acceptable local practice ranges up to a jurisdiction-wide moratorium, and asks for firewalls keeping surveillance data away from prosecutors ([PACHA draft resolution, October 2022](https://files.hiv.gov/s3fs-public/2022-10/DRAFT-PACHA-Resolution-on-Molecular-HIV-Surveillance-Cluster-Detection-and-Response-Resolution.pdf)). CDC states that molecular analysis "cannot determine if a specific person transmitted HIV to another person" ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/hivcluster)).

**Program status (dated note, November 2026).** In April 2025 a near 20% cut to CDC staff effectively eliminated five branches of the HIV prevention division ([KFF, April 8, 2025](https://www.kff.org/policy-watch/what-do-federal-staffing-cuts-and-hhs-restructuring-mean-for-the-nations-hiv-response/)); some laid-off HIV staff were reinstated in June 2025, and some states had laid off or reassigned HIV surveillance staff ([KFF Health News, June 13, 2025](https://kffhealthnews.org/public-health/hiv-research-cdc-staffing-upheaval-wastes-money/)). No source found confirms the current operating status of Secure HIV-TRACE or of cluster detection funding. Workflows 5.1 and 5.5 describe the program as documented through 2024, and the requirements in section 10 assume no CDC-hosted service will necessarily keep running.

## 3. Personas

### 3.1 Dana Okafor -- HIV molecular cluster epidemiologist, state health department (id: hiv-molecular-cluster-epidemiologist)

One of two people running HIV cluster detection in a mid-size state; MPH, strong SAS, some R, no command-line bioinformatics. She exports persons and sequences from eHARS, runs CDC's de-identifying SAS program, uploads to Secure HIV-TRACE monthly, keeps line lists in Excel, opens MicrobeTrace when contact data must be drawn on a cluster, and files quarterly cluster reports through REDCap. Her data is 1,000 to 5,000 HIV-1 _pol_ sequences (CDC uses a 1,497-nt protease plus reverse transcriptase segment) with diagnosis dates, demographics, transmission category, viral suppression and county; only 52% of diagnoses had a sequence in 2021-2023, so every cluster is an undercount. She needs to catch priority clusters before CDC's quarterly report, write a narrative and line list for the monthly review, and track growth.

Frustrations: 80% of CDC-detected clusters span several jurisdictions and local analysis misses about 20% of national clusters ([EID 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12078553/)); going from "node has cluster id" to "cluster has these members" took Excel work until MicrobeTrace added export by cluster ([issue 360](https://github.com/CDCgov/MicrobeTrace/issues/360)); line lists must be access-limited because "even line lists that don't include personally identifiable information can include sensitive information", and network diagrams are not to be used with partners ([CDC prioritization guidance](https://www.Cdc.gov/hiv/programresources/guidance/hiv-cluster-detection-and-response-guidance/cluster-review-and-prioritization.html)).

### 3.2 Marcus Bell -- outbreak response epidemiologist, CDC field team or state outbreak unit (id: outbreak-response-epidemiologist)

A former EIS officer deployed to HIV among people who inject drugs, COVID-19 in congregate settings and hepatitis; competent in R, comfortable with trees. He overlays genetic links on named contacts and person-place exposures in MicrobeTrace, imports Nextstrain trees as Newick, and sometimes works from a temporary Access database during a surge. Scale is tens to hundreds of cases plus contacts: Scott County, Indiana had 411 people and 1,060 risk links ([PMC5853229](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5853229)); an Arkansas university outbreak linked 565 of 965 cases to 56 residences and 54 gatherings ([MMWR](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/70/wr/mm7001a5.htm)). He needs to show the true size of an outbreak (in Massachusetts MicrobeTrace showed "55 percent more people were affected than the health department had realized", [CDC AMD](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/amd/whats-new/hiv-amd-mass.html)), organize field work by cluster, and find bridge people and venues.

Frustrations: 3,700 SARS-CoV-2 genomes (about 7 million links) crashed Chrome after 15 to 30 minutes ([issue 432](https://github.com/CDCgov/MicrobeTrace/issues/432)); a 12,070-row edge list took 10 minutes to load ([issue 349](https://github.com/CDCgov/MicrobeTrace/issues/349)); several link types on one pair collapse to one color ([issue 436](https://github.com/CDCgov/MicrobeTrace/issues/436)); styling is lost after reopening a session ([issue 693](https://github.com/CDCgov/MicrobeTrace/issues/693)).

### 3.3 Linh Tran -- TB genomic cluster epidemiologist, state TB program (id: tb-genomic-cluster-epidemiologist)

Runs genotype and WGS cluster review; clinical background, moderate R. She works from TB GIMS genotype cluster alerts scored by log-likelihood ratio, ranks likely sources with LITT, compares shared-location stays with LATTE, and draws pictures in MicrobeTrace. Inputs are a pairwise SNP matrix, epi links graded definite, probable or possible, infectious-period dates, smear and cavitation status, and shelter or venue rosters, for clusters of tens of cases (LITT was validated on 534 cases in 56 clusters). She must decide which genotype-matched cases are recent transmission, name a probable source, and prioritize contacts and locations.

Frustrations: a Los Angeles County analysis concluded that pairwise SNP distance alone cannot establish recent transmission ([Nelson et al. 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2021.790544/full)); LITT discrepancies "often reveal data entry errors", so she iterates on inputs; LITT outputs Excel ranking tables, so the network picture needs another tool ([LITT](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2021.667337/full)).

### 3.4 Rosa Delgado -- partner services supervisor and cluster response lead (id: dis-supervisor)

Supervises disease intervention specialists, attends the monthly cluster review and turns clusters into field assignments; not a data analyst. She works from partner services records and the epidemiologist's line lists, and needs to know who in a cluster has not been interviewed, which partners are untested and who is not virally suppressed.

Frustrations: in West Virginia only 39 of 192 reported contacts (20%) were interviewed ([PMC11304816](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11304816)); across seven jurisdictions interviewed early syphilis patients reported 84,224 partners, 75% of them unnamed, and an estimated 78% to 85% of partners were never reached ([Cope et al. 2020](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/119646)).

### 3.5 Priya Natarajan -- genomic epidemiologist, state public health laboratory (id: phl-genomic-epidemiologist)

A bioinformatician running SARS-CoV-2, mpox and influenza genomic surveillance and supporting outbreak investigations, comfortable on the command line. She runs Nextstrain builds viewed in Auspice, places local genomes with UShER and explores them in Taxonium, shares projects with epidemiologists through Microreact, and builds SNP-threshold networks in MicrobeTrace; CDC's genomic epidemiology toolkit covers exactly that stack. Her inputs are local FASTA plus a metadata TSV (hundreds to thousands of genomes) read against public trees with millions of tips (about 5.6 million sequences in the UShER tree behind Cov2Tree, [Sanderson 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9704803)). She must place local genomes in national context, say whether a suspected cluster is one introduction or several, and flag new lineages.

Frustrations: network tools need trees exported in divergence rather than time units and thresholds re-tuned, and CDC's own training warns that some web tree builders "do not clean Ns from sequences and may yield misleading results" ([Toolkit 3.2](https://www.cdc.gov/advanced-molecular-detection/media/pdfs/ToolkitModule_3.2-508C.pdf)); Taxonium's in-browser mode topped out around 6 million tips on some systems.

### 3.6 Tomas Herrera -- PulseNet laboratory scientist and foodborne cluster coordinator (id: pulsenet-foodborne-cluster-scientist)

Sequences enteric isolates (Salmonella, STEC, Listeria, Campylobacter), reviews clusters and hands them to the state foodborne epidemiologist. He works in PulseNet 2.0, which replaced a customized BioNumerics 7.6 in September 2024 and calls alleles with BLASTn against reference alleles ([Leeper et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12195096/)); elsewhere the equivalents are Ridom SeqSphere+ or chewBBACA, with GrapeTree or PHYLOViZ for minimum spanning trees and NCBI Pathogen Detection for national context. His data is allele profile tables (STEC cgMLST: 2,513 loci; chromosomal wgMLST: 30,717), isolate metadata (source, state, collection date) and hqSNP matrices for confirmation.

Frustrations: chaining -- a New York Salmonella Enteritidis cluster defined at 5 alleles grew to 14 alleles of internal diversity because each new isolate was within 5 of one member but not of others ([PMC10282972](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10282972/)); NCBI clusters cannot be customized and NCBI "does not identify outbreaks or outbreak membership" ([FAQ](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pathogens/faq/)).

### 3.7 Grace Mensah -- hospital infection preventionist and healthcare epidemiologist (id: hospital-infection-preventionist)

Investigates clusters of carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales, C. difficile, Candida auris and VRE across wards, and with the health department across facilities. She works from EHR exports of patient admission, discharge and transfer histories, microbiology results, and a genomics partner's trees and plasmid analyses, drawn in Cytoscape. Her data is one row per stay per ward with start and end times, plus isolate and plasmid typing and environmental sink samples; a Manchester study sampled 349 wastewater sites and 1,247 patients across six wards ([JAC-AMR 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11369815/)). She must find which patients overlapped where and when, decide which wards and sites to sample, and alert linked facilities.

Frustration: single-species outbreak definitions miss plasmid spread. In a London IMP-CPE outbreak only 5 of 116 cases met the conventional definition, and 57 of 96 patient contacts involved two different species ([Wan et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11272044/)).

### 3.8 Joseph Okello -- contact tracing supervisor in an emergency response (id: emergency-contact-tracing-supervisor)

Leads a contact tracing pillar in an Ebola-family or COVID-19 response, nationally or with WHO support. He works in Go.Data (cases, contacts, events, daily follow-up lists, chains of transmission) and Chainchecker, from a case line list with onset dates and a likely source field, contact lists, exposure events and a daily follow-up status per contact. He needs every contact seen every day of the follow-up window, every new case attached to a known chain, and possible hospital-acquired transmission flagged.

Frustrations: before Chainchecker, Uganda tracked chains on paper and in PowerPoint; with it the team linked 11 previously unconnected cases, corrected 13 wrong links and flagged 5 possible nosocomial events ([Akunzirwe et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12011214/)). Go.Data's cluster feature shows only as text on edges, so users model clusters as event nodes ([reference data guide](https://worldhealthorganization.github.io/godata/refdata)).

### 3.9 Anna Lindqvist -- veterinary epidemiologist, national veterinary institute (id: veterinary-movement-epidemiologist)

Traces livestock movements during outbreaks (foot-and-mouth disease, African swine fever, bovine tuberculosis) and designs risk-based surveillance, working from national movement databases in R with EpiContactTrace. Her data is one row per movement -- source holding, destination holding, date, optionally species and number of animals; the package's example dataset holds 70,190 movements over three months and a 2010 Swedish exercise used real cattle, pig, sheep and goat movements over 90 days ([Noremark and Widgren 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3974595/)). Within hours of a positive farm she must list every holding that could have infected it and every holding it could have infected, respecting the order of movements, and rank holdings for sampling.

Frustration: plain graph paths overstate risk because they ignore time order, which is why the package counts contact chains that respect movement order.

Outbreak tracing is only one part of her field. A 2025 scoping review found 203 livestock and poultry movement network studies in 52 countries, published from 2006 to 2023 (cattle 40.3%, swine 33.2%, poultry 13.0%), and grouped them into five themes: network characteristics and structural analysis (144 studies, 73.4%), pathogen spread and epidemic modeling (35.0%), targeted control and surveillance (31.5%), retrospective analysis of outbreaks (14.8%) and network inference (3 studies; the paper prints 14.8% for this theme too, which cannot be right for 3 of 203) ([Sequeira et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12279109/)). So between outbreaks she describes the trade network as a whole and per month or year, ranks holdings and markets for targeted sampling or control, and, where movement records are partial, works with simulated contact networks (section 5.14). National networks are large: the German pig trade network held 97,980 premises and 6,359,697 trade transactions over 2011-2014 ([Lentz et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4859575/)), and the British cattle network roughly 64,600 to 85,400 holdings a year from about 158 million animal movements over 2001-2015 ([Fielding et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6408381/)). Her tools beyond EpiContactTrace are R with igraph (Fielding et al.), Python NetworkX with SAS (Buttner et al. 2013, [PMC3771899](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3771899/)), and the review names R as dominant since 2013, with Pajek, UCINET and NetDraw in earlier studies.

Further frustrations: no international movement database exists and only 8 national databases were electronically available; 89.2% of the studies come from high- or upper-middle-income countries, and in low- and middle-income countries surveys and snowball sampling stand in for a database, which "limits the robustness of network models" ([Sequeira et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12279109/)); and routine identification and traceability systems may not exist or may not be shared, "especially in low- and middle-income countries" ([Leung, Rudge and Fournie 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10189310/)).

Why this persona stays deferred: no source gives a headcount of people doing this work. The evidence is a specialist research and government population -- about a dozen published studies a year worldwide, concentrated in national veterinary agencies and university groups in Europe and the Americas -- whose analysis already runs in R or Python scripts. That is narrower than the public health populations above, so the persona and its workflows (W74, W3048, W3049, W3050) stay deferred; the requirements they share with in-scope personas (time-respecting paths, static and temporal comparison, ranked removal curves) are carried by those personas.

### 3.10 Eva Doyle -- health services researcher building provider networks from claims (id: patient-sharing-network-researcher)

A faculty member in a health policy or general internal medicine department, or an analyst in a health system or a state all-payer claims program, who studies how the informal structure of care delivery relates to cost, quality and referral patterns. Her graph is not a transmission network: two physicians are tied because the same patient saw both, which is read as a working relationship. The method is established enough to have a canonical paper -- Landon et al. used 2006 Medicare claims covering 4,586,044 beneficiaries and 68,288 physicians in 51 hospital referral regions ([JAMA 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3528342)) -- and a scoping review of 49 studies ([DuGoff et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6086089)).

She works in SAS for the claims extract and in R (igraph) or Python (NetworkX) for the network, and draws force-directed pictures for publication; Landon's group names exactly that stack ([Landon et al., Medical Care 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3723338)). Scale is the defining feature: a single region ranged from 135 physicians in Minot, North Dakota to 8,197 in Boston, with a mean of 1,339 physicians and 50,927 ties per region, and the national public extract of the same data has been described as 940,492 provider nodes and 49,685,810 shared-patient edges ([DocGraph presentation](https://de.slideshare.net/davefauth/graphconnect-nyc)).

Frustrations: the field has no standard method -- the review counted 180 distinct network measures in 13 categories across 49 studies, and noted that every included study reported at least one significant finding; one payer's claims see only part of the structure (Medicare claims missed 14.6% of shared-patient relationships in a North Carolina colorectal cancer comparison against private and Medicaid claims, [Pollack et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6816773)); sampling patients distorts the projection unevenly, with degree and counts recovered from small samples but transitivity, clustering and community counts needing the full cohort ([O'Malley et al., Health Services Research 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7968944)); and every figure and table she publishes is governed by the CMS cell size suppression policy, which forbids displaying any cell of 1 to 10, including percentages and any value a reader could derive from the rest of the output ([ResDAC](https://resdac.org/node/1506)).

## 5. Step-by-step workflows

### 5.1 Monthly HIV molecular cluster detection with Secure HIV-TRACE

Sources: EID 2025 / PMC12078553, CDC cluster detection, CDC prioritization. (URLs in section 11.)

1. **Export.** Export persons and sequences from the local eHARS. Laboratories report sequences electronically from routine drug-resistance genotyping. The median time from sample to health department was 34 days in 2023.
2. **De-identify and format.** Run the CDC-supplied SAS program, which removes personal identifiers. Identifiers never leave the health department.
3. **Upload and run.** Log in through CDC Secure Access Management Services and upload. The service:
    - aligns to HXB2 _pol_;
    - computes pairwise TN93 distances;
    - builds networks at both 0.5% and 1.5%;
    - runs a data quality screen.
4. **Read priority flags.** Since May 2023 the tool automatically flags and tracks national priority clusters. The definition is a 0.5% threshold, among people diagnosed in the past 3 years, with 5 or more diagnoses in the previous 12 months, or 3 or more in jurisdictions with fewer than 200 diagnoses a year. (The paper's wording reads as "more than"; the CDC criterion is commonly given as "at least". Check the current CDC definition.) Users can also define locally monitored clusters.
5. **Review.** Read the visualizations, summary statistics, epi curves and line lists against CDC's quarterly line lists; the national median cluster size at detection was 7 (range 3 to 24).
6. **Assemble the case.** Pull partner services, sexually transmitted infection and care data, then write a cluster case definition (diagnosis year, demographics, area), a narrative and a line list.
7. **Prioritize at the monthly meeting.** The group weighs increase over baseline, alternative explanations (more testing, data changes), potential for ongoing transmission, how well the network is understood (for example low sequence completeness) and potential for poor outcomes; priority moves either way as data arrive.
8. **Report and record.** File a cluster report form through REDCap (initial, quarterly, annual, closeout) and enter cluster ids in eHARS without identifiers.

Hand-off: line list and narrative to DIS and program staff. CDC advises against using molecular network diagrams with partners.

### 5.2 Choosing a distance threshold (HIV-TRACE command line plus AUTO-TUNE)

Sources: HIV-TRACE paper PMC5995201, hivtrace README, hivclustering README, AUTO-TUNE, Frontiers in Bioinformatics 2024, MicrobeTrace threshold guidance. (URLs in section 11.)

This is research and academic-partner work, plus CDC's own installation. Most health departments use Secure HIV-TRACE's fixed 0.5% and 1.5% thresholds instead.

1. **Prepare the FASTA.** Names can carry attributes separated by `|` (for example `ISOLATE|2005|SAN DIEGO|MSM`).
2. **Compute distances.** Run `tn93` with a generous maximum, for example 0.03, which the paper says "may be revised upwards for sufficiently divergent sequences". tn93 handles about 10^5 to 10^6 pairs per second per core. Distance time grows with N squared: 185,849 sequences took about 32 minutes on 64 cores, plus about 20 minutes to align.
3. **Score candidate thresholds.** Run `hivnetworkcsv -A` (auto-profile). It adds edges from shortest to longest and writes `autotune_report.tsv` with network statistics at each distance. AUTO-TUNE scores each candidate (0 to 2) from two things: a jump in R12 (largest cluster size over second-largest) and a Gompertz-penalized cluster count. A threshold is accepted outright when it scores at least 1.9 and is unique or tightly grouped. Otherwise, inspect the score plot at autotune.datamonkey.org. The Seattle example took under 16 seconds.
4. **Judge the result.** Across 21 HIV datasets AUTO-TUNE chose a larger threshold than the published one in 13 cases and smaller in 8, and scores exceeded 1.5 in only 6 of 18 comparisons. The authors advise shorter thresholds for rapid injection-drug-use outbreaks, stratifying by subtype, checking risk-group assortativity across thresholds, and caution with sparse sampling (25% sampling pushed the selected threshold from 0.0170 to 0.0215).
5. **Build the network.** `hivtrace -i in.fasta -a resolve -r HXB2_prrt -t .015 -m 500 -g .05 -c` writes `results.json`. The flags set ambiguity handling (resolve, average, skip, gapmm), minimum overlap (about 2/threshold), maximum ambiguity fraction, drug-resistance site masking, removal or reporting of spurious transitive "triangle" edges, and a contaminant check against the public Los Alamos database.
6. **Look at it.** `hivtrace_viz results.json` draws clusters as circles sized by member count; an expanded cluster colors nodes by risk factor and shapes them by gender.

Typical thresholds by pathogen (starting points only, from the MicrobeTrace wiki):

| Pathogen              | Threshold                                                                                                                                        |

### 5.3 Overlaying genetic and contact networks in MicrobeTrace

Sources: MicrobeTrace wiki: Loading Files, 2D Network View, Nearest neighbor pruning, Timeline, PLOS Comp Bio paper, CDC Toolkit Module 3.2. (URLs in section 11.)

1. **Prepare inputs outside the tool.** Sequences need quality control (remove runs of N) and alignment first. The training slides show "bad alignment" examples that produce misleading networks.
    - For large genomes, compute distances outside MicrobeTrace with SeqRuler: aligned FASTA, a threshold, TN93 or SNP, an ambiguity policy and a core count. The output is a thresholded edge-list CSV ([SeqRuler wiki](https://github.com/CDCgov/MicrobeTrace/wiki/Distance-computation-outside-MicrobeTrace-:-SeqRuler)).
    - For SARS-CoV-2, export a Nextstrain tree in **divergence** mode as Newick, so branch lengths are mutation counts.
2. **Open and accept.** Open microbetrace.cdc.gov and accept the EULA. The app then runs locally.
3. **Add Files.** A genetic input (FASTA, distance matrix as CSV or PHYLIP, pairwise distance list, Newick, or HIV-TRACE JSON), a contact edge list (CSV or XLSX) and a node list of case metadata. Places can be nodes too, as a separate node type.
4. **Check each file's type.** The type guess "often gets this wrong, especially between node and link CSVs": map `source` and `target` on an edge list, `id` on a node list.
5. **Settings (only with sequences).** On the alignment tab, use Smith-Waterman against a reference or the first sequence (no multiple alignment). On the distance metric tab, choose TN93 or SNPs. Set the initial **Link Threshold** in the gear settings.
6. **Launch.** 5,000 HIV _pol_ sequences take about 2.7 minutes. 1,000 SARS-CoV-2 genomes take 1.5 minutes. 1,500 genomes froze the tool (16 GB test machine).
7. **Filter.** Adjust the threshold with arrows or by clicking a bar in the mini distance histogram (hovering shows that bar's threshold); set a minimum cluster size (the paper's HIV example at 1.5% with 5 or more members hid 73.1% of sequences and kept 9.0%); show or hide singletons; optionally turn on Nearest Connected Neighbor pruning, which keeps each node's shortest link (ties kept, epsilon 0.001 admits near-ties) and should be applied to molecular data before epi links are added.
8. **Style.** Color nodes by cluster or a variable. Color links by a variable, which separates genetic from contact links. Set width by distance and size by degree. Choose shapes from about 46 icons. Use grouping polygons by variable. Save a reusable style file.
9. **Read.** Look for components where contact and genetic links coincide, genetic links with no named contact (unnamed partners) and named contacts without genetic support. Check the topologies the wiki warns about: a "starburst" usually means a parsing error such as a blank row read as a node, and a "two-step" chain usually means a bad alignment ([wiki](https://github.com/CDCgov/MicrobeTrace/wiki/Suspicious-Network-Topologies)).
10. **Other views.** Timeline playback over a node date field (onset or diagnosis), with a red Play button. Map or choropleth for geography. Gantt chart for exposure periods. Epi curve, cross-tab, flow diagram, phylogenetic tree and table.
11. **Save and export.** A `.microbetrace` session; "by cluster" export (a zip with one folder of node and edge lists per cluster); images, tables, GeoJSON, FASTA or MEGA (section 8).

Typical time: under an hour per session (mean visit 8.7 minutes, about 2.7 visits per user).

### 5.4 TB source-case ranking and location exposure (LITT, LATTE, WGS)

Sources: LITT, Frontiers in Public Health 2021, CDC TB analytic tools. (URLs in section 11.)

1. **Compile inputs** from templates: case data (disease site, age, smear, cavitation); a SNP matrix, with cases lacking sequencing allowed; epi links graded definite, probable or possible plus user-defined shared risk factors such as homelessness; infectious-period dates, estimated by rule when missing; and an infection-acquisition start (birth or arrival in the country).
2. **Filter.** For each case, drop candidate sources more than 5 SNPs away (or, without sequencing, with no epi link or shared risk), non-infectious ones (for example extrapulmonary), those not infectious before the case's onset or before its acquisition start, and those under 10 years old.
3. **Score.** Four ratings sum to a total where lower means more likely: SNP rating 0 to 5; infectiousness (cavitary 0, smear-positive 1, smear-negative 5); timing 0 to 2; epi link (definite 0 up to none 3). Totals of 8 or more are dropped and the rest graded high, medium or low; a score without SNPs is also produced.
4. **Review.** Results come back as Excel from the Shiny interface in under 60 seconds. Investigators and LITT agreed on the top source for 145 of 181 cases (80%). Use disagreements to find data errors, then re-run with alternative infectious-period lengths.
5. **LATTE.** Compares location stay dates against infectious periods to prioritize location contacts.
6. **Draw it.** Draw the inferred network, a directed source-to-case graph, in MicrobeTrace or by hand. The LITT paper's figures are network diagrams, but the tool does not produce them.

### 5.5 Monitoring cluster growth over time

Sources: hivclustering, Wertheim JID 2018, BC system PMC4853759, Rhode Island pipeline, FitzMaurice EID 2019 time-space. (URLs in section 11.)

1. **Keep cluster names stable.** Re-run the network on the new data month with `hivnetworkcsv --prior previous.json`, so clusters keep their identity across runs (match nodes by entity or sequence). Restrict to a date window with `--after` / `--before`.
2. **Count growth per cluster.** Count new members over the last 12 months (or 3, or 6). Rank clusters by growth relative to size: the NYC retrospective analysis of 65,736 people found cluster-level schemes using past growth beat individual-level schemes at predicting next-year cases.
3. **Automate where possible.** BC queries its drug-resistance database hourly, rebuilds the phylogeny, and uses a minimum cluster size of 5. Monthly reports, produced from a LaTeX template filled by Jinja2, go to public health officers. That system caught a cluster of 11 new cases in three months, 8 with transmitted resistance. Rhode Island ran 18 monthly datasets and found 76% more clustered cases with phylogenetic methods than with distance-only clustering.
4. **Run time-space detection alongside.** An alert fires when a county's 12-month diagnoses exceed the prior 3-year mean by more than 2 standard deviations and more than 2 cases. A 2018-2021 evaluation found many short-lived alerts ([JAIDS 2026 abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41198093/)).
5. **Show growth.** Epi curves per cluster, MicrobeTrace timeline playback, and "growing since last report" lists for review.

### 5.6 Prioritizing contacts and places by network position

Sources: Oklahoma TB, AJPH 2007, Klovdahl Houston TB, Gardy NEJM 2011, San Francisco syphilis PMC5527084, Arkansas MMWR. (URLs in section 11.)

1. **Build the contact graph** from interviews: person to person, or person to place (shelter, bar, residence, event). In San Francisco, partners were linked when their critical periods overlapped (3, 6 or 12 months by stage), giving 2,428 nodes, 2,046 links and 457 components; 78% of components had 2 or 3 people.
2. **Compute metrics.** Degree, reach and betweenness (Oklahoma TB: network-prioritized contacts had an odds ratio of 7.8, 95% CI 1.6 to 36.6, for latent TB); place betweenness (gay bars in Houston linked patients with few direct contacts); eigenvector centrality (San Francisco, model only: alerting about 1.5% of nodes halved modeled prevalence); community detection (Arkansas: in MicrobeTrace, community labels were swapped between neighboring nodes and swaps kept when they improved connectivity, citing Blondel et al. for Louvain modularity and Gephi for partitioning, with no other algorithm named; 565 of 965 cases entered the network, 471 in one component, and 54 gatherings were detected).
3. **Overlay genomics** to separate lineages. In British Columbia, WGS split one MIRU-VNTR genotype into two concurrent outbreaks, and the combined social-plus-genomic network found superspreaders. That network was built in Cytoscape.
4. **Caution.** Evidence that centrality-based prioritization improves outcomes is observational or modeled. In Kampala, only 35% of infected people had a known exposure inside the index case's social network ([PMC11083641](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11083641/)).

### 5.7 Building and reading a time-scaled phylogeny (Nextstrain Augur plus Auspice)

Sources: Nextstrain tutorial, creating a workflow, Auspice view settings, CDC Toolkit Module 3.2. (URLs in section 11.)

1. **Assemble inputs.** A sequences FASTA and a metadata TSV (strain, date, region, country, plus local fields).
2. **Index, filter, align.** `augur index` precalculates sequence composition; `augur filter` drops excluded strains, subsamples (for example 20 per country per month) and applies a date cutoff; `augur align` aligns to a reference and fills gaps.
3. **Build and date the tree.** `augur tree` infers a tree whose branch lengths are nucleotide divergence; `augur refine` runs TreeTime to date internal nodes, place tips by sample date, drop outlier tips and record date confidence intervals. This is the step that turns divergence into a time axis.
4. **Annotate.** `augur ancestral` and `augur translate` put nucleotide and amino acid mutations on branches; `augur traits` infers region and country for internal nodes; `augur distance` adds divergence measures.
5. **Export.** `augur export v2` combines tree, metadata and node data with colors and coordinates into one Auspice JSON.
6. **Read it in Auspice.** Switch layout between rectangular, radial, unrooted, clock and scatter; switch the x axis between divergence and time; color by any field; filter by attribute; show the map (with transmission lines), the mutation entropy panel and the measurements panel. Every interaction is captured in the URL, so a view can be sent to a colleague as a link.
7. **Hand off to a network tool.** To use the tree in MicrobeTrace, view it in **divergence** mode so branch lengths are mutation counts, export Newick, import it, and "start with a link threshold of 6 SNPs and see if the network mimics the tree", then adjust ([Toolkit 3.2](https://www.cdc.gov/advanced-molecular-detection/media/pdfs/ToolkitModule_3.2-508C.pdf)).

### 5.8 Sharing a tree with epidemiologists (Microreact)

Source: [Argimon et al., Microbial Genomics 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/5320705/).

1. **Prepare a CSV** with one row per isolate: a unique `ID` that exactly matches the Newick leaf labels, optional `Latitude` and `Longitude` in decimal WGS84, optional `Year`, `Month`, `Day`, and optional `Column__colour`, `Column__shape` (circle, square, triangle, star) or `Column__autocolour`.
2. **Add the Newick tree.**
3. **Create the project** by drag and drop, by linking files on cloud storage (re-read on every open), or through the API from a pipeline. The project gets a permanent URL with an eight-character id.
4. **Explore the linked views.** Map, tree, timeline and table are linked: clicking a map location, a tree node or a timeline point highlights the same isolates everywhere. Ctrl or Cmd adds to a selection; a lasso selects on map or tree; the search box queries one column or all; a timeline interval filters by date; right-clicking an internal node redraws that subtree, with a history panel to step back.
5. **Share.** Send the URL, or set the project private, public, or public and listed. URL parameters set the opening view (tree type, labels, node size).
6. **Note where the data lives.** Microreact stores project data in its own database, so a jurisdiction with identifiable or embargoed data has to decide whether it may upload at all.

### 5.9 National-scale placement and exploration (UShER plus Taxonium)

Source: [Sanderson, eLife 2022, PMC9704803](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9704803).

1. **Place local genomes** onto an existing mutation-annotated tree with UShER, instead of rebuilding a tree from scratch.
2. **Convert for viewing.** `taxoniumtools` turns a mutation-annotated tree, or a Newick tree plus metadata TSV, into a Taxonium JSONL that carries tree, metadata and mutations together.
3. **Open it.** The browser (client) mode keeps all processing local, which the paper calls out for custom or sensitive data; server mode streams only the visible part of the tree, so a 5.4 million-sequence tree loads in seconds on a modest device; an Electron desktop app handled a 12.5 million-sequence tree on an 8 GB laptop. Client-side mode reached roughly 6 million terminal nodes on some systems.
4. **Explore.** Pan and zoom each axis independently with a minimap; switch between the chronological and the distance tree; color by any metadata field or by genotype at a chosen site; search by metadata or by mutation with AND, OR and NOT, filtering mutation searches by the number of descendant tips. Matches are circled in place rather than listed.
5. **Context.** The public Cov2Tree instance was a daily-updated UShER tree of about 5.6 million SARS-CoV-2 sequences, so a state's few hundred genomes are read against millions of tips.

### 5.10 Enteric cluster detection from allele profiles (PulseNet 2.0, GrapeTree, PHYLOViZ)

Sources: Leeper et al., Microorganisms 2025, PMC12195096, Inter-EURL NGS working group, Guidance document for cluster analysis of whole genome sequence data, v03 2024, Zhou et al., GrapeTree, Genome Research 2018, PHYLOViZ Online, NCBI Pathogen Detection help, chewBBACA. (URLs in section 11.)

1. **Sequence and QC.** Trim reads, check for interspecies and intraspecies contamination, assemble (the EURL guidance names fastp, ConFindr, CheckM and Shovill in the chewieSnake pipeline).
2. **Call alleles.** Run the cgMLST or wgMLST scheme for the organism against the assembly. PulseNet 2.0's caller uses BLASTn against reference alleles and hashes allele sequences with MD5; chewBBACA calls alleles from Prodigal-predicted coding sequences scored by a BLAST score ratio. Scheme sizes are large: 2,513 loci for the STEC cgMLST scheme, 30,717 for the chromosomal wgMLST scheme. The output is a table of allele identifiers, one column per locus.
3. **Turn profiles into distances.** Count allele differences pairwise. The EURL guidance notes that the proportion of loci successfully called is itself a quality metric, because missing loci are excluded from a comparison.
4. **Draw a minimum spanning tree.** GrapeTree's MSTree V2 was written for exactly this: minimum spanning trees over allele profiles with much missing data. Nodes group identical profiles with diameters scaled to the number of isolates, any metadata column can color or label them, and allele differences are written on the branches. PHYLOViZ Online does the same with goeBURST, laid out force-directed, and can delete links above a chosen distance to split the tree.
5. **Watch the scale.** GrapeTree handled up to 8,000 cgMLST sequence types on a 4-core, 8 GB laptop and failed above that on Windows; 100,000 types took a Linux server about 300 GB of RAM and under 700 minutes; trees above 20,000 nodes open collapsed.
6. **Apply the cluster rule.** PulseNet's national STEC definition is five or more clinical cases published within 60 days of each other and within 0 to 10 cgMLST allele differences. Published rules differ by organism, and chaining is a known failure: a New York Salmonella Enteritidis cluster defined at 5 alleles grew to 14 alleles of internal diversity ([PMC10282972](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10282972/)).
7. **Confirm with SNPs.** Re-analyze the candidate cluster with a reference-based high-quality SNP pipeline and tree (CDC's validation used Lyve-SET with RAxML trees and SNP matrices, compared against allele-based dendrograms with tanglegrams). The EURL guidance frames allele-based analysis as the routine surveillance method and SNP analysis as the focused follow-up, and insists that "the interpretation of clustering data cannot only rely on cut-off values or phylogenetic trees; epidemiology and traceback evidence are also needed".
8. **Check national context.** NCBI Pathogen Detection groups isolates by k-mer distance, then builds 50-SNP single-linkage clusters and publishes a tree per cluster as PDF, Newick and ASN.1 through the Isolates Browser and FTP. Its clusters cannot be customized, are re-analyzed continuously (so accessions are not archival), and NCBI states the system "does not identify outbreaks or outbreak membership".
9. **Hand off.** Give the epidemiologist a short list of isolates with source, state and date, and the tree picture, for interviews and traceback.

### 5.11 Healthcare-associated cluster investigation from patient movements

Sources: Wan et al., J Infect Dis 2024, PMC11272044, Stoesser, George et al., JAC-AMR 2024, PMC11369815, Ray et al., Clin Infect Dis 2016, PMC8376187. (URLs in section 11.)

1. **Export movements.** Pull each case patient's ward location history from the EHR: one row per stay, with ward, admission time and discharge time. The London IMP-CPE study used Cerner records for 116 cases across five hospitals over three and a half years.
2. **Derive contact edges from intervals.** Link two patients when they "were present on the same ward on the same day", then aggregate repeated overlaps into a weighted edge by time shared. In London this gave 96 contact pairs among 77 of the 116 cases (66%).
3. **Cluster the contact network.** Walktrap community detection split that network into 12 clusters, the largest holding 45 patients with 7 subclusters. The analysis and drawing were done in Cytoscape 3.10.1.
4. **Overlay genomics and plasmids.** Compare species, strain and plasmid lineage on the same nodes. Here three IncHI2 plasmid lineages carrying *bla*IMP and _mcr-9_ crossed species boundaries and correlated with patient contacts (Kendall coefficient 0.19, p = 3e-7). Only 5 of 116 cases met the conventional same-species outbreak definition, and 57 of 96 contact episodes involved two different species, so the conventional definition would have discarded most of the signal.
5. **Pick sampling targets.** Weighted contacts concentrated in one hospital (about 72%). A Manchester study of the same organism class sampled 349 wastewater sites and 1,247 patients across six wards and linked patients to sink sites through an anonymized ward-movement database; shower, bath, sluice and mop sites were the most often positive (101 of 349 sites, 28.9%).
6. **Zoom out to facilities.** For regional spread, build a facility network where an edge counts patients who visited both facilities in a window. An Illinois analysis built that from one quarter of statewide discharge data across 185 hospitals and found that higher degree centrality predicted higher carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae rates, most strongly in rural counties; it is used to decide which facilities to alert and which should screen admissions.

### 5.12 Chains of transmission in an emergency response (Go.Data, Chainchecker, epicontacts)

Sources: Go.Data reference data guide, WHO Go.Data, PAHO Go.Data country information pack, Akunzirwe et al., PLOS Global Public Health 2025, Nagraj et al., epicontacts, F1000Research 2018, Neo4j, canton of Geneva, 2021. (URLs in section 11.)

1. **Configure the outbreak.** Set the follow-up period (how many days contacts are followed, how many times a day) and edit reference data so each dropdown value carries an icon and a color -- this is what makes the chain graph readable later.
2. **Register cases and contacts.** Cases, contacts and events are the three entity types drawn as nodes in the chains-of-transmission view. Exposure relationships become the edges.
3. **Model clusters as events.** Go.Data's built-in cluster feature only shows as text on an edge, so teams instead create each cluster (a funeral, a ward, a market) as an **event** and link cases and contacts to it as an exposure, which makes it a node in the graph. A third option is to store the cluster in reference data (for example Context of Exposure) and color the edges by it under Edge Display Options.
4. **Run daily follow-up.** Generate the day's contact follow-up lists (the mobile app is used in the field) and record each visit. No source found gives the exact list of follow-up status values.
5. **Read the chain graph.** Filter by date and status, collapse or expand events, and color edges by context of exposure to see which setting drives spread.
6. **Check link plausibility.** Chainchecker takes a CSV or Excel line list with case ids, onset dates and a likely source per case, computes each case's exposure window from an editable incubation period, and shows chains on a timeline plus a healthcare-facility view of which patients passed through which facility. In Uganda's 2022 Sudan virus disease outbreak it linked 11 previously unconnected cases, corrected 13 wrong links and flagged 5 possible hospital-acquired infections. It can overlay genetic distances on the epidemiologic links.
7. **Analyze in R where needed.** epicontacts joins a line list (at minimum a unique case id column) to a contact list (source and target columns), remembers whether the network is directed, and offers interactive plots and network measures.
8. **At larger scale, a graph database.** Geneva loaded laboratory results, venue attendance and travel records into Neo4j, with people as one node type and exposure events as another, to ask who likely infected whom, in which direction, who is a prolific spreader and which chains to prioritize.

### 5.13 Probabilistic who-infected-whom reconstruction (outbreaker2, TransPhylo)

Sources: Campbell et al., outbreaker2, BMC Bioinformatics 2018, PMC6196407, Didelot et al., TransPhylo, Current Protocols 2021, PMC7995038. (URLs in section 11.)

1. **Decide which tool fits.** outbreaker2 works from case data directly; TransPhylo works from a dated phylogeny.
2. **outbreaker2.** Supply sampling dates, genetic data as DNA sequences or a tree, and generation-time and incubation-period distributions. It samples the transmission tree, infection times and unobserved cases as augmented data. The result is an MCMC chain object with plot, print and summary methods: a trace plot for convergence, and a posterior ancestry plot where the size of each circle is how often that individual was sampled as the infector of a given case (index case shown as infector 0).
3. **TransPhylo.** Start from a dated phylogeny whose leaves sit at sampling dates (from BEAST, BEAST2 or BactDating). It returns who infected whom and when, including unsampled intermediate hosts, a phylogeny colored by host so a color change marks a transmission event, a matrix of the probability of direct transmission for every pair, a matrix of the number of transmission steps between pairs, and a medoid tree as a single summary.
4. **Draw it with uncertainty kept.** Every edge carries a probability and a node can have several candidate ancestors, so the picture needs edge weight or opacity by probability, and a way to show the alternatives rather than only the consensus parent.
5. **Compare with interviews.** Read the posterior ancestries against the contact data; disagreements are either a missing interview link or an unsampled case.

### 5.14 Livestock movement networks: tracing, structure, targeted control and simulation

Source: [Noremark and Widgren, BMC Veterinary Research 2014, 10:71](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3974595/).

1. **Get the movement extract.** One row per movement: source holding, destination holding, date, optionally animal category, number of animals and individual animal ids. The package's example dataset holds 70,190 movements over three months; a 2010 Swedish foot-and-mouth disease exercise used real cattle, pig, sheep and goat movements over 90 days.
2. **Set the root and the window.** Name the holding under investigation and the time window (for foot-and-mouth disease, the presumed infectious window).
3. **Trace both directions, respecting time order.** The **ingoing contact chain** is the set of holdings that could have passed infection in to the root, traced backward through time; the **outgoing contact chain** is the set it could have passed infection to, traced forward. Both follow only movement sequences in the correct order, and a holding appearing several times counts once.
4. **Read the summary.** `NetworkStructure` gives source, destination and distance from the root; `NetworkSummary` gives in-degree, out-degree and both contact chains per root holding.
5. **Report.** Generate per-holding HTML or PDF reports, for one holding or many at once, plus network diagrams and, where coordinates exist, maps of contacts over time. These go to the field veterinarians who visit the holdings.
6. **Reuse for surveillance.** The same measures rank holdings by movement exposure for risk-based sampling between outbreaks.

On national data these chains reach far: over the British Cattle Tracing System, 47% of farms had ingoing contact chains of more than 1,000 farms over 12 months and 16% of more than 10,000; the authors computed single 12-month chains with EpiContactTrace and igraph to "reduce computational load" ([Fielding et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6408381/)).

The rest of the field, by the themes of [Sequeira et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12279109/), uses the same movement data for three more jobs.

**Describing the network, static and over time (W3048).** Nodes are holdings in 60.9% of studies and aggregated regions in 29.1%; 99.5% of networks are directed and 57.6% weighted, by animals moved or by number of movements. Data comes from national databases (86 studies, mostly European), regional permits (46, mainly North America and Brazil) or surveys and snowball sampling (43). 125 studies (61.6%) looked at time scale, mostly as monthly networks, though the review says "Most studies also relied on static or largely aggregated networks". The measures in use are in- and out-degree, betweenness, closeness, giant weakly and strongly connected components, path length and diameter, and range (how many holdings each holding can reach). Static and temporal answers differ: on the German pig network the giant weakly connected component held 99% of premises and the strongly connected one 28%, and the average range was 23,154 premises statically against 17,187 along time-respecting paths, an overestimate of almost 35% ([Lentz et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4859575/)).

**Choosing holdings for targeted surveillance and control (W3049).** Rank holdings by a measure, remove them in rank order, and watch the largest component shrink, with random removal as the baseline. In a German pork producer community (15,372 movements between 658 holdings, 2006-2009), removing 31 holdings (6%) ranked by out-degree cut the largest weakly connected component of the three-year network by more than 75%; ranked by ingoing infection chain it took 362 (75%), which the authors put down to "the pyramidal structure and the directed nature of the pork supply chain" ([Buttner et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3771899/)). On the national German pig network, halving the giant strongly connected component took about 100 targeted removals against about 30,000 random ones, with closeness or degree best up to about 50 removals and betweenness beyond 100 ([Lentz et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4859575/)). The best measure therefore depends on the network, and the review notes that "few have demonstrated their implementation in real-world scenarios".

**Simulated contact networks where data is partial (W3050).** A systematic review found 37 models in 52 publications (2009-2022) that generate livestock contact networks: 17 mechanistic (configuration, scale-free, small-world, spatial and agent-based models), 19 statistical (gravity models, exponential random graph models, Bayesian kernel models) and one random forest. About 16 were used to generate networks from limited data, calibrated mostly on network surveys (13 models) or traceability records (12). Validation varied: structural statistics were compared for 14 models, dyad-level accuracy for 6 and epidemic outcomes for 4, and the authors warn that calibrating on "a few select network statistics is unlikely to be sufficient" ([Leung, Rudge and Fournie 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10189310/)).

### 5.15 Provider patient-sharing networks from insurance claims

Sources: Landon et al., JAMA 2012 / PMC3528342; Landon et al., Medical Care 2013 / PMC3723338; DuGoff et al. scoping review / PMC6086089; O'Malley et al., Health Services Research 2021 / PMC7968944; Onnela et al., Applied Network Science 2018 / PMC6214299; CMS Physician Shared Patient Patterns; ResDAC cell size suppression policy. (URLs in section 11.)

1. **Get the claims and the agreement.** Three routes. Identifiable Medicare claims (research identifiable files or limited data sets) come through a CMS data use agreement, usually arranged via ResDAC. A state all-payer claims database, Medicaid or a private insurer's claims cover the people Medicare does not. Or skip the agreement entirely and use the public CMS **Physician Shared Patient Patterns** files, which are already the projected provider network: pairs of NPIs that both treated the same Medicare beneficiary, one file per year from 2009 to 2015, in four windows (the second provider seeing the patient within 30, 60, 90 or 180 days of the first), published as directed dyads so the sequence is preserved, and excluding any pair that shared fewer than 11 different patients that year ([NBER mirror](https://www.nber.org/data/physician-shared-patient-patterns-data.html); [CMS technical requirements, archived](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20170124215733mp_/http://downloads.cms.gov/foia/physician_shared_patient_patterns_technical_requirements.pdf)). Whatever the route, know who is missing: Medicare Advantage enrollees have no encounter data, so they are excluded.
2. **Define what counts as an encounter.** Landon's definition, which later papers reuse: paid carrier (physician) claims, keeping evaluation and management services plus procedures with a relative value unit of at least 2.0, and dropping laboratory and other non-visit services. Specialties whose physician is not individually chosen by the patient (anesthesia, radiology) are dropped as nodes.
3. **Decide the nodes and who is in.** Physicians identified by NPI, or facilities by organizational NPI for facility-level work. Landon dropped physicians who saw fewer than 30 Medicare patients in the year or practiced outside the study regions. Patients are usually attributed to the primary care physician who provided the plurality of their visits, which matters later for linking outcomes.
4. **Project the two-mode network onto providers.** Patients and providers form a bipartite graph; multiplying its adjacency matrix by its transpose gives the provider-provider network, with the tie weight equal to the number of shared patients. Each patient becomes a clique among that patient's providers, so the projection cannot be inverted: a triangle may come from one patient who saw three doctors or from three separate pairs.
5. **Throw away the weak ties, and say how.** The raw projection is far too dense to read (mean density 0.23 before thresholding). Three families are in use:
    - **Relative.** Keep each physician's strongest 20% of ties, and keep a tie only if it is in the top 20% for both endpoints; sensitivity analyses at 10% and 30%. This brought mean density to 0.09 (0.05 at 10%, 0.12 at 30%).
    - **Fixed.** Require at least 1, 2, 5 or 9 shared patients. The review notes a validation study in which the chance that two physicians said they had a professional relationship rose with the number of shared patients and peaked at nine.
    - **Statistical.** A null-model backbone such as the disparity filter, which sets a significance level instead of a weight cutoff (the R `backbone` package implements it, [arXiv 2203.11055](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.11055)). No source found applies it to a patient-sharing network and reports how the conclusions change.
      An alternative sidesteps the choice: tie two physicians only when they treated the same patient within the same clinical **episode of care** (grouped by a commercial episode grouper). Onnela et al. compared this with thresholded networks over 2005-2010 Medicare claims in the same 51 regions and found episode-based ties more persistent year to year (72.4% versus 67.4%) and communities more stable, and argue the episode definition "avoids the thresholding problem altogether".
6. **Measure.** The measures that recur: degree; degree adjusted for panel size (Landon divided by shared Medicare patients and reported a mean of 27.3 ties per 100 patients, range 11.7 to 54.4); betweenness centrality reported relative to the other specialty group (primary care mean 0.38, specialists about 3.1 to 3.5); density; and clustering coefficient (mean 0.55, range 0.40 to 0.67). Expect to defend the choice: the scoping review counted 180 measures across 49 studies and concluded the lack of standard methods makes results hard to compare.
7. **Find the groups and name them.** Newman modularity maximization, one non-overlapping community per physician, is the usual choice. In the 2013 paper the resulting communities held a median of roughly 150 to 165 physicians, and about 77% of their patients' physician visits stayed inside the community, against 56% when physicians were instead assigned to the hospital where they admitted most patients -- the argument that these communities are "naturally occurring" groups that could serve as accountable care organizations. Episode-based networks produced more communities (median 14 versus 9 per region).
8. **Check what the data cannot see, before reading anything into it.** Sampling patients, even at random, distorts the projection unevenly: node and tie counts, degree and centrality are recovered from small samples, while transitivity, clustering and the number of communities need a far larger fraction or the full cohort, and density and transitivity come out too low. One payer is one sample: Medicare claims missed 14.6% of shared-patient relationships in the North Carolina comparison, while showing more relationships per provider than the private claims did.
9. **Link to outcomes and compare places.** Attach spending, utilization and quality measures to physicians or to their attributed patients, and compare regions -- the unit of comparison is usually the hospital referral region, so the output is as often a map or a set of per-region summaries as a drawing of one network.
10. **Publish under the cell rule.** CMS's cell size suppression policy forbids displaying any cell with a value of 1 to 10, applies to percentages and to anything a reader could derive from the other reported numbers, and requires suppressing a second cell when row or column totals would give the hidden one away. In practice that means a labeled edge or node whose count falls below 11 cannot be shown, which is why published figures are unlabeled force-directed layouts (Fruchterman-Reingold in igraph) rather than inspectable networks, and why the public shared-patient files start at 11 shared patients.

## 7. Analysis methods actually used

- **Thresholded distance networks**, then connected components as clusters: TN93 distance for HIV (0.5% and 1.5%), SNP counts for TB, SARS-CoV-2, mpox and bacteria, allele differences for enteric cgMLST.
- **Threshold selection.** AUTO-TUNE scores candidates from a jump in the largest-to-second-largest cluster ratio and a penalized cluster count; across 21 HIV datasets it chose a larger threshold than the published one in 13 cases and smaller in 8 ([Frontiers in Bioinformatics 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/bioinformatics/articles/10.3389/fbinf.2024.1400003/full)).
- **Nearest-neighbor pruning** of weighted genetic links, applied before epi links are added.
- **Bipartite projection then thresholding.** Patient-provider claims projected onto providers, then cut to a backbone by a relative rule (each node's strongest 20% of ties, kept only if mutual), a fixed count of shared patients (1, 2, 5, 9), or a null-model filter; or replaced by an episode-of-care tie definition that needs no threshold (section 5.15).
- **Growth ranking.** New members in the last 3, 6 or 12 months, relative to cluster size; cluster-level growth beat individual-level schemes at predicting next-year cases among 65,736 people ([Wertheim et al.](https://doi.org/10.1093/infdis/jiy431)).
- **Rule-based source ranking** (LITT) and **probabilistic transmission-tree inference** (outbreaker2, TransPhylo; section 5.13).
- **Community detection.** Modularity methods (Arkansas, citing Blondel et al. and Gephi; Newman modularity on claims-derived provider networks) and Walktrap (London hospital contact network).
- **Centrality** (degree, reach, betweenness, eigenvector) in TB, STI and facility patient-sharing work. The evidence that it improves outcomes is observational or modeled: an odds ratio of 7.8 (95% CI 1.6 to 36.6) for latent TB among network-prioritized contacts in Oklahoma, a modeled halving of prevalence from alerting about 1.5% of nodes in San Francisco, and in Kampala only 35% of infected people had a known exposure inside the index case's social network ([PMC11083641](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11083641/)).
- **Time-respecting paths.** Ingoing and outgoing contact chains in livestock tracing; ward-overlap windows in hospital work.
- **Phylogenetics.** Maximum-likelihood trees, TreeTime dating, ancestral states, UShER placement, minimum spanning trees over allele profiles, and cutting a time-scaled tree into clusters (the Rhode Island pipeline found 76% more clustered cases than distance-only clustering).
- **Space-time alerting.** An alert fires when a county's 12-month diagnoses exceed the prior 3-year mean by more than 2 standard deviations and more than 2 cases; a 2018-2021 evaluation found many short-lived alerts ([JAIDS abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41198093/)).

## 8. Outputs and collaboration

- **Line lists**, access-limited even without names, because "even line lists that don't include personally identifiable information can include sensitive information" ([CDC prioritization](https://www.Cdc.gov/hiv/programresources/guidance/hiv-cluster-detection-and-response-guidance/cluster-review-and-prioritization.html)).
- **Cluster narratives and one-page summaries** for the monthly review, giving the cluster case definition (diagnosis years, demographics, area), what is known and what is missing.
- **Structured reports to a funder or national program**: REDCap cluster report forms (initial, quarterly, annual, closeout) and cluster ids entered in eHARS without identifiers.
- **Epi curves, maps and choropleths, Gantt charts of exposure periods, cross-tabs and tables**, which in MicrobeTrace are views beside the network rather than separate products.
- **Images and session files**: PNG, JPEG or SVG; CSV, XLSX or PDF tables; GeoJSON; FASTA or MEGA; a `.microbetrace` session; export "by cluster" producing a zip with one folder of node and edge lists per cluster. A Reproducibility Report, in development, bundles session, thresholds, filters, captioned images and calculation records and marks results stale when inputs change ([wiki](https://github.com/CDCgov/MicrobeTrace/wiki/Reproducibility-Report)).
- **A shareable link** when the data allows it: a Microreact project URL, or an Auspice view whose URL encodes layout, coloring and filters.
- **Per-holding or per-facility reports** in veterinary and hospital work (EpiContactTrace writes HTML or PDF reports per farm; facility networks are used to alert linked hospitals).
- **Figures and tables under a cell-size rule.** Anything drawn from CMS research identifiable files or limited data sets must show no cell of 1 to 10, including percentages and any number a reader could back out from the others, and must suppress a second cell when totals would reveal the first ([ResDAC](https://resdac.org/node/1506)). Published provider networks are therefore unlabeled force-directed pictures used to show structure, with the inspectable detail living in aggregate tables.
- **What is deliberately not shared.** CDC: "Avoid using molecular network diagrams, which are easy to misinterpret, for communicating with partners." Program managers get counts, curves and narratives instead. The ethics debate in section 2 is the reason this constraint is firm rather than stylistic.
