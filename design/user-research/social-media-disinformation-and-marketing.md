# Social Media Researchers, Disinformation Analysts and Marketing Network Analysts

## 1. Summary

This population turns public social media activity into graphs: accounts are nodes, and an edge is one observable act between two accounts (a reply, a mention, a repost, a quote, a follow), or a shared choice that links two accounts indirectly (both used the same hashtag, shared the same URL, or reposted the same post within seconds of each other). Three groups do this work. Communication scholars and think tanks (the Pew Research Center's 2014 Twitter topic-network study is the canonical example) map who talks to whom around a keyword and classify the conversation's shape; media scholars map outlets and websites by hyperlinks and audience co-sharing. Disinformation investigators (DFRLab, Graphika, EBU Spotlight, Bellingcat, the EU's EEAS, university observatories) add follow-network community maps, diffusion traces with timelines, and "coordination networks" that find accounts acting in concert, and present the evidence to platforms, newsrooms and the public. Marketing analysts use network views inside commercial listening tools. All depend on collection that broke in 2023-2024 (X's paid API, the end of CrowdTangle) and are shifting to Bluesky, Telegram, YouTube, Reddit, browser capture and regulated research access.

## 2. Population

**Job titles and sectors.**

- Academia: communication, journalism, political science, information science and sociology faculty and PhD students; computational social scientists. NodeXL studies cover disasters, public health, political movements and gun control (Ahmed, Meier and Smith, SAGE chapter: https://dspace.stir.ac.uk/retrieve/f1292af1-5cdb-400b-820a-bccae8f82e1f/NodeXL%20-%20a%20guide%20to%20network%20insights%20-%20Author%20Version.pdf). A PhD student asking how to draw a 1-million-node network shows the scale ambitions and skill gap (https://lists.aoir.org/archives/list/air-l@lists.aoir.org/thread/HBH254KSAUDCUKJKE5ARBGIGNNSOF4E3/).
- Think tanks and research centers: Pew Research Center with the Social Media Research Foundation (https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2014/02/20/part-1-in-depth-analysis-research-method-and-strategy/).
- Disinformation and OSINT: research analysts at DFRLab (Atlantic Council), Graphika (asks for "experience with network graph analysis" and produces "structured intelligence reports": https://www.graphika.com/careers/senior-research-analyst-influence-operations), Blackbird.AI (Gephi "a strong plus"), Netanomics (ORA-PRO), Moonshot (https://www.ziprecruiter.com/Jobs/Disinformation, https://netanomics.com/careers/2/, https://www.python.org/jobs/8212/). Broadcaster OSINT units in the EBU's Spotlight network (https://spotlight.ebu.ch/about).
- Media-ecosystem and digital-methods researchers: political-communication scholars who map outlets rather than accounts (Berkman Klein's 2016 election study mapped media sources from over two million Media Cloud stories: https://cyber.harvard.edu/sites/cyber.harvard.edu/files/2017-08_electionReport.pdf), and issue-mapping researchers who crawl curated hyperlink corpora with Sciences Po medialab's Hyphe (https://medialab.sciencespo.fr/en/tools/hyphe).
- Government and intergovernmental counter-disinformation analysts: the EU's EEAS strategic communication and foreign information manipulation and interference (FIMI) teams, and national election and security agencies. The 3rd EEAS FIMI report (March 2025) covers 505 incidents in 2024 on about 38,000 channels, about 90 countries and 322 targeted organizations, with X hosting 88 percent of incidents (http://www.eunews.it/en/2025/03/19/the-galaxy-of-foreign-interference-operations-in-brussels-crosshairs).
- Platform trust-and-safety teams publish the downstream product: Meta's threat reports, which since 2017 describe takedowns of "coordinated inauthentic behavior" networks (https://transparency.meta.com/en-us/metasecurity/threat-reporting/).
- Marketing: social listening and intelligence analysts at agencies and brands (https://careers-hunterpr.icims.com/jobs/7167/social-listening-%26-intelligence-analyst/job), influencer marketing managers reporting "engagement, reach, and ROI" (https://www.traackr.com/blog/influencer-marketing-job-responsibilities), and audience-insight strategists using Audiense (1,500+ paid customers claimed: https://audiense.com/why-audiense).

**Rough size (evidence only).** The Coalition for Independent Technology Research surveyed 167 academic and civil-society researchers affected by X's 2023 changes (https://silicon.co.uk/e-marketing/socialmedia/twitter-x-misinformation-537479). CrowdTangle had "tens of thousands" of users; only "several hundred" researchers were reportedly admitted to the Meta Content Library at shutdown (https://techcrunch.com/2024/08/15/meta-shut-down-crowdtangle-a-tool-for-tracking-disinformation-heres-how-its-replacement-compares). No count of marketing network analysts was found.

**How often they touch graph tools.**

- Scholars: project-based bursts, often re-running one saved NodeXL recipe (Ahmed et al.).
- OSINT investigators: per investigation, often under news deadlines; network graphs are one technique beside account forensics (creation dates, name histories, archives).
- Coordination scientists: continuous, code-first.
- Marketers: most never open a standalone graph tool. Network views live inside listening platforms, and influencer selection uses creator-platform metrics. The existing persona's "weekly" frequency has no source.

**Skill profile.**

- Graph literacy: moderate. Practitioners know degree, betweenness, modularity and force layouts; First Draft's widely used tutorial contradicts itself on which direction in-degree points (https://firstdraftnews.org/latest/how-to-use-network-analysis-to-explore-social-media-and-disinformation/).
- Coding: bimodal. NodeXL's stated purpose is "removing the need to master programming skills" (Ahmed et al.). Bellingcat ran a 2022 hackathon because many steps "require specialised scripts and data conversion routines" (https://www.bellingcat.com/app/uploads/2022/08/What-is-a-Social-Network-Analysis-Tool.pdf). Coordination researchers write Python or R (CooRTweet, coordination-network-toolkit, NetworkX, igraph).
- Statistics: scholars are comfortable with descriptive network statistics; coordination researchers use similarity measures, percentile thresholds and manual precision checks.

**Team context.** Academic labs of a PI plus students; OSINT teams of a few analysts plus editors and a global research team for translation (https://dfrlab.org/2023/03/01/networks-of-pro-kremlin-telegram-channels-spread-disinformation-at-a-global-scale/); agency insight teams producing client decks.

## 3. Personas

### Persona A: Dr. Maya Okafor -- communication network scholar (id: communication-network-scholar)

- **Role.** Assistant professor of communication; studies how health and political topics are discussed online. Often co-authors with a think tank.
- **Background.** Social science PhD; Excel-fluent; learned NodeXL in a methods course; can run a provided Python or R script but does not write collectors.
- **Tools today.** NodeXL Pro with the standard Twitter "settings options file" recipe; Gephi for publication figures (NodeXL exports GEXF and GraphML for Gephi); Communalytic or 4CAT for Bluesky, Reddit and Telegram since 2023; SPSS or R for statistics.
- **Data.** One keyword or hashtag collection per topic: 1,000-20,000 accounts. Pew's "pew internet" example had 2,814 vertices and 3,437 edges; Himelboim et al. classified 60 topic networks with 64,445 users and 433,937 ties in total (about 1,000 users per network) (https://www.academia.edu/72459287/Classifying_Twitter_Topic_Networks_Using_Social_Network_Analysis).
- **Goals.** Classify a conversation as polarized, tight, brand, community, broadcast or support (the Pew typology); name each cluster from its top hashtags, URLs and words; identify the influential accounts in each cluster; publish with shareable data (journals such as PLOS One require it -- Ahmed et al.).
- **Frustrations.** Free collection disappeared: academic access ended in 2023 and Enterprise cost $42,000-$210,000 per month (https://www.niemanlab.org/2023/04/among-all-of-his-mistakes-dont-forget-elon-musk-is-singlehandedly-crushing-a-big-chunk-of-internet-research-for-no-good-reason/). NodeXL memory limits (about 80-100 thousand edges on 16 GB). Handles of private individuals must be anonymized in print (Ahmed et al.).

### Persona B: Lukas Brenner -- OSINT disinformation investigator (id: osint-disinformation-investigator)

- **Role.** Analyst in a broadcaster's verification unit or at a DFRLab-style lab; investigates suspected influence operations for publication.
- **Background.** Journalism or international-relations degree; strong at account forensics, archives and translation workflows; light Python; Gephi learned from tutorials.
- **Tools today.** Telegram API and TGStat (creation dates, name histories, subscriber counts), Telepathy, Gephi, Maltego, Wayback Machine, spreadsheets; Hoaxy2 and OSoMeNet for Bluesky diffusion views (Workflow 7); Junkipedia for cross-platform monitoring lists (https://gijn.org/stories/new-tools-monitoring-social-media-junkipedia/).
- **Data.** Seed list of suspect accounts or channels, expanded by forwards or reposts: DFRLab's pro-Kremlin Telegram graph had 3,871 channels and 57,646 forwarded messages from 56 seed channels.
- **Goals.** Show that a set of accounts is connected and acts together; separate amplifiers from originators; tie the network to an operator through shared creation dates, naming patterns and administrators; publish a graph that a general reader can follow, with every unconfirmed claim flagged.
- **Frustrations.** "Hairball" figures, low-quality images and overlapping labels (Bellingcat hackathon brief); sharing an interactive map requires converting a Gephi GEXF to a web viewer; no ground truth (a Telegram propaganda study admits it "lack[s] definitive ground truth": https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08084v1); bot scores are gone because Botometer depended on the X API (OSoMe, above).
- **Variant: government FIMI analyst** (a team like the EEAS's). The unit is the incident: each links to the channels that carried it, a two-mode incident x channel graph over tens of thousands of channels. Each channel carries an attribution tier from the EEAS "FIMI Exposure Matrix": official state, state-controlled (overt), state-linked (undisclosed) and state-aligned (covert) (https://www.coe-civ.eu/kh/3rd-eeas-report-on-foreign-information-manipulation-and-interference-threats-exposing-the-architecture-of-fimi-operations). Incidents are coded with tactics, techniques and procedures (TTPs) from shared catalogues and exchanged in STIX (https://www.eeas.europa.eu/node/410751_en); the open DISARM framework is one such catalogue (ENISA: https://enisa.europa.eu/publications/foreign-information-manipulation-interference-fimi-and-cybersecurity-threat-landscape/@@download/fullReport). Tier and TTP are categorical attributes to color, filter and aggregate by, to show how overt and covert channels interact across platforms.

- **Role.** Research scientist in a university observatory or a trust-and-safety team; builds and validates coordination detectors.
- **Background.** Computer science or network science PhD; Python (NetworkX, igraph, pandas), R packages CooRTweet and CooRnet; job ads ask for "network science, graph ML or computational social science" with NetworkX or igraph (https://www.python.org/jobs/8212/).
- **Tools today.** Custom pipelines following Pacheco et al.'s framework (https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2001.05658); CooRTweet (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/CooRTweet/vignettes/vignette.html); Graham, Hames and Alpert's coordination network toolkit, which writes GraphML for Gephi (https://pypi.org/project/coordination-network-toolkit/1.0.1/); Gephi or visone for figures.

## 4. Use cases (ranked by how often they appear in the sources, with stakes)

1. **Map a topic conversation and name its clusters.** Who participates around a keyword, which groups form, what each group shares. Very frequent (thousands of NodeXL Graph Gallery maps; Pew; Starbird's lab). Stakes: published claims about polarization and echo chambers.
2. **Find the influential accounts in each cluster.** Rank by betweenness, in-degree or most-retweeted. Very frequent across all three groups. Stakes: whom to engage, whom to name in a report.
3. **Classify the conversation's structure.** Pew's six types; network-level density, modularity, isolates share and centralization (Himelboim et al.). Frequent among scholars and marketers ("brands often want to foster communities": Ahmed et al.).
4. **Detect coordinated behavior.** Co-retweet, co-URL, co-hashtag, synchronized posting, forwarding chains. Frequent in disinformation work. Stakes: high -- platform takedowns, attribution to states, legal risk.
5. **Trace amplification of a narrative or URL.** Diffusion networks of who reposted whom with a linked timeline (Hoaxy, OSoMeNet), Telegram forward graphs. Frequent for fact-checkers (Workflow 7).
6. **Two-mode account-hashtag or co-hashtag analysis.** Which hashtags travel together; which communities use which tags. Moderate (Public Data Lab recipe, 4CAT, ORA).
7. **Track change over time.** Repeated snapshots, longitudinal initiators (Jackson and Foucault Welles tracked #Ferguson daily rather than "a single snapshot": https://repository.upenn.edu/asc_papers/788). Moderate.
8. **Select a creator roster and avoid audience duplication.** Marketing; frequent inside creator platforms, rarely in graph tools. Stakes: budget.
9. **Segment an audience into communities.** Audiense interconnectivity (Louvain over follows) versus affinity clustering (https://intercom.help/pulsar/en/articles/11056036-new-community-segmentation-options-in-trac-affinity-vs-interconnectivity). Moderate in marketing.
10. **Map relationships between channels or outlets.** Telegram channel graphs, YouTube channel networks from YouTube Data Tools (https://cc.au.dk/cdmm/tools-and-tutorials/data-collection/youtube-data-tools). Moderate.
11. **Map a population's communities from who follows whom.** The core product of Graphika, the best-known commercial disinformation mapper; also used in health communication research (Workflow 6). Frequent among commercial OSINT vendors; stakes: attribution and client reports.
12. **Map a media ecosystem.** Outlets or websites as nodes, linked by hyperlinks or by shared audiences, colored by audience partisanship (Workflow 8). Moderate among media and political-communication scholars; stakes: published claims about asymmetric polarization.

## 5. Step-by-step workflows

### Workflow 1: Topic network map with NodeXL (the Pew method)

Sources: Pew "How we analyzed Twitter social media networks with NodeXL" (https://legacy.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/9/2014/02/How-we-analyzed-Twitter-social-media-networks.pdf), Ahmed, Meier and Smith (SAGE chapter, above), Pew report (https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2014/02/20/mapping-twitter-topic-networks-from-polarized-crowds-to-community-clusters/).

1. **Design the query.** Keyword, hashtag or handle with search operators. Search is not fuzzy ("haemophilia" misses "hemophilia"), so variants are ORed. Re-collect, because the API "can perform unpredictably, omitting prolific tweeters at peak tweeting times" (Ahmed et al.).
2. **Collect.** NodeXL Pro, Import, Twitter Search Network. Historically up to 18,000 tweets from about the last 7-10 days per search; "Studying longer time periods requires repeated data collections" (Pew). Options: edges for follows, replies and mentions; user data. Since 2023 NodeXL has an X importer using "the web JSON interface" plus Reddit, YouTube, Wikipedia and Flickr importers (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NodeXL).
3. **Inspect raw worksheets.** Edges sheet: one row per relationship event (tweet ID, date, language, counts). Vertices sheet: one row per account (followers, following, bio, join date). The sheets are the data model the analyst trusts.
4. **Edge semantics (as NodeXL defines them).** Four types: follows (one author follows another who also tweeted; the timestamp is the query date because follow dates are unavailable), mentions, replies (a mention at the very start of a tweet), and "tweet" (a self-loop for a post with no reply or mention). Pew did not merge duplicate edges, "to retain details about the individual connections". In the example, 1,586 of 3,437 edges were self-loops.
5. **Run the recipe.** Automate dialog, or import a downloadable "settings options file" (Ahmed et al. recommend the standard Twitter file). One click runs: all graph metrics (degree, betweenness, closeness, eigenvector, PageRank, clustering coefficient), Group by Cluster (Clauset-Newman-Moore by default), Harel-Koren Fast Multiscale layout, Group-in-a-Box (each cluster in its own treemap rectangle: https://lccd.umiacs.umd.edu/node/17181), word counts, top items, time series.
6. **Map attributes to visuals (Autofill Columns).** Pew: vertex size by followers; opacity inverse to followers so big profile photos do not hide small ones; profile photo with screen name; edge width by weight. Image export at 4096 x 3072 px.
7. **Filter.** Dynamic Filters remove isolates or low-degree vertices to see the connected core (Pew Figure 15 shows "only users with at least one connection"). Decision: keep isolates for classification (they define brand clusters) but hide them in the figure.
8. **Read the overall metrics.** Example: 2,814 vertices; 1,370 components of which 1,120 isolates; density 0.00022; modularity 0.62. Isolate share and modularity place the network in the typology.
9. **Name clusters.** Network Top Items sheet lists top URLs, domains, hashtags, words, word pairs and influencers by group. Analysts label groups with their top hashtags and characterize them by the domains they share (Pew: polarized groups "use different URLs, hashtags, and words").
10. **Classify the structure.** Six types: polarized crowd (two dense groups, few bridges), tight crowd (unified, few isolates), brand clusters (many isolates, small fragments), community clusters (several medium groups with own hubs), broadcast network (inward hub-and-spoke: many repost one account), support network (outward hub-and-spoke: one account replies to many). Hybrids are common (Pew). Himelboim et al. formalized this using density, modularity, centralization and isolates (cut-off values not retrieved).
11. **Rank key accounts.** Sort by betweenness; Ahmed et al. list the top five per network in prose.
12. **Publish.** NodeXL Graph Gallery (auto-generated report: edge definitions, algorithms, metrics, top accounts and items per group), PowerPoint, Power BI "INSIGHTS", or GEXF/GraphML to Gephi. No timing measurements found.

### Workflow 2: Retweet or mention map in Gephi from a script or tool collection

Sources: Stewart, Arif and Starbird (https://faculty.washington.edu/kstarbi/examining-trolls-polarization.pdf); First Draft tutorial (above); Public Data Lab co-hashtag recipe (https://recipes.publicdatalab.org/co-hashtag_network.html); Jacomy et al. ForceAtlas2 (PLoS ONE 2014, https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0098679).

1. **Collect.** Keyword collection over a period (Starbird: nine months of #BlackLivesMatter, #BlueLivesMatter and #AllLivesMatter tweets, 248,719 tweets from 160,217 accounts). Today: Communalytic (Bluesky up to 5,000 posts on EDU, 50,000 on PRO: https://socialmedialab.ca/2024/05/01/bluesky-data-is-now-available-via-communalytic-a-no-code-computational-social-science-research-tool-for-studying-online-discourse-2/), 4CAT, Zeeschuimer browser capture (https://bellingcat.gitbook.io/toolkit/more/all-tools/zeeschuimer), Bluesky Jetstream, X pay-per-use API.
2. **Build the edge list.** One relation per graph: retweet (direction conventions differ between tutorials), mention, reply, quote; weight = repeats. Table2Net or a script converts a post table to GEXF (First Draft). IDs are 64-bit integers that "do not render in Excel" and "trip up R's default importer" (ORA tutorial: https://www.cmu.edu/casos-center/research/tools/using-ora-for-social-media.pdf).
3. **Reduce.** Starbird kept nodes with degree greater than one, cutting 160,217 accounts to 22,020 ("to elicit more established information channels"). The co-hashtag recipe deletes the query hashtags themselves (they connect to everything), applies an attribute range filter on frequency and a degree range filter. Pulsar's Gephi export can sample by betweenness for "limited machine capacity". Gephi claims up to 1M nodes and 5M edges (https://gephi.org/desktop).
4. **Lay out.** ForceAtlas2: Scaling, Gravity and LinLog adjusted, run until stable, then Prevent Overlap (recipe). OpenOrd then Yifan Hu for very large graphs (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5540468).
5. **Detect communities.** Statistics, Modularity (Louvain) with a resolution setting (lower gives more, smaller communities; Grandjean's tutorial suggests 0.1-2: https://www.martingrandjean.ch/introduction-to-network-visualization-gephi/). Starbird used Infomap: about a thousand communities, two of which held 91.7% of nodes.
6. **Encode.** Appearance, Nodes, Color, Partition by modularity class; Size, Ranking by in-degree or retweet count; labels only for the top accounts.
7. **Label clusters (the per-cluster content summary).** Starbird: top 10 hashtags in account bios per cluster, top 5 most-retweeted accounts per cluster, top 10 most-followed accounts per cluster. From those, one cluster was read as right-leaning, the other as left-leaning; accounts between clusters "are not necessarily politically centrist".
8. **Overlay external lists.** Starbird matched Twitter's list of 2,752 Internet Research Agency accounts against the graph, found 29 active, and located them in both clusters.
9. **Interpret with caution.** First Draft: visualizations are "by no means conclusive charts"; annotate figures; use a simpler chart if it communicates better.
10. **Publish.** Static image from Preview; Gephi Lite opens a GEXF hosted on GitHub by URL (https://docs.gephi.org/lite/user-manual/share-graph-as-url); Retina for web publication.

### Workflow 3: Coordination network detection

Sources: Pacheco et al., ICWSM 2021 (https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2001.05658); CooRTweet vignette (above); CooRnet (https://rdrr.io/github/fabiogiglietto/CooRnet/man/get_coord_shares.html); Weber and Neumann 2021 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8557266).

1. **State the conjecture.** Which behavior would be suspicious: reposting the same posts, sharing the same links within seconds, using the same hashtag sequence, posting at the same times, sharing the same images, swapping handles.
2. **Extract traces and filter by support.** Drop low-activity accounts: Pacheco et al. required 5-10 actions per account depending on the trace (for example at least 10 retweets for co-retweet). Exclude self-retweets.
3. **Build a two-mode graph.** Accounts linked to features: retweeted posts, URLs, hashtags, 30-minute time bins, image histograms, handles; TF-IDF weights so a viral post counts less.
4. **Project to accounts.** Edge weight = cosine similarity (TF-IDF vectors), Jaccard, or co-occurrence count.
5. **Apply a time window (for co-action methods).** CooRnet estimates the coordination interval from the data or takes it in seconds, plus a minimum repetition count. CooRTweet's `detect_groups()` takes `time_window` (examples 30 and 60 seconds) and `min_participation` (example 2); `generate_coordinated_network()` keeps edges above the median weight by default. Broniatowski used 25 seconds (https://iddp.gwu.edu/sites/g/files/zaxdzs5791/files/downloads/Coordinated%20Behavior%20On%20Facebook%3B%20Broniatowski.pdf); Weber and Neumann used 15 to 1,440 minutes across five criteria, summing weights.
6. **Filter edges.** Keep the most suspicious: top 0.5% of edges (co-retweet, synchronized timing) or top 1% (images); or normalized weight above 0.1, k-nearest neighbors with k = ln(|V|), or focal-structure extraction (Weber and Neumann).
7. **Extract groups.** Connected components of the filtered graph, singletons removed; Louvain to split a giant component; k-core, k-clique and label propagation named as alternatives.
   7b. **Variant: semantic coordination (paraphrase-resistant).** Cinus et al. studied coordination around the 2024 US election on X, Telegram and Facebook (https://arxiv.org/html/2410.22716v4). Cross-platform: a user x URL two-mode graph, TF-IDF, cosine similarity between users on different platforms, minimums of 10 unique URLs per user and 5 occurrences per URL. Within a platform: embed each post with a multilingual sentence model, average cosine similarity between user pairs inside one-day sliding windows, keep edges at 0.95 or above, flag the top 0.5% by eigenvector centrality. The two signals barely overlap (1.3% of Telegram actors, 0.5% of X actors were caught by both), and coordinated Telegram actors posted far more AI-generated text than organic users. TeleTwin (Antonakaki and Ioannidis, https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.23944) shows identical-text, co-URL and synchrony detectors fail once an LLM campaign rewords, staggers or drops links; multilingual semantic similarity recovers "loud" campaigns but misses "quiet" ones, and its false positives fall unevenly across political sides. Every edge therefore needs provenance: the criterion and post pairs that created it.
8. **Validate.** Manual annotation of cluster content and precision at several support thresholds; comparison to random samples; bot scores (Botometer, now archival for X); suspensions; case evidence (price spikes for crypto pump-and-dump). Many coordinated accounts score as human, so bot scores are not proof.
9. **Present.** Account networks with node size by degree, components colored by group, exemplar posts, images or hashtags shown beside each cluster (Pacheco et al.); heatmaps of membership and content similarity (Weber and Neumann). Content types are run separately and combined; "Including multiple platforms does not by itself establish cross-platform coordination" (CooRTweet vignette).

Decision points: the time window, the support minimum and the edge percentile; each changes which accounts are accused. The output is a hypothesis to be confirmed by forensics, not a verdict.

### Workflow 4: Telegram forwarding network investigation (DFRLab)

Source: DFRLab, "Networks of pro-Kremlin Telegram channels spread disinformation at a global scale" (above); companion study of 19 aggregator channels (https://dfrlab.org/2023/06/01/coordinated-telegram-channels-aggregate-anti-ukrainian-news-from-around-the-globe/).

1. **Seed.** 56 channels grouped by naming pattern ("Node of Time", "Surf Noise", "Info Defense").
2. **Collect.** Telegram API: 45,054 messages forwarded by the seeds. TGStat: 12,593 messages other channels forwarded from them, plus creation dates, name histories and subscriber counts. Window: 24 February 2022 to 17 January 2023.
3. **Build.** Nodes = channels (3,871). Directed edges from source channel to forwarding channel (57,646 forwards); weight = forward count. Node size = incoming forwards.
4. **Cluster.** Gephi modularity: nine clusters, colored by class.
5. **Label clusters.** Channel name suffixes ("_ger", "_ita", "SPAIN"), channel descriptions, post language. Labels did not always match clusters (one German channel fell outside the German cluster), which the report states.
6. **Corroborate outside the graph.** Shared creation dates and name changes linked two networks; subscriber charts flagged inactive channels; archived captures; tracing forwards from embassy channels; translation-quality judgments.
7. **Publish.** Full graph plus annotated close-ups, charts, screenshots, archive links, and explicit flags on unconfirmed claims. Graphika's comparable paid reports list "complete network graph maps, raw attribution indicators, cross-platform topology analysis, and the full takedown timeline" (https://graphika.com/spamouflageUS).

GESIS Telegram Toolkit outputs a channel-to-channel GML graph with forward counts (https://methodshub.gesis.org/library/methods/TelegramToolkit). Telegram has no channel directory, so discovery is snowball sampling (https://demtech.oii.ox.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/sites/12/2020/07/Junk-News-Distribution-on-Telegram.-Data-Memo.pdf). No source found for a standard forward-count threshold that defines coordination.

### Workflow 5: Audience and influencer mapping in marketing (what is actually done)

Sources: Pulsar TRAC help (above and https://intercom.help/pulsar/en/articles/7210694-trac-influencer-network-analysis); Audiense/Pulsar segmentation (above); HypeAuditor overlap help (above); Brito, influencer mapping (https://www.britopian.com/influencer-marketing/influencer-mapping-what-is-it-and-doesnt-even-matter/); Ahmed et al. (above).

1. **Define the conversation or audience.** A listening query (brand, category, competitor) or an audience (followers of an account, people with a bio keyword).
2. **Open the vendor network view.** Pulsar's Influencer Network: nodes are authors sized by engagement generated; edges are comments, replies and reposts; clusters color-coded automatically; 2D or (since February 2026) 3D for overlapping communities (https://intercom.help/pulsar/en/articles/13701245-introducing-the-3d-influencer-network-your-audiences-are-3d-your-network-visualisation-should-be-too); updates live with filters.
3. **Segment the audience.** Audiense "interconnectivity" (Louvain over who-follows-whom inside the audience) for niche, local or professional sub-communities, or "affinity" clustering (shared accounts followed) for interests.
4. **Read the map.** Large nodes and cluster centers = accounts driving engagement; accounts between clusters = bridges; click a node to read its posts. Dense interaction patterns "may signal coordination or amplification" (Pulsar 3D note).
5. **Shortlist creators in a creator platform.** Screen by engagement rate, fake-follower scores (https://digiday.com/media-buying/how-influencer-agencies-vet-and-navigate-past-fake-influencers-and-followers), audience demographics, fit and rates, then human review.
6. **Check overlap.** HypeAuditor: up to 15 creators on one platform; outputs overlap count and percentage and a pairwise overlap matrix. Low overlap for reach, high for repeated exposure; overlap is not delivered reach (https://iqfluence.io/public/faqs/influencer-audience-overlap).
7. **Hand off.** PNG, SVG or XLS export from the vendor; Gephi export for a bespoke figure; a ranked creator list with tiers for the campaign team.

Not supported by evidence: centralities over a marketer-owned follower graph of 10,000-1,000,000 nodes (they almost never have one), and churn prediction from network position (no source found). Supported: seeding well-connected people is up to eight times more successful because hubs participate more, "but do not have more influence on their peers" (Hinz et al.: https://ama.org/2019/01/16/seeding-strategies-for-viral-marketing-an-empirical-comparison).

### Workflow 6: Follow-network community map (the Graphika method)

Sources: Graphika maps of pain and oncology Twitter communities (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6932781/); attentive clustering patent US 8,635,281 (https://patents.google.com/patent/US8635281); Graphika's 2018 Congress map (https://techcrunch.com/?p=1698763). A follow graph records standing attention, not acts, and is far denser than an engagement graph (see "Typical sizes").

1. **Seed the population.** A short list of accounts that define the topic: 12 seed accounts for the pain map, 22 for oncology; about 13,500 well-connected accounts following members of Congress and national party accounts for the political map. Today a seed can also be a set of keyword authors or the members of Bluesky starter packs (https://arxiv.org/html/2505.11608v2).
2. **Collect followers and followings.** For each seed, collect its followers and the accounts they follow, adding accounts found during mining (PMC6932781). Follow data is no longer scarce: Bluesky follows are open (39.7M accounts, 2.4B follow edges in one dataset), and X sells follower and following reads at $0.010 per account returned (https://docs.x.com/x-api/getting-started/pricing), so the budget, not access, sets the collection depth.
3. **Cut to the well-connected core.** Keep accounts that are well connected inside the collected set; the published maps describe their accounts as "the most well-connected" (PMC6932781). No published value of the in-set follow minimum was found; the analyst chooses it, or applies k-core pruning.
4. **Build the two-mode profile.** Account x followed-account (or account x cited-source) matrix. Attentive clustering groups accounts by what they pay attention to: the patent clusters sources "that share a similar linking history", and the health maps used a bipartite structural similarity measure with hierarchical agglomerative clustering.
5. **Cluster in two levels.** Fine clusters, then grouped into audience segments: pain map 12,086 accounts, 47 clusters, 8 groups; oncology 12,617 accounts, 48 clusters, 7 groups. A practical substitute is Louvain or Leiden on the in-set follow graph.
6. **Lay out and encode.** Fruchterman-Reingold with a force pushing nodes outward and a force pulling strongly connected nodes together; a node sits near the accounts it follows; node size = number of follow relationships inside the map; color = attentive cluster (PMC6932781). The 2018 map sized nodes by follower count.
7. **Label clusters.** Reviewers read each cluster's bios, cited sources, mentioned users and the accounts it follows, giving priority to the accounts with the most followers inside the map (PMC6932781). Labels are checked by hand.
8. **Interpret and report.** Shared followers between groups (none, in 2018, between extreme pro- and anti-Trump groups), where amplifiers sit, which clusters a suspect network borders.

### Workflow 7: Diffusion trace of a claim or URL

Sources: Hoaxy documentation in the Bellingcat toolkit (https://bellingcat.gitbook.io/toolkit/more/all-tools/hoaxy); Hoaxy2 and OSoMeNet (https://osome.iu.edu/research/blog/exploring-the-latest-osome-tools); Hoaxy system paper (https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1707.07592).

1. **Query.** A claim's keywords, a URL or a post. Hoaxy2 searches Bluesky in real time; paying users can supply their own API keys (OSoMe). Hoaxy was retired in July 2025 and its functions moved to OSoMeNet (Bellingcat toolkit).
2. **Build the network.** Directed edges from reposts, quotes, replies and mentions between accounts, each edge carrying its post and timestamp; nodes are accounts, sized by influence (how often reposted) (Hoaxy paper).
3. **Read the timeline.** A temporal-trends panel plots the cumulative number of posts over time beside the network; the user zooms into an interval, hovers for daily counts, and adds older or newer posts (Bellingcat toolkit).
4. **Replay the spread.** A Play control animates how the network unfolds from the start to the end date of the timeline (Bellingcat toolkit). 2D and 3D views are offered.
5. **Identify early spreaders and amplifiers.** Who posted first, which large nodes caused bursts, which edges bridge communities. Edge colors encode type: blue reposts, orange mentions, yellow replies, red quotes, gray multiple types.
6. **Open the evidence.** Clicking an edge opens the post or posts behind it; clicking a node shows the account's mentions, replies and quotes with links (Bellingcat toolkit).
7. **Export.** Image or data export; networks may be pruned for performance (Bellingcat toolkit). For a published investigation the figure is paired with the timeline and, for platform reports, "the full takedown timeline" (Graphika: https://graphika.com/spamouflageUS).

### Workflow 8: Media ecosystem map

Sources: Berkman Klein Center 2016 election report, methods appendix (https://cyber.harvard.edu/sites/cyber.harvard.edu/files/2017-08_electionReport.pdf); Benkler, Faris and Roberts, "Network Propaganda" (https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/id/4439e7a2-e60f-49cc-b706-384fb6283cbc/9780190923624.pdf); Hyphe (https://github.com/medialab/hyphe, wiki: https://github.com/medialab/hyphe/wiki/User-Manual).

1. **Assemble outlets.** Either a story database (Media Cloud: over two million open-web election stories, plus nearly 900,000 stories resolved from URLs in a 4.5 million tweet sample) or a curated crawl. In Hyphe the researcher defines "web entities" (a page, a site, a subdomain or a combination), crawls their links with a depth limit (`max_depth`; a few hundred depth-2 crawls can take 50 GB), and sorts suggested entities into IN, OUT, UNDECIDED or DISCOVERED, tagging them, in repeated rounds.
2. **Aggregate to outlet nodes.** Stories or pages roll up to their media source or web entity; this is a many-to-one aggregation of the raw data.
3. **Build edges.** Hyperlinks between sources (weight = number of story-to-story links between the pair; each linking source counted once for size), or audience co-sharing: Berkman Klein linked two stories when the same Twitter user shared both on the same day, then projected the user x source two-mode network to sources only.
4. **Size and filter.** Node size = number of other sources linking in (inlinks), or Twitter or Facebook shares. ForceAtlas2 in Gephi on the giant component (about 40,000 sources), then all but the top 900 by inlinks hidden.
5. **Color by audience.** Partisanship attention score per source on -1 to 1: the share of its stories tweeted by users who retweeted one candidate or the other (44,074 users, 206,955 tweets, 5,798 sources), cut into five quintiles from left to right. These colors describe audiences, not content.
6. **Compare views.** The same sources mapped by inlinks, Twitter shares and Facebook shares side by side, and over time periods.

## 6. Data

**Sources after the 2023-2024 access changes.**

- X/Twitter: free academic access ended in early 2023 (Coalition survey: 30 projects cancelled, 47 stalled, 27 moved platforms). X now sells pay-per-use reads: posts at $0.005 each with a 3 million monthly cap, follower and following lists at $0.010 per account returned (https://docs.x.com/x-api/getting-started/pricing), so one million follower records cost about $10,000 (computed). Historical datasets and Tweet ID lists remain in use.
- Facebook and Instagram: CrowdTangle shut on 14 August 2024; Meta Content Library is limited to qualified academic and nonprofit researchers and lacks list-tracking and follower-history features (TechCrunch above). Its API allows 500,000 records per rolling 7 days, use only inside Meta's environment or cleanrooms such as ICPSR's, and no downloading (https://developers.facebook.com/docs/content-library-and-api/content-library-api/guides/rate-limiting). Graph work must happen inside a hosted notebook.
- Bluesky: open firehose. Raw CBOR firehose or Jetstream (JSON over WebSocket, filters by collection and account, resumable cursors) (https://docs.bsky.app/docs/advanced-guides/firehose, https://github.com/bluesky-social/jetstream). Research datasets: 4M users and 235M posts with follow, reply, repost and quote interactions (https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.18984); 39.7M accounts and 2.4B follow edges with starter packs (https://arxiv.org/html/2505.11608v2).
- Telegram: Telegram API, TGStat, Telepathy, GESIS Telegram Toolkit; channels and forwards; snowball discovery.
- YouTube: YouTube Data Tools channel and video networks as GDF for Gephi; co-commenting networks (videos linked when the same user commented on both, weight = number of shared commenters: https://arvel-scholar.pages.dev/resources/workshop/module3-analyse-reseaux/section4-youtube/).
- Reddit: NodeXL and Communalytic importers.
- TikTok: Research API for US and European nonprofit academics (non-academic nonprofits largely refused: https://kinit.sk/when-research-apis-close-the-door-when-you-finally-get-it-it-is-not-what-you-have-asked-for/); Zeeschuimer capture.
- EU: Digital Services Act Article 40 delegated act (Regulation 2025/2050) in force 29 October 2025, giving vetted researchers access to non-public platform data for systemic-risk research via a Data Access Portal (https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/commission-adopts-delegated-act-data-access-under-digital-services-act).
- Marketing: vendor-computed engagement graphs and segments; creator platforms; aggregate API counts.

**Workflow change.** Collection is now per platform and per tool, often manual (browser capture) or quota-bound; datasets are smaller and more fragmented; cross-platform merging of account identities is manual, even though cross-platform graphs are a standard deliverable (Graphika's "cross-platform topology analysis": https://graphika.com/spamouflageUS; Cinus et al.'s cross-platform URL network); and some data cannot leave a cleanroom.

**File formats.** NodeXL workbooks (.xlsx); GEXF (Gephi, 4CAT processors, NodeXL export); GraphML (NodeXL, coordination toolkit); GDF (YouTube Data Tools); GML (Telegram Toolkit); CSV, JSON and NDJSON (Zeeschuimer, Jetstream, API dumps); Gephi Lite JSON; vendor PNG, SVG, XLS.

**Typical sizes.** Topic maps: about 1,000-20,000 accounts (Pew 2,814; Himelboim mean about 1,000). Thesis-scale collections: 100,000-200,000 accounts reduced to about 20,000 for drawing (Starbird). Investigations: 1,000-5,000 channels or accounts (DFRLab 3,871). Coordination detection: 10,000-900,000 accounts before filtering, often hundreds to a few thousand after . Follow-network maps: about 12,000-13,500 accounts after the core cut (Graphika). Follow graphs are much denser than engagement graphs: the Bluesky follow graph averages about 60 follows per account (2.4B edges over 39.7M accounts, computed from https://arxiv.org/html/2505.11608v2), against about 1.2 edges per account in Pew's reply and mention example (3,437 over 2,814) and about 15 forwards per channel in DFRLab's Telegram graph. Media ecosystem maps: about 40,000 sources laid out, 900 shown (Berkman Klein). Government FIMI inventories: about 38,000 channels (EEAS). Platform-scale research: millions of nodes and billions of edges (Bluesky datasets), not drawn whole. NodeXL ceiling: about 200,000 edges with 32 GB RAM.

**Attribute columns.** Per account: handle, display name, numeric ID, platform, followers, following, post count, bio, location, join date, profile image URL, verified flag, computed metrics, cluster; forensic attributes used by DFRLab (creation date, handle or name history, subscriber history); attribution tier and TTP codes in government work. Per edge: relation type, timestamp, post ID, text, URLs, domains, hashtags, media, language, device, engagement counts.

**Identifier problems.** 64-bit numeric IDs corrupted by Excel and R; handles change (Pacheco Case 1 studies handle swapping); one person across platforms has unrelated IDs; deleted and suspended accounts disappear on re-collection; follow edges carry the query date, not the follow date.

**Graph properties.** Directed (reply, mention, retweet, forward); weighted by repeat count; multigraph (several relation types between the same pair, and NodeXL keeps duplicates); self-loops (NodeXL's "tweet" edges); two-mode (account-hashtag, account-URL, account-post, author-subreddit); temporal (every edge has a timestamp; coordination depends on seconds; dynamic GEXF with time spells carries this into Gephi); rarely geographic (tweet coordinates were seldom present).

**Sensitivity and access.** Public but personal data; ethics review; handles of private people anonymized in publications; platform terms forbid redistribution (share Tweet IDs, not tweets); Meta data cannot leave the cleanroom; DSA vetting; legal exposure when naming coordinated actors (X's 2023 restructuring "spurs legal fears": https://www.japantimes.co.jp/business/2023/11/07/tech/musk-x-restructuring-disinformation/).

## 7. Analysis methods

- **Centrality.** Betweenness is NodeXL's default ranking (bridge or broker); in-degree and follower count are the default reading in Gephi and listening tools (broadcaster); PageRank and eigenvector rarely headline.
- **Community detection.** Clauset-Newman-Moore (NodeXL default), Louvain modularity with a resolution parameter (Gephi, Audiense, Weber), Infomap (Starbird), Newman grouping (ORA), connected components after edge filtering (coordination), hierarchical agglomerative "attentive clustering" over shared-following or shared-citation profiles (Graphika: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6932781/, https://patents.google.com/patent/US8635281).
- **Network-level typology statistics.** Density, modularity, isolates share, centralization, reciprocity, components, diameter.
- **Two-mode projection.** Agent x hashtag to hashtag x hashtag (ORA), co-tag networks (4CAT), TF-IDF cosine projections (Pacheco), user x outlet co-sharing to outlets (Berkman Klein).
- **Per-cluster content summaries.** Top hashtags, URLs, domains, words and accounts per group (NodeXL, Starbird); LDA topics (ORA); most-followed accounts and bios per follow cluster, checked by human reviewers (Graphika).
- **Temporal.** Activity time series, cumulative-post timelines with playback (Hoaxy), repeated snapshots, ORA 4-hour windows.
- **Coordination.** Time-windowed co-action, percentile edge thresholds, support minimums, content similarity (5-character n-grams; sentence-embedding cosine similarity in daily windows, Cinus et al.), bot scores.
- **Validation habits.** Re-collect to check completeness; share preliminary maps with the community studied; read cluster members by hand; cross-check takedown lists; review translations; flag uncertainty. Authors report precision but not recall (no ground truth).

## 8. Outputs and collaboration

- **Academic:** journal figures (often side-by-side comparisons: Ahmed et al. compare 12 networks); tables of top accounts and hashtags per cluster; shared workbooks or Tweet ID lists.
- **Think tank:** illustrated report with an archetype gallery (Pew's six structures) and a methods appendix.
- **OSINT:** published investigations with network graphs, annotated close-ups, screenshots, archive links, and explicit statements of what is not confirmed; client intelligence reports with "attribution indicators with confidence scores" (Graphika, 2026 reports: https://graphika.com/spamouflageUS). Platforms publish takedown reports with threat indicators (Meta). Government FIMI reports present incidents, channels by attribution tier and TTP counts, and exchange incident data in STIX (EEAS).
- **Galleries and interactive maps:** NodeXL Graph Gallery, Gephi Lite links to GEXF on GitHub Gists, Retina, sigma.js exports; the Bellingcat hackathon's top suggested tool was a GEXF-to-interactive-web converter.
- **Marketing:** decks with network images, creator shortlists, overlap reports shared by link without login (HypeAuditor), Power BI dashboards (NodeXL INSIGHTS).
- **Reproducibility and audit:** NodeXL recipes and auto-generated method text naming edge definitions, clustering and layout algorithms; ORA advises saving every edited network under a new name.
