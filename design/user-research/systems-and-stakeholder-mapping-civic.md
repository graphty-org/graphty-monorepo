# Systems Mappers and Civic Power-Mappers: Foundations, NGOs, Evaluators and Organizers

Foundation program and evaluation staff, NGO and UN teams, monitoring and evaluation officers, evaluation consultants,
government policy labs, extension educators, participatory modellers, community organizers and corporate stakeholder
engagement teams draw networks to reason about social change. Their graphs come in five shapes:

1. **Theory of change (outcomes pathway).** The most common causal diagram in nonprofits, philanthropy and development:
   a directed, mostly acyclic graph in tiers (interventions, then early, intermediate and long-term outcomes), with an
   "accountability ceiling" line, indicators on outcomes and a rationale or assumption on each arrow. Funders ask for
   one in grant and evaluation plans.
2. **Systems map (causal loop diagram).** Factors that can go up or down joined by signed arrows and grouped into named
   reinforcing and balancing feedback loops. Unlike a theory of change it is cyclic by design. Tens of factors.
3. **Fuzzy cognitive map.** A causal map whose links carry a sign and a weight, stored as an adjacency matrix, often
   collected from dozens of stakeholders and merged; used for "what if" scenarios in environmental management and
   global health.
4. **Actor, stakeholder or power map.** People and organizations joined by typed relationships (board seat, grant,
   donation, advice), built from spreadsheets, interviews, roster surveys, public records or crowdsourcing; twenty to a
   few thousand actors; often paired with an influence-interest grid.
5. **Effects tree.** Ripple Effects Mapping produces a radial tree of a program's effects, kept in mind-mapping
   software and coded in Excel.

They use graph software to make a shared mental model visible, find leverage, decide whom to fund, recruit, pressure or
introduce, report to funders, and present the picture to boards, partners and the public. No survey of tool share was
found. Kumu appears in most of the published foundation, UN and UK government systems maps reviewed here, but cited
cases also used Miro (UK Ministry of Justice), LOOPY (Defra), Vensim (TRUUD), PRSM (CECAN), TOCO and PowerPoint (theory
of change), yEd, Mental Modeler and CIETmap (fuzzy cognitive mapping), XMind (Ripple Effects Mapping), UCINET and
VisuaLyzer (Net-Map), PARTNER and Polinode (network surveys), Graph Commons, LittleSis's Oligrapher, and paper.

## 1. Population

**Job titles and sectors.**

- Philanthropy: program officers running a "systems" strategy; strategy, learning and evaluation officers (Walton Family
  Foundation posts prefer "place-based or systems-oriented work"). Hewlett's Madison Initiative, Democracy Fund and
  Omidyar Network published Kumu systems maps.
- Monitoring, evaluation and learning (MEL) staff and grant writers in nonprofits and development agencies, who build
  and maintain the theory of change a funder asks for. ActKnowledge's facilitator sourcebook notes a professional
  evaluator for monitoring and evaluation "is typically required by any funder".
- Evaluation consultancies: the Center for Evaluation Innovation (guided the Madison map), FSG (actor maps, causal loop
  diagrams), Network Impact and Visible Network Labs (network evaluation), Causal Map Ltd (coding causal claims in
  interviews).
- University extension (Washington State, Minnesota, Florida IFAS, Virginia Tech): community development educators and
  evaluators who run Ripple Effects Mapping; actor mapping at the University of Arizona (AEA 2024 poster).
- Development and multilateral: UNDP Accelerator Labs (UNDP Jordan mapped food-system "stakeholders and existing
  efforts" in Kumu), ODI-style policy influence teams (AIIM matrix), Net-Map interviewers in agricultural and natural
  resource governance (IFPRI origin).
- Government policy labs: UK Policy Lab and UCL (net zero clusters, Kumu), Ministry of Justice Digital (Miro), Defra
  Policy Design Lab (36 colleagues, 150 evidence cards, 8 "system stories").
- Participatory modellers: CECAN Participatory Systems Mapping (PRSM), system dynamics group model building, public
  health complexity researchers (TRUUD, 123 interviews), and fuzzy cognitive mapping teams in environmental management
  (Mental Modeler was designed for stakeholder-based environmental decision-making) and global health (25 practices in nine countries).
- Organizers and activist researchers: community power analysis (Commons Library guides), LittleSis "Map the Power"
  trainees, Networks of Dispossession (Istanbul, Graph Commons), journalists borrowing these maps.
- Nonprofit operations, development and data staff: the "accidental techie" (studio persona Grace), prospect
  researchers mapping board relationships, coalition backbone staff running network surveys.
- Corporate and public-sector stakeholder engagement: public affairs and government relations teams, consultation and
  community relations staff on infrastructure, energy and resource projects. They keep a stakeholder register with
  influence and interest scores, sentiment tracked over time, and owner and date for each engagement action
  (Tractivity), place stakeholders on a two-axis interest-influence grid and log every meeting, email and call so the
  map shows who has rapport (Quorum), and some tools draw relationship network maps (Simply Stakeholders). Their data is
  CRM-like: stakeholders, organizations, issues, a timestamped interaction history and sentiment. Evidence for this
  group is almost entirely vendor pages; it is in scope because it does power and stakeholder mapping, but its
  register, permissions and audit features belong to CRM products, not to a graph tool.

**Size.** No source found for user numbers of Kumu, Graph Commons, PRSM, TOCO or Mental Modeler. LittleSis holds "over
1.6 million relationships between over 400 thousand people and organizations" (littlesis-rails README). PARTNER's
benchmark data held 1,200 networks, 45,000 organizations and 135,524 dyads by September 2017 (Visible Network Labs
webinar). Omidyar sizes one systems mapping effort at a core team of 2-4, an extended team of 3-10 and 10-200+
participants.

**How often they touch graph tools.** In bursts. A systems map takes "2-4 weeks of work over 1-3 months" (internal) to
"9-11 weeks over 6-12 months" (highly participatory) (Omidyar workbook p.12), then refreshes: Hewlett planned to revise
"about every six months or so"; nonprofit relationship maps are refreshed quarterly (GoodUnited). A theory of change is
built in a few facilitated sessions and revised as programs change; its long-term outcome "may continue to be refined
years into the future" (ActKnowledge sourcebook). Network evaluations repeat at baseline and follow-up. A Ripple Effects
Mapping session takes one to two hours (Minnesota Extension). Kumu's docs describe teams with "one designated Kumu
expert, who builds and manages all of the Kumu projects for the team".

**Skill profile.** High domain and facilitation skill, low graph and coding skill. Systems mappers know feedback-loop
vocabulary but rarely network metrics; network surveyors learn degree, density and centralization from tool
descriptions; fuzzy cognitive mappers use R scripts or yEd for centrality (Global Health Action review). Qualitative
researchers know NVivo and Vensim. Nonprofit staff are spreadsheet-strong and often cannot install software (studio
persona, assumed). Many maps start on paper with participants who may not read: Net-Map uses figurines "if you are
working with illiterate interviewees".

## 2. Personas

### 2.1 Maya Okafor -- systems change strategist at a foundation (id: systems-change-strategist)

**Role.** Senior program officer, later strategy, learning and evaluation officer, at a mid-size foundation that took
a systems approach to a field. Works with a developmental evaluator and a Kumu-savvy consultant.

**Tools today.** Sticky notes for loops, Kumu for the digital map and board presentations, Google Docs for the
narrative, a grants database export for the grantee overlay. Her grantees still send her theories of change.

**Data.** 30-80 factors and 15-30 named loops ("generally they include about 15-30 loops", Omidyar workbook p.46), each
with narrative and citations; a second layer of grants on the factors they address (Hewlett built "an integrated
'overlay' map detailing all of our grants and the network of grantees", hewlett.org/?p=66747).

**Goals.** Pressure-test the portfolio: Hewlett examined whether its grants "correspond with the conditions and
dynamics that drive Congressional dysfunction" (Kumu story). Generate learning questions; publish for comment.

**Why not a theory of change.** Hewlett chose a systems map "if we weren't going to use a conventional logic model or
theory of change" (?p=66747) because a map avoids isolating the initiative "from other change efforts as theories of
change often do" (?p=66760). The theory of change is the default she is departing from.

**Frustrations.** The process "is not a one-day endeavor!"; risks of "an overemphasis on 'getting it right'" and of
finding "the right level of 'zoom'"; fear the map becomes "a static communications artifact" (all hewlett.org/?p=66760).
Systems maps "can be notoriously difficult to navigate" (?p=66747). Kumu collaboration: "you have to refresh
constantly" (G2 review, 2021).

### 2.2 Amara Mensah -- monitoring, evaluation and learning officer (id: mel-theory-of-change-officer)

**Role.** MEL officer at an international NGO or a US nonprofit, also drafting grant applications. Owns the theory of
change, its indicators and the evaluation plan.

**Tools today.** Flip charts and post-its in sessions; PowerPoint or Miro (Miro's theory of change extension covers
"assumptions, indicators, learning questions"); TOCO; TASO's free builder, which exports a diagram to PowerPoint and a
narrative to Word; Excel for the indicator table.

**Data.** One long-term outcome, a first row of "4-6 preconditions", pathways three or four levels deep (ActKnowledge
guideline), so roughly tens of outcome boxes (an estimate from that guideline; no published size distribution found);
interventions on arrows; indicators answering who, how many, how much and by when; a one-to-two-page narrative.

**Goals.** A theory the funder accepts; every outcome below the ceiling measurable; a diagram that fits one slide.

**Frustrations.** Drawing software makes revision hard: ActKnowledge built TOCO because "continuous revision requires
drawing software" and there are "many pieces of information to store and organize". Indicator definition is "probably
the most difficult part" (sourcebook).

### 2.3 Dr. Tom Reyes -- participatory mapping facilitator and evaluator (id: participatory-systems-mapping-facilitator)

**Role.** Consultant or researcher who designs and runs mapping workshops for government, NGOs and coalitions, then
digitizes and analyzes the maps. Trained in CECAN Participatory Systems Mapping or group model building; codes
interviews in NVivo.

**Tools today.** Post-its and string in the room; Miro remotely; PRSM or Kumu; Vensim for coded causal maps; NVivo.

**Data.** About 20 factors to start ("if you have more than 20, pick out the 20 most important", CECAN), signed and

**Goals.** A map participants own; analysis that answers their questions; every causal claim traced to a transcript.

### 2.4 Dr. Lena Ortiz -- participatory fuzzy cognitive mapping modeller (id: fcm-participatory-modeller)

**Role.** Environmental management or global health researcher who collects causal maps from many stakeholder groups
(community members, resource managers, clinicians), merges them and runs scenarios with the community.

**Tools today.** Paper and cards in the field; Mental Modeler or yEd to digitize; CIETmap for transitive closure and
merging; R or yEd for centrality; a spreadsheet for themes (Global Health Action review).

**Data.** Per map 10-20 concepts (the Humber bio-economy map settled on 16 factors; a Guerrero map had 12 factors and 38
relationships); links weighted 1-5 or weak/medium/strong, rescaled to 0-1 or -1 to 1; up to 86 stakeholder maps in
one study (review, Table 1); "12 to 15 maps for each stakeholder group" for saturation (Sarmiento et al.).

**Goals.** Category-level maps per stakeholder group; a comparison of groups; scenarios that start a discussion.

**Frustrations.** Maps live in several tools (one to draw, one to merge, one for metrics); label reconciliation across
dozens of maps is manual.

## 3. Use cases (ranked by frequency times stakes)

1. **Build or revise a theory of change for a grant, program or evaluation plan.** Frequency: every major proposal and
   program redesign; the most common causal diagram in the sector. Stakes: high (funding, evaluation design).
2. **Build a shared causal systems map and tell its story.** One build per strategy cycle, refreshes every 3-12 months.
   Stakes: high; it frames grant portfolios (Hewlett, Democracy Fund) or government programs (Defra).
3. **Present the map to funders, boards, partners and the public.** Every board meeting and convening. Stakes: high.
4. **Find leverage and pressure-test a portfolio or intervention.** After each build. Stakes: high.
5. **Map actors and relationships in a field or coalition, or for stakeholder engagement.** Annual or per project;
   continuous for engagement teams. Stakes: medium-high.
6. **Power analysis for a campaign.** Per campaign. Stakes: high, with legal and safety risk.
7. **Network evaluation of a coalition across survey waves.** Baseline plus follow-ups. Stakes: medium (funder report).
8. **Facilitate a participatory workshop or interviews that produce maps** (PSM, group model building, Net-Map, fuzzy
   cognitive mapping, Ripple Effects Mapping). 1-3 sessions per process, or one map per interviewee. Stakes: medium;
   the conversation is "often one of the main useful outputs" (CECAN).
9. **Merge and compare many individual maps** (fuzzy cognitive maps, Net-Maps). Per study. Stakes: medium-high.
10. **Code interviews into a causal map with traceability.** Per evaluation. Stakes: medium-high.
11. **Board and donor relationship mapping.** Quarterly. Stakes: medium.
12. **Crowdsource a public map.** Ongoing. Stakes: medium; quality control is the problem.

## 4. Step-by-step workflows

### Workflow A -- Theory of change by backwards mapping (ActKnowledge method, TOCO)

Sources: ActKnowledge Theory of Change Facilitator's Sourcebook (2012-2013); TOCO introduction slides; Wikipedia
"Theory of change"; TASO builder; Miro marketplace.

1. **Prepare.** Flip chart sheets for the map, a "Parking Lot" and "Interventions"; identify stakeholders and their
   positions.
2. **Name the long-term outcome.** Participants write one definition per post-it; the group sorts and refines;
   ideas that are really preconditions go to the parking lot. The outcome is not final "until the group has finished
   the project".
3. **Backwards map.** Ask "What outcomes must be brought about before we can achieve our LTO?" and place them directly
   underneath; "ideally, the first row should include 4-6 preconditions". Repeat for each outcome ("unpacking"). Turn
   barriers into positive preconditions ("flipping"). Most initiatives have several parallel pathways (the sourcebook's
   example: law enforcement, community involvement and community resources leading to safe neighborhoods). Pathways
   run "from early outcomes at the bottom to longer-term outcomes at the top". Stop when "an 'intervention' at this
   point is obvious"; "three or four steps down from the first row" is typically enough.
4. **Draw the accountability ceiling.** A dashed line across the pathway separates outcomes the organization will be
   held to from higher outcomes it contributes to but does not control (Wikipedia; sourcebook).
5. **Place interventions.** "An intervention can be placed along a connecting arrow leading to the outcome that will
   most directly come about". Map existing programs to see if they connect to any outcome.
6. **Record rationales and assumptions.** Rationales for each connection go on the map (or in a separate document
   without TOCO); assumptions are conditions in the environment; uncertain assumptions can become outcomes. Post-it
   colors may code outcome kinds (orange for longer-term, green for organizational capacity).
7. **Define indicators** with a small group and the evaluator: "every outcome on the map (below the dashed
   accountability line) should have an indicator"; each answers who, how many, how much, by when ("improve reading
   scores by 20% or more ... by December 2012").
8. **Write the narrative** (one or two pages, sometimes one per audience).
9. **Quality review.** The theory must be plausible ("Are the outcomes in the right order? ... Are there big gaps in the
   logic?"), feasible ("Do they need to bring in additional partners?") and testable ("Have you identified solid,
   measurable indicators?").
10. **Hand off.** PowerPoint diagram and Word narrative (TASO builder exports both), the grant's evaluation plan, and
    later revisions as the program learns.

**How it differs from a systems map.** A theory of change is a layered, mostly acyclic outcome hierarchy owned by one
organization, with a ceiling, indicators and interventions; a systems map is cyclic, signed, loop-centered and
describes the whole system rather than one actor's contribution. Hewlett's stated reason for choosing a map was exactly
this difference.

### Workflow B -- Causal loop systems map by hand (Omidyar Systems Practice plus Kumu)

Sources: Omidyar Group Systems Practice workbook (2017); Kumu docs; Hewlett and Democracy Fund posts.

1. **Plan.** Pick a process model (internal, hybrid, highly participatory) and team sizes (above).
2. **Explore forces (2-3 hours).** Enablers and inhibitors "one per sticky note", clustered into 6-8 themes (pp.29-31).
3. **Cause and effect, then loops (6-8 hours).** Chain factors per theme; mark "++", "--", "+-" or "-+"; close chains
   into loops named "R" or "B" plus a label ("R15: Trust in Government", Kumu); or "vicious"/"virtuous" (p.39).
4. **Deep structure (3-4 hours).** One central dynamic; loops arranged "in thematic regions" (pp.43-44).
5. **Build the map (6-8 hours).** Knit loops, merge duplicate factors, reuse connections across loops, "minimize arrows
   that cross", "close all the loops" (pp.45-46).
6. **Digitize.** In Kumu's systems template (elements pinned; build by hand under 50 elements). Polarity as connection
   type ("same"/"opposite" drawn solid or dashed, or "+"/"-" labels at the ends); delay as two ticks; loops added by
   clicking member connections, or "detect loops automatically", which fails on dense maps ("can quickly run into the
   thousands") and on Google Sheets-linked maps.
7. **Enrich.** Narrative and citations on every loop; definitions on factors; "a brief description of why two elements
   are causally related" on connections; fields such as trend and needed change.
8. **Socialize (2-4 hours).** "What resonates? What surprises you? What is missing?"; "capture the rationale" (p.55).
   Hewlett published its draft for public comment.
9. **Find leverage (3 hours).** Mark "frozen", "energy for change", "mixed bag", "bright spots", "ripple effects" on a
   print; "entire loops ... often have the highest potential for impact" (pp.57-58). Optionally Kumu MICMAC.
10. **Hand off.** Presentation (Workflow G), narrative, grants overlay; Democracy Fund calls its maps "living
    documents". Democracy Fund's local news map went from "roughly 43 feedback loops" to 17 over a year (Media Impact
    Funders).

### Workflow C -- Participatory systems mapping workshop and sub-map analysis (CECAN PSM plus PRSM)

Sources: Penn and Barbrook-Johnson, PSM practical guide and "How to design a PSM process" (CECAN); PRSM manual;
Scriptapedia.

1. **Design.** Focal problem, stakeholders ("impacted on or participate directly with the system"), focal factors and
   analysis questions, because "the information we collect and how maps are built is usually modified to allow the
   analysis that we want".
2. **Brainstorm and consolidate.** Factors that "should be able to increase or decrease"; merge duplicates; about 20.
3. **Connect.** Focal factor first, then influences with "+" and "-", links "you are unsure of", agreed rationales.
4. **Check.** Weakly connected nodes, positive-link bias, A->B->C duplicated by A->C, over-connected favorites.
5. **Collect attributes.** Controllability, importance and ownership per stakeholder group, vulnerability, link
   strength; by stickers, voting or online.
6. **Digitize** in PRSM (free, browser-based, live multi-user rooms; imports GraphML, GEXF, GML, DOT, XLSX, draw.io,
   Kumu JSON; History with rollback) or Kumu.
7. **Analyze sub-maps.** Downstream of an intervention; upstream of an outcome; unions and intersections across
   several; paths between intervention and outcome with ego networks of radius 1-3; size by out-degree, in-degree,
   betweenness or leverage; drivers and sinks; influential-but-uncontrollable factors as vulnerabilities. PRSM also has
   a trophic layout by position in causal chains.
8. **Verify and scenario.** Alternative maps "to preserve and examine disagreements"; maps as "updatable living
   documents".

### Workflow D -- Fuzzy cognitive mapping across many stakeholders

Sources: Global Health Action review of 25 FCM practices (PMC11103993); Sarmiento et al. 2024, "Four analysis moments"
(PMC11613336); Gray et al., Mental Modeler (HICSS 2013); Humber bio-economy FCM (PMC3820682).

1. **Initiate.** Define the focal question, obtain ethics review, gather other knowledge sources (literature,
   questionnaires).
2. **Map in small groups or individually.** Groups of "not more than five mappers" were easier to facilitate, often
   split by age and gender; individual sessions for sensitive topics. A facilitator and a reporter run each session.
3. **Name concepts, draw arrows, weight links.** Participants weight each link on a 1-5 scale (no link = 0),
   facilitators anchoring the scale on the strongest and weakest links; or "weak, medium or strong" (Humber).
   Participants verify the map.
4. **Digitize as a matrix.** Adjacency matrices or edge lists, weights rescaled to 0-1 or -1 to 1. Mental Modeler's
   matrix interface lists "all concepts ... on the i and j axes"; literature weights from odds ratios are converted to
   -1 to 1.
5. **Reconcile labels** into standard concepts and categories with pattern correspondence tables.
6. **Merge.** Weighted or unweighted averages of each relationship across maps; category-level maps per stakeholder
   group; Bayesian procedures that update literature maps with stakeholder weights. Report the degree of disagreement
   between sources. Saturation at about 12-15 maps per group.
7. **Analyze.** Probabilistic or fuzzy transitive closure (CIETmap) giving positive, negative and net matrices; then
   degree, in-degree, out-degree and betweenness centrality (yEd, R, igraph).
8. **Run scenarios.** In Mental Modeler each concept can be set "between -1 (strong negative change) and +1 (strong
   positive change)" and relative change shows as a bar chart; the Humber study iterated linear and sigmoid models to a
   fixed point and treated factors ranked consistently under both as robust. The review stresses FCM was not used as a
   predictive tool.
9. **Return to stakeholders.** Simplified summary maps go back to communities for discussion and priority setting.

### Workflow E -- Net-Map influence interviews (IFPRI)

Sources: Schiffer, Net-Map Manual (IFPRI 2007); MSP Guide.

1. **Prepare.** Define the guiding question, the link types (each with a color: "Red: Money, Black: Command, Green:
   Advice, Blue: Information"), a shared definition of influence and a set of goal codes; pre-test. Plan "one to two
   hours" per interview.
2. **Who is involved?** The interviewee names actors; each goes on a card, optionally colored by group (pink for
   government); figurines help illiterate interviewees.
3. **How are they linked?** One link type at a time, starting with the least common. "In cases where the actors
   exchange something, the arrow has two heads"; where they exchange several things, one link carries "arrow heads of
   different colors".
4. **How influential?** Stack pieces into an influence tower per actor ("the more influence an actor has the higher the
   tower"; zero is allowed); the facilitator reads the towers back and the interviewee adjusts; heights are written on
   the map.
5. **What are their goals?** Mark each actor with goal codes (D for development, P for protection); "more than one
   symbol/acronym per actor" is allowed.
6. **Discuss.** Why does this actor have the highest tower? Why is a well-linked actor not influential?
7. **Digitize.** The manual says to "transform the map into a matrix", "one matrix for each kind of link", and names
   UCINET and VisuaLyzer, sizing nodes by tower height. Studies produce one map per interviewee and compare them.

### Workflow F -- Network evaluation of a coalition across survey waves (PARTNER, Polinode, Kumu)

Sources: Visible Network Labs PARTNER webinar (2017) and trust and value brief; Varda et al., AJPH 2015 (PMC4355706);
Polinode survey docs; Kumu SNA guide; HEALing Communities protocol.

1. **Build the roster.** The manager identifies member organizations (PARTNER) or uploads a respondent list whose extra
   columns become node attributes (Polinode).
2. **Survey.** Each respondent rates each roster member: frequency of interaction, type of relationship, and the
   validated dimensions of trust (mission alignment, openness to discussion, reliability) and value (resource
   contribution, power and influence, support and commitment). Polinode stores a slider rating as an edge weight;
   Kumu suggests questions like "Who do you turn to for advice?".
3. **Chase non-response.** Polinode tracks Not Started, Started, Submitted, Bounced and Opted Out and sends reminders.
   Varda et al. kept only networks with response rates above 70% (162 of 177).
4. **Export** one worksheet per relationship question plus node attributes (Polinode), or a respondent-by-member
   matrix.
5. **Decide on direction.** Varda et al. treat each reported tie as directed and report reciprocity (the share of
   mutual ties). No source found on a standard rule for symmetrizing ties when one side did not respond.
6. **Compute.** Density, degree centralization ("most networks start out centralized, become decentralized over time"),
   in- and out-degree, trust and value scores (network-level scores read as percentages; below 3.0 on a dimension flags
   an area for growth, per vendor guidance).
7. **Repeat at follow-up.** Polinode duplicates the survey but not the respondent list, so the new roster is uploaded
   again; members join and leave. No source found on how tools match organizations across waves.
8. **Report.** Side-by-side maps of waves, network-level figures (reports often show only "network-level outcomes such
   as centralization or density", HEALing Communities), and action steps for the coalition.

### Workflow G -- Present the map: narrated walkthroughs, embeds, posters

Sources: Kumu Presentations and Export guides; Hewlett; Democracy Fund; Defra; Oligrapher.

1. Kumu recommends "creating a presentation instead" of a raw link, "so you can break your system map down into smaller
   pieces". Map slides save a view, zoom and focus or filter, so a map is revealed loop by loop; text slides carry
   Markdown.
2. Publish a public or password link; "/embed" and "?autoplay=10"; iframe embeds; republish after edits.
3. Print a PDF poster; PNG at the current view.
4. Alternatives: Hewlett's narrative "walks the user through the map as it unfolds step by step" (?p=66747); Defra split
   a map into 8 "system stories" with animated visuals (LOOPY) and "how might we" questions; Oligrapher's story mode
   walks "through the map"; Ripple Effects Mapping and theory of change results go to funders as PDF, PowerPoint and
   Word.

### Workflow H -- Ripple Effects Mapping (extension and community development evaluation)

Sources: WSU REM facilitator handout (Hansen and Higgins, 2010); Minnesota Extension; Virginia Tech ALCE-297.

1. **Prepare.** Team of facilitator, wall mapper and optional software recorder; invite participants and stakeholders
   (2-20 per WSU; 12-20 ideal per Minnesota). Questions can come from the program's logic model (Virginia Tech).
2. **Appreciative inquiry interviews (10-15 minutes).** Pairs, "not a good friend", ask "Tell me a story about how you
   have used the information received".
3. **Group mapping (60+ minutes).** Program name in the center of butcher paper; immediate results as first branches;
   outer ripples from probes like "who benefits from the activity". Effects are grouped into themes the group confirms;
   unintended consequences are recorded.
4. **Transfer to software.** WSU and Virginia Tech use XMind; Virginia Tech projected the map but it was too small to
   read. Follow-up interviews with absentees are added.
5. **Export and code.** Export to Excel; code each effect short, medium or long term and by the Community Capitals
   Framework (natural, cultural, human, social, political, financial, built) or the triple bottom line.
6. **Report.** A PDF map printed to size for funders and officials, plus counts of coded effects.

The graph is a rooted tree (program, themes, ripples), and the coding happens in a spreadsheet, not on the map.

### Workflow I -- Code interview transcripts into a causal loop diagram

Sources: Eker and Zimmermann 2016 (Systems 4(3):28); Newberry and Carhart 2024 (System Dynamics Review, PMC7616860);
Causal Map app.

1. **Interview.** 16 semi-structured interviews, "21 hours in total", steering toward "specific causal relationships".
2. **Open and axial coding in NVivo**; variables grouped by stakeholder group and theme.
3. **Record causal relationships**, each linked to its passage (Kim and Andersen's "data source reference table", or
   NVivo relationships).
4. **Draw** microstructures in Vensim and merge per interview or theme "over three iterations between a primary and
   secondary modeller" (TRUUD).
5. **Semi-automated variant.** Text search for causal expressions; 284 hits gave 145 real ones (49% false positives).
6. **Report** variables, microstructures and loops per map (TRUUD: 55-133 variables, 0-8 loops); validate in group
   model building workshops. The Causal Map app codes each claim in a narrative as a link and analyzes the claims
   network.

**What the tool must keep.** Every link carries its quotes and sources, a count of sources, and the coder; factor
merges are reversible.

### Workflow J -- Actor, stakeholder or power map from a shared spreadsheet

Sources: Kumu docs; UNDP Jordan; Graph Commons API; LittleSis; Commons Library; University of Arizona poster; ODI AIIM;
Networks of Dispossession interview.

1. **Frame.** Organizers: decision-maker, influencers, allies, opponents (Commons Library). Evaluators: a bullseye by
   issue experience and sector, plus power type (University of Arizona). Policy and engagement teams: alignment or
   interest against influence (ODI AIIM; Quorum's two-axis grid).
2. **Collect.** Brainstorm power holders, find "hidden power", research each; corporate registries and newspapers
   (Networks of Dispossession), campaign finance and lobbying (LittleSis); interviews (Net-Map) or surveys (Workflow F).
3. **Structure the sheet.** Kumu: elements with "Label", connections with "From" and "To", "|" separating multiple
   values, an optional "ID" column for updates. Graph Commons: an edge names its endpoints by name and type, missing
   nodes are created, one edge per type per pair.
4. **Load.** Import, or a live Google Sheet that Kumu re-reads on refresh (read-only, one-way; UNDP Jordan found two-sheet
   sync "requires attention to detail and regular updates").
5. **Derive.** Kumu Cluster and Bridge (one-mode projections); LittleSis interlocks.
6. **Analyze.** Degree, closeness, betweenness, eigenvector, two-step reach, MICMAC; overlapping communities (SLPA),
   with the warning to use them "to ask better questions ... but don't take them as fact" (Kumu). Network weavers look
   for core, periphery and bridges and plan to "close triangles" (June Holley).
7. **Decide and publish or protect.** Who is missing, who bridges clusters; publish on Kumu or Graph Commons (Creative
   Commons), or keep private.

Scale: Networks of Dispossession about 1,550 entities; Kumu suggests a big data template above "a few thousand
elements" and calls "10,000+ data points" likely too large for one project.

### Workflow K -- Nonprofit board, partner and grant network from a CRM export

Sources: studio persona; DonorDock; GoodUnited; Salesforce NPSP Trailhead; Candid.

1. Export contacts, affiliations (person to organization), reciprocal relationships, gifts and grants; or compile board
   data from LinkedIn ("Current and past employers, alma maters, current and recent board service ...", DonorDock).
2. Shape node and edge lists; clean name variants; no shared identifier exists across CRM, LinkedIn and grant data.
3. Load and check counts against the spreadsheet.
4. Overlay the 20-40 prospect list to find "shared employers, alma maters, or industry circles"; bipartite
   funder-grantee views (Candid's "constellation" view).
5. Ask: most connected board members; two-step paths to a prospect; single points of failure.
6. Hand off a slide and a list back to the CRM ("note the connector's name on the prospect record"); refresh
   "quarterly or before a major campaign" (GoodUnited).

## 6. Analysis methods

- **Theory of change checks.** Plausible (order, gaps), feasible, testable (indicators on every outcome below the
  ceiling). Structurally: every outcome reaches the goal, no outcome lacks a precondition or an intervention, every
  below-ceiling outcome has an indicator. No tool was found that runs these checks automatically.
- **Loop identification.** R or B by sign parity (even number of negative links is reinforcing); Kumu lists loops
  shortest first without classifying them; PRSM does not enumerate loops.
- **Leverage.** Qualitative categories (Omidyar); MICMAC influence against exposure (Kumu); drivers, sinks,
  controllability overlays (CECAN).
- **Sub-maps.** Upstream and downstream sets, unions and intersections, paths, ego radius 1-3 (PRSM).
- **Fuzzy cognitive map methods.** Transitive closure (probabilistic or fuzzy), averaging and Bayesian merging,
  centrality after closure, scenario clamping with linear or sigmoid iteration; results indicative, not predictive.
- **Network survey metrics.** Density, degree centralization, reciprocity, in- and out-degree, trust and value scores,
  compared across waves.
- **SNA on actor maps.** Degree, closeness, betweenness, eigenvector, two-step reach; overlapping communities (SLPA);
  one-mode projections (Kumu Cluster and Bridge, LittleSis interlocks).
- **Coding.** Ripple effects coded by time horizon and community capital; interview claims coded to links.
- **Validation habits.** Expert walkthroughs; loop-by-loop workshops (Democracy Fund); two coders (TRUUD); positive-link
  bias checks (CECAN); a public source for every item (Networks of Dispossession); maps returned to stakeholders (FCM).

## 7. Outputs and collaboration

- **Deliverables.** Theory of change diagram on one slide plus narrative and indicator table; interactive published
  maps (Kumu, Graph Commons, Oligrapher) with narratives; PDF posters; network evaluation reports with density figures
  and wave comparisons; ripple maps and coded spreadsheets; scenario bar charts; prospect lists back to the CRM.
- **Collaboration.** Kumu contributors and Pro roles (one 2021 reviewer had to refresh to see edits); PRSM live rooms;
  Graph Commons co-editing; Miro boards; Google Sheets as shared backend; survey tools for many respondents.
- **Versioning.** Kumu metric values overwrite unless renamed; XLSX export drops loops; PRSM has rollback; systems
  mappers want "the rationale behind how you are evolving your map" (Omidyar); coders want arrows traced to transcripts.

**Cross-cutting pain points.**

1. Readability: maps are "visually overwhelming" (Defra), "notoriously difficult to navigate" (Hewlett); remedies are
   sub-maps, stories and narrated slides.
2. Effort: mapping "is not a one-day endeavor!" (Hewlett); coding takes days per transcript; indicators are the
   hardest part of a theory of change.
3. Work spread across tools: draw in one, merge in another, compute in a third (FCM); draw in XMind, code in Excel
   (Ripple Effects Mapping); draw on paper, retype as matrices (Net-Map).
4. Keeping maps alive: quarterly or half-yearly refreshes, survey waves, two-sheet sync, re-import duplicates.
5. Semantics lost in export: loops (Kumu XLSX), outcome tiers and indicators (drawing tools).
6. Provenance and sensitivity: links need rationale and source; power and donor data must stay private.
