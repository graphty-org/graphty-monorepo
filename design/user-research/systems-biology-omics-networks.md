# Systems Biologists and Bench Scientists Working With Molecular Interaction Networks

## 1. Summary

This population turns omics measurements -- RNA-seq, single-cell and spatial transcriptomics, mass spectrometry proteomics and metabolomics, CRISPR and genetic-interaction screens -- into networks and reads biology off them. Most are not graph specialists; they reach for a network because a reviewer expects a "PPI network, clusters and hub genes" figure, because a pathway map explains a mechanism, or because a model predicts which kinase or transcription factor (TF) to target.

Their networks come from four places:

- **Knowledge-based networks.** A gene or protein list mapped onto known interactions: undirected confidence-weighted networks (STRING, BioGRID, IntAct); directed, signed pathways (KEGG, Reactome, SIGNOR, OmniPath, WikiPathways); bipartite metabolite-reaction networks (BiGG, Escher).
- **Networks inferred from a matrix.** Co-expression (WGCNA), partial correlation (MetaboAnalyst DSPC), regulatory networks from single cells (pySCENIC, SCENIC+, and CellOracle, which builds one network per cell type), and cell-cell communication networks whose nodes are cell types (CellChat, LIANA). The main control is a threshold or a top-N cut.
- **Similarity graphs from a feature matrix.** Single-cell analysis builds a k-nearest-neighbor (kNN) graph of 10^4 to 10^6 cells, clusters it (Leiden) and abstracts it (PAGA). Mass-spectrometry molecular networking (GNPS) links spectra by cosine similarity, keeps mutual top-10 neighbors and caps components at 100 nodes, giving thousands of small "molecular families". Immune repertoire analysis links T-cell or B-cell receptor clones whose CDR3 sequences differ by one amino acid and compares the resulting clusters across people (5.16).
- **Spatial neighbor graphs on tissue coordinates.** Spatial transcriptomics (Visium HD, Xenium) and multiplexed tissue imaging (CODEX, imaging mass cytometry) give each cell a position in a tissue section. Squidpy, Giotto and histoCAT join each cell to its Delaunay, k-nearest or within-radius neighbors, then test which cell-type pairs touch more often than chance and cluster cells into recurrent "cellular neighborhoods" drawn over the tissue (3.11, 5.18). Positions are data, not a layout choice.
- **Residue networks in a 3D structure** (adjacent). Structural biologists link amino acids by typed contacts from a PDB file or a simulation trajectory and read allosteric paths from them beside the molecule.

Plant scientists are a large share: Plant Science is 9.0% of works citing Cytoscape, and their gene networks are mostly crop and tree co-expression studies on non-model, often polyploid genomes whose gene ids belong to one genome release and whose annotation is borrowed from Arabidopsis (3.10, 5.17).

The center of gravity is Cytoscape with stringApp and the STRING website; single-cell users stay in R or Python; GNPS users finish in Cytoscape. Graphty's existing records cover the undirected gene-list case; directed signed networks, matrix-derived networks, cell-type networks, metabolic maps, many-small-component molecular networks, kNN graphs, immune repertoire similarity networks, per-cell-type network collections, spatial cell-neighbor graphs over a tissue image and residue networks tied to 3D coordinates are not covered.

## 2. Population

**Job titles.** Postdocs, PhD students and research scientists in genomics, cancer, immunology, neuroscience, metabolism, natural products and plant and crop science labs; staff bioinformaticians in omics and single-cell cores; pharma computational biologists; metabolic engineers; structural biologists (adjacent). Postings list Cytoscape next to R/Bioconductor, for example a Karolinska proteomics engineer (https://www.academicjobs.com/university-jobs/research-engineer-in-bioinformatics-ms-based-proteomics-stockholms-l-n/405981) and a Krogan Lab AP-MS postdoc (https://careers.iscb.org/jobs/view/7736).

**Size (evidence only).**

- Cytoscape: about 300,000 downloads a year, 300+ apps (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10076771/); 20,610,746 starts from 2014-02-09 to 2025-02-25 (https://cytoscape.org/stat.html). No unique-user count found.
- App downloads (read 2026-11-25): ClueGO 442,220; stringApp 436,862; MCODE 272,551; cytoHubba 248,660; EnrichmentMap 182,864 (https://apps.cytoscape.org/apps/stringapp and sibling pages) -- the dominant workflow: STRING network, cluster, rank hubs, annotate. In 2022 papers, 42% cited Cytoscape for its apps (PMC10076771).
- STRINGdb Bioconductor package: 23,532 distinct IPs in 2025 (https://bioconductor.org/packages/stats/bioc/STRINGdb). NetworkAnalyst: more than 220,000 jobs from more than 14,000 users a year (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6602507/).
- GNPS: over 300,000 accesses a month in February 2021, 87% of usage classical or feature-based molecular networking (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8705952); more than 6,767 feature-based jobs in 2019 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7885687).
- Immune repertoire networks: no count of papers that build them found. Citations as a proxy (OpenAlex, read 2026-11-25): GLIPH (TCR specificity grouping, https://doi.org/10.1038/nature22383) 1,007; GLIPH2 (https://doi.org/10.1038/s41587-020-0505-4) 558; Miho et al. 2019, large-scale antibody repertoire networks (https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-019-09278-8) 156; NAIR (https://doi.org/10.3389/fimmu.2023.1181825) 10.
- Spatial neighbor-graph analysis: no user count found. Citations as a proxy (OpenAlex, read 2026-11-25): Squidpy (https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1038/s41592-021-01358-2) 1,313; Giotto (https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1186/s13059-021-02286-2) 1,043; histoCAT (https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1038/nmeth.4391) 724; Schurch et al. 2020, cellular neighborhoods from CODEX (https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1016/j.cell.2020.07.005) 1,168. Rough reading (inference): medium in size, and growing as commercial in situ platforms spread.
- Metabolomics networks without a flux model: MetaboAnalyst processed "over three million jobs submitted from >100 000 users worldwide in the past 12 months" before its 5.0 paper (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8265181); no count of its network jobs was found. Europe PMC full-text hits (read 2026-11-25; mentions, not users): "MetaboAnalyst" 23,053; with "Network Explorer" 66; with DSPC or "debiased sparse partial correlation" 132 (66 in 2022-2024); with "correlation network" 1,101; with "Cytoscape" 2,317; "MetScape", the older Cytoscape app for metabolomics networks, 757. For comparison, flux maps: "Escher" with "COBRApy" or "flux balance" 189; "GNPS" with "molecular networking" 2,862. Citations (OpenAlex, read 2026-11-25): MetaboAnalyst 5.0 4,292, 4.0 3,949, 6.0 2,715; Escher 459; COBRApy 1,584; Metscape 2 460; the DSPC paper (https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/btx012) 300. Reading (inference): metabolomics scientists who draw metabolite networks outnumber flux-map modelers several times over, and most draw them in MetaboAnalyst or Cytoscape rather than in code.
- Plant science: 1,596 works from 2022-2024 cite Cytoscape with Plant Science as their primary subfield (OpenAlex, read 2026-11-25); a sample of 60 is broken down in 3.10.
- Single-cell tools: no user counts found. Rough reading (inference): low hundreds of thousands of Cytoscape, STRING or GNPS users a year.

**How often.** Bench scientists: a few times per project, near a figure deadline. Lab bioinformaticians: weekly. Core staff: daily.

**Skill profile.** Strong domain knowledge; graph literacy low to intermediate; coding from none to fluent R or Python. Most follow tutorials literally, including parameter values, which is why tutorial defaults (STRING 0.4, WGCNA power 6, CellChat p < 0.05, GNPS cosine 0.7, Scanpy k = 15, CellOracle top 2,000 edges) recur across papers.

**Team context.** A bench scientist generates data; a bioinformatician analyzes; the PI decides the story; reviewers ask for database version, cutoff and background.

## 3. Personas

### 3.1 Genomics Cytoscape user (existing id `genomics-cytoscape-user`) -- confirmed, with corrections

The existing record (a cancer genomics postdoc turning 300 DE genes into a STRING network with logFC overlay, MCL clusters, enrichment, hubs, NDEx) matches the published protocols. Additions:

- **Disambiguation is a step.** stringApp preselects the first match for ambiguous names (https://jensenlab.org/training/stringapp/); the Cytoscape DE protocol maps UniProt -> NCBI Gene before the expression table joins (https://cytoscape.org/cytoscape-tutorials/protocols/differentially-expressed-genes/).
- **Spreadsheet-mangled identifiers.** Excel gene-name errors (SEPT2 -> date) appeared in 30.9% of articles with Excel gene lists in 2014-2020 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8357140).
- **Background and cutoff are decisions.** STRING recommends a custom enrichment background (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11701646/); cutoffs vary by query: 0.4 for gene lists, 0.95 for a disease query, 0.999 in the AP-MS protocol.
- **She also builds ceRNA (5.11) and WGCNA (5.7) networks**, and is the main source of cytoHubba top-10 "hub gene" papers, criticized for weak validation and run-to-run instability (https://biostars.org/p/9529294).

### 3.2 Dr. Mei Tanaka -- proteomics bench scientist (new id `proteomics-bench-scientist`)

- **Role.** Cell biologist in a signaling lab; gets 2,000-6,000 quantified proteins, an AP-MS result of one to a dozen baits with tens to hundreds of scored preys (CRAPome, SAINT, MiST or CompPASS: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4332878), or a phosphosite table.
- **Tools today.** STRING website; Metascape Express Analysis, returning a report, Excel and PowerPoint (https://link.springer.com/10.1038/s41467-019-09234-6); Cytoscape only with a core bioinformatician.
- **Goals.** One figure: "my bait's interactors, which are known, which are new, what complexes they form"; a few candidates to validate.
- **Frustrations.** Asked how to color STRING nodes by logFC, a Biostars user was told "use Cytoscape" (https://www.biostars.org/p/322442); bait-prey import keeps only the last value for a repeated prey; hairballs from long lists (https://www.biostars.org/p/496173); site-level data does not fit one row per protein; small lists disconnect at 0.4, and dropping to 0.15 was called close to 9 in 10 false positives (https://support.bioconductor.org/p/9137624); legends redrawn in Inkscape (https://creativedatasolutions.github.io/CDS.courses/courses/network_mapping_101/docs/partial/cytoscape).

### 3.3 Dr. Lukas Weber -- signaling network modeler (new id `signaling-network-modeler`)

- **Role.** Systems biologist in a pharmacology or cancer signaling group; builds directed, signed networks and fits them to perturbation or phosphoproteomics data.
- **Tools today.** OmnipathR / decoupler with `is_directed`, `is_stimulation`, `is_inhibition` columns (https://www.bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/vignettes/OmnipathR/inst/doc/bioc_workshop.html); SIGNOR (about 33,000 causal relations among 8,900 entities: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9825604/); CollecTRI (https://github.com/saezlab/CollecTRI); CARNIVAL and COSMOS (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6848167); CausalPath (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8633371); logic models via SIF and SBML-qual.
- **Data.** Prior networks of 10,000+ signed edges; results of 20-300 nodes with activity scores.
- **Frustrations.** Arrow vs T-bar mapped by hand in Cytoscape; its Analyzer defaults to undirected (https://manual.cytoscape.org/en/latest/Network_Analyzer.html); CausalPath's web viewer has no image export; KGML imports can be incomplete (https://groups.google.com/g/cytoscape-helpdesk/c/67mxNKN9yhs); KEGG needs a license for non-academic use (https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/download/).

### 3.4 Sam Okafor -- network biology core bioinformatician (new id `network-biology-core-bioinformatician`)

- **Role.** Core-facility bioinformatician running the same build/cluster/enrich/figure pipeline for many projects, and WGCNA for sample series.

### 3.5 Dr. Ana Ribeiro -- single-cell computational biologist (new id `single-cell-computational-biologist`)

- **Role.** Immunology or tumor-microenvironment postdoc with 10x single-cell (increasingly multiome and spatial; spatial graphs are 3.11) data: 10,000-100,000 cells in 10-30 cell types; CellChat's examples use 25 and 12 groups (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7889871).
- **Tools today.** Seurat or Scanpy; CellChat, CellPhoneDB, NicheNet or LIANA; pySCENIC, SCENIC+ or CellOracle; the packages' own plots.
- **Graphs she makes.** (a) The cell kNN graph every clustering run builds (5.14), seen as a UMAP, rarely as edges. (b) Cell-cell communication networks: cell-group nodes, directed weighted edges, one layer per ligand-receptor pair or pathway (5.8). (c) Regulatory networks (5.9): regulons, TF-enhancer-gene networks, and one GRN per cluster (24 in the CellOracle tutorial).
- **Goals.** Which cell types exist and talk to which, through what pathways, which TFs drive each type, and what changes with disease or a knockout.
- **Frustrations (from the tools' structure, not a survey).** Cluster count depends on a resolution chosen by eye; UMAP distances between clusters are not meaningful; each pathway is a separate plot; method choice "strongly influence[s]" predictions (PMC9184522); per-cluster GRNs are compared one score plot at a time, and a gene with no edges in a cluster simply has no score.

### 3.6 Dr. Jonas Berg -- metabolic network modeler (new id `metabolic-network-modeler`)

- **Role.** Metabolic engineer using genome-scale models (BiGG, COBRApy) to predict fluxes and knockouts, sometimes painting measured metabolite levels on the same curated maps. Metabolomics scientists who build metabolite networks without a flux model are a separate persona (3.13).
- **Tools today.** COBRApy plus Escher; Escher-FBA; KEGG maps (sources in 5.10).
- **Graphs.** Bipartite metabolite-reaction graphs with direction, reversibility and flux; metabolites with concentrations or shadow prices.
- **Frustrations.** Currency metabolites (water, ATP, NADH) connect to nearly everything and are deleted (https://arxiv.org/pdf/0806.2763) or hidden on the map (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4552468); maps are built by hand; SBML needs conversion to COBRA JSON.

### 3.7 Dr. Lena Okoro -- molecular networking chemist (id `molecular-networking-chemist`, promoted to a full persona)

- **Role.** Natural products chemist, metabolomics scientist or microbiome researcher running untargeted LC-MS/MS. First sketched as minor in network-medicine-and-biomedical-knowledge-graphs.md; GNPS usage (Section 2) makes it a full persona.
- **Tools today.** GNPS or GNPS2; MZmine or MS-DIAL before feature-based networking (PMC7885687); NAP, MolNetEnhancer; Cytoscape with chemViz2.
- **Graph.** Nodes are consensus spectra or LC-MS features (precursor m/z, retention time, abundance per group, library hit, SMILES); edges are cosine similarity with a mass difference; many small components plus singletons.
- **Goals.** Families that differ between sample groups; names for unknowns from annotated neighbors; new analogs of a known compound.
- **Frustrations.** The browser viewer is "limited to a single component at a time" (https://ccms-ucsd.github.io/GNPSDocumentation/cytoscape/); Cytoscape search "is very slow, especially with large networks"; reruns with other settings give families that differ "in size, composition, and natural ordering", per-family random layouts "make setting comparisons an arduous task" (specXplore: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11024886); conventional networking "leave[s] many nodes disconnected" (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.crmeth.2026.101468).

### 3.8 Dr. Priya Nair -- structural network biologist (new id `structural-network-biologist`, adjacent)

- **Role.** Biophysicist or structural biologist studying allostery, mutation effects or protein-RNA communication from PDB, AlphaFold or molecular dynamics (MD) ensembles.
- **Tools today.** RING web server; Cytoscape with structureViz2, UCSF Chimera and RINalyzer; VMD NetworkView with Carma; Bio3D in R (5.15).
- **Graph.** Residues as nodes at 3D coordinates; typed contacts (hydrogen bond, ionic, pi stacking, van der Waals) as a multigraph; correlation weights and contact frequencies from trajectories.
- **Goals.** Critical residues and the paths that carry a signal between two sites; how a mutation or state change rewires contacts.
- **Frustrations.** RINalyzer last released in 2014; hundreds of suboptimal paths to read (5.15); network and structure live in two linked windows (inferred from the tools, not a user report).

### 3.9 Dr. Kofi Mensah -- immune repertoire analyst (new id `immune-repertoire-analyst`)

- **Role.** Computational immunologist in a vaccine, infection, autoimmunity or immuno-oncology group, working on bulk or single-cell T-cell receptor (TCR) and B-cell receptor (BCR) sequencing.
- **Tools today.** Repertoire tables in the AIRR Rearrangement TSV format (`junction_aa`, `v_call`, `j_call`, optional `clone_id`, `duplicate_count`, `cell_id`: https://docs.airr-community.org/en/stable/datarep/rearrangements.html); NAIR or ClustIRR in R, both building igraph objects; GLIPH2 or tcrdist for specificity groups; scirpy's clonotype network for single-cell data (https://scirpy.scverse.org/en/latest/generated/scirpy.tl.clonotype_network.html); Gephi for large drawings (Miho et al.).
- **Graph.** Each node is a clone (one unique CDR3 amino acid sequence, sized by cell count) or a cell; an edge joins two sequences within Hamming or Levenshtein distance 1 (NAIR) or above an alignment-similarity score (ClustIRR). Clusters are pooled across samples and labeled by how many samples and subjects they contain.
- **Goals.** "Public" clusters shared across people, clusters whose frequency differs between patients and controls, and their likely antigen.
- **Frustrations.** Network visualization "limits the informative graphical display of a network to a few hundred antibody clones", while natural repertoires exceed that by at least three orders of magnitude (Miho et al., https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6428871); NAIR's authors write that "only networks with hundred thousand nodes can be visualized" and that the picture gives "only marginal quantitation" (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10443597/); all-pairs distances become "prohibitively expensive" above 100,000 unique sequences (Miho et al.); NAIR was archived from CRAN on 2025-12-15 because its maintainer's email bounced (https://cran.r-project.org/package=NAIR).
- **Variant of whom.** The edge rule (a threshold on sequence similarity) is the protein-family similarity network analyst's (`protein-family-ssn-analyst`), and paired-chain data comes from the single-cell computational biologist's 10x runs. The node (an abundance-weighted clone), the unit of comparison (sample and subject) and the question (which clusters are shared by people with the same disease) belong to neither, so this is a separate persona, a variant of `genomics-cytoscape-user` in the persona roster.

### 3.10 Dr. Halima Duarte -- crop and tree co-expression biologist (new id `crop-coexpression-biologist`, variant of `genomics-cytoscape-user`)

- **Why a persona.** Plant Science is 9.0% of works citing Cytoscape (population-to-designloom-persona-index.md, section 2.1). OpenAlex lists 1,596 works from 2022-2024 that cite Cytoscape and have Plant Science as their primary subfield (query: `cites:W2159675211, primary_topic.subfield.id:1110, publication_year:2022-2024`, read 2026-11-25). In a seeded random sample of 60 of them, classified by title and abstract, the largest group is crop or tree transcriptome networks (co-expression, WGCNA hub genes, regulatory networks): 12. Next come genome-wide gene-family surveys, which usually draw an interaction or co-expression network of the family members (11); plant microbiome studies (9); miRNA, lncRNA and ceRNA networks (6); metabolomics and molecular networking (5); network pharmacology and non-plant subjects that the subfield tag pulled in (5); and other molecular genetics (12). Gene-level crop networks (the first two groups plus the ncRNA one) are 29 of 60. Species seen in the sample: soybean, maize, barley, wheat, rice, Arabidopsis, tomato, cotton, peanut, Brassica, poplar, peach, tea, bamboo, birch, oil palm and Catharanthus. The classification shows what the papers are about, not how each one used Cytoscape; the Gephi-citing plant share is mostly microbiome work and belongs to ecological-interaction-networks.md.
- **Role.** Plant molecular biologist or crop geneticist in an agricultural university or breeding institute, studying drought, heat, cold, salt or pathogen response in a crop, a tree or a non-model plant.
- **Pipeline.** The same WGCNA pipeline as 5.7, ending in hub genes drawn in Cytoscape. A sugarcane drought study (Saccharum spontaneum genome, gene ids such as `Sspon.02G0045390-1B` plus `MSTRG` ids for new transcripts) ran WGCNA at power 10 with minimum module size 50, kept the top 100 gene pairs by edge weight and called the five highest-degree genes hubs, drawn with node size by degree in Cytoscape 3.7.2 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10406934). A second sugarcane study had no reference genome: genes are de novo `Unigene` numbers, the module kept 132 genes with weight above 0.25 for the Cytoscape figure, and annotation came from BLAST and DIAMOND against UniProt, KEGG, GO, Nr, Pfam and eggNOG (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12636891/). A wheat landrace drought study used 84,888 genes from 28 samples, power 16 (signed hybrid), and got 81 modules of 30 to 19,380 genes; Cytoscape 3.9.1 drew the modules, but modules of about 2,000 genes or more were analyzed in R "because Cytoscape struggled to load them" (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10326901/). Sunflower seedling drought (https://Bmcplantbiol.Biomedcentral.com/counter/pdf/10.1186/s12870-024-04932-w.pdf) and maize cold response (https://doi.org/10.3390/genes14081598) follow the same pattern.
- **Identifiers tied to a genome release.** Gene ids carry the assembly and annotation version: wheat `TraesCS4D01G207500` (IWGSC RefSeq annotation v1.0) and `TraesCS4D02G212300` (a later annotation) appear in two papers from the same group (https://doi.org/10.1111/tpj.16248, https://doi.org/10.3389/fpls.2023.1252885); maize `Zm00001d` (B73 v4) became `Zm00001eb` (B73 v5), and Ensembl Plants maps the old models to the new assembly through its ID History Converter (https://plants.ensembl.org/Zea_mays/Info/Annotation/). In the wheat drought study, GO terms existed only for the v1.0 annotation, so the authors copied them to v1.1 genes that were ">99% identical across >90% of the sequence" (PMC10326901).
- **Annotation borrowed from another species.** Enrichment runs in plant-specific tools rather than the human-centered ones: agriGO v2.0 in the wheat study (PMC10326901); PlantRegMap, whose predecessor PlantTFDB 4.0 annotated 59.3% of genes in 165 species by combining sequence prediction with "orthology-based projection" and raised GO coverage 9.1 times on average (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5210657). Gene-family papers that want an interaction network query STRING with the Arabidopsis orthologs of their genes (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11577866/).
- **Interaction coverage.** STRING v12.0's species list (https://stringdb-downloads.org/download/species.v12.0.txt, read 2026-11-25) includes Arabidopsis, rice (japonica), maize, bread wheat, soybean, sunflower, tea and grapevine as "core" organisms and sorghum, barley, upland cotton, poplar, peach and Medicago as "periphery" organisms, but has no entry for sugarcane, bamboo, birch, oil palm or Catharanthus. STRING lets a user upload a FASTA proteome and transfers interactions from related organisms by orthology (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9825434/). For the uncovered species the network is the co-expression network itself, or a STRING network of Arabidopsis orthologs.
- **Frustrations.** Annotation and gene ids belong to different genome releases; borrowed Arabidopsis annotation reaches only the genes with an ortholog; large modules do not load in Cytoscape; hub rules vary from paper to paper (top-degree genes after a top-100-edge cut, kME, or the highest-scoring differentially expressed gene).
- **Variant of whom.** The pipeline is `genomics-cytoscape-user`'s WGCNA workflow (5.7). What differs is the data: polyploid and non-model genomes, release-specific ids, annotation by ortholog and missing interaction sources. Workflow 5.17 covers it.

### 3.11 Dr. Matteo Ferraro -- spatial omics tissue analyst (new id `spatial-omics-tissue-analyst`)

- **Role.** Computational biologist or computational pathologist in a cancer, immunology or tissue atlas group, analyzing spatial transcriptomics (10x Visium HD, Xenium) or multiplexed protein imaging (CODEX, also sold as PhenoCycler; imaging mass cytometry, IMC) of tissue sections.
- **Tools today.** Squidpy on AnnData or SpatialData (https://doi.org/10.1038/s41592-021-01358-2); Giotto in R (https://doi.org/10.1186/s13059-021-02286-2); histoCAT and its successor imcRtools for IMC (https://doi.org/10.1038/nmeth.4391, https://github.com/BodenmillerGroup/neighbouRhood); the Nolan lab cellular-neighborhood scripts for CODEX (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2020.07.005); Voyager in R (https://pachterlab.github.io/voyager/articles/vig5_xenium.html).
- **Graph.** One node per segmented cell (or per Visium bin) fixed at its centroid in micrometers or image pixels, with a cell-type label from clustering or marker gating. Edges join spatial neighbors by Delaunay triangulation, k nearest neighbors or a radius, built per tissue section. Sizes: 118,708 to 531,165 cells per Xenium section in 10x's public datasets (https://www.10xgenomics.com/datasets/xenium-human-lung-preview-data-1-standard; 140,702 cells in the Xenium breast section Voyager uses), 258,385 cells across 140 CODEX regions in Schurch et al. (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7479520/), 720 IMC images from 352 patients in one breast cancer cohort (https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-019-1876-x).
- **Goals.** Which cell types sit next to each other more (or less) than chance, which recurrent multicellular neighborhoods exist, and how their make-up or arrangement differs between patient groups or tracks survival.
- **What she looks at.** Mostly summary statistics: a cell-type by cell-type enrichment heat map and neighborhood maps painted on the tissue. Edges are drawn to check how the graph was built -- Squidpy's guide plots one cell and its neighbors under each graph rule (https://squidpy.readthedocs.io/en/stable/notebooks/examples/graph/compute_spatial_neighbors.html), and Giotto overlays the network on the cells with `spatPlot2D(show_network = TRUE)` (https://giottosuite.com/articles/networks.html). Squidpy's IMC tutorial and Voyager's Xenium vignette build the graph and never draw its edges (https://squidpy.readthedocs.io/en/stable/notebooks/tutorials/tutorial_imc.html).
- **Frustrations (from the tools' structure, not a survey).** The graph rule (Delaunay, k, radius, distance cutoff) changes every downstream statistic yet is chosen once from defaults; Squidpy's own guide shows a radius of 0.3 giving a graph with no edges; the enrichment result is a heat map with no way back to the cells that produced a cell; neighborhood maps and the tissue image live in different plots or tools; at 10^5 to 10^6 cells per section, interactive edge views are rare.
- **Variant of whom, and why a persona of her own.** The cell-type labels come from the single-cell computational biologist's pipeline (5.14), and many spatial analysts are that person with a new assay. But the graph is a different object -- spatial adjacency, not expression similarity -- and the product needs differ: positions are fixed by the image, a raster tissue image sits under the graph, the unit of statistics is a cell-type pair over a spatial graph, and imaging-cytometry users (CODEX, IMC) often come from pathology rather than sequencing. So `spatial-omics-tissue-analyst` is its own persona, in the roster under `genomics-cytoscape-user` like the single-cell persona; workflow 5.18 covers it.

### 3.13 Dr. Amira Haddad -- metabolomics network analyst (new id `metabolomics-network-analyst`, variant of `genomics-cytoscape-user`)

- **Role.** Metabolomics scientist in a clinical, nutrition or biology group running targeted or untargeted LC-MS, GC-MS or NMR studies. Not a flux modeler and not a GNPS molecular networking chemist: her inputs are a list of significant metabolites (often with fold changes) and a samples-by-metabolites concentration table.
- **Why a persona.** The population is larger than the flux modelers' (Section 2: MetaboAnalyst's 100,000-plus users a year; 2,317 full-text papers name both MetaboAnalyst and Cytoscape against 189 for Escher with COBRApy or flux balance), and both her data and her steps differ from W250: an inferred, dense correlation matrix, or a heterogeneous network of metabolites, genes and diseases, instead of a curated bipartite map. She is a variant of `genomics-cytoscape-user` because her networks end in Cytoscape and her steps (paste a list, map ids, pick a network, rank hubs, enrich, export) are the same shape as the gene-list workflow, over other identifiers and node types.
- **Tools today.** MetaboAnalyst Network Explorer, knowledge-driven and DSPC (5.19); MetScape in Cytoscape (Basu et al. 2017: https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/btx012); OmicsNet for multi-omics.
- **Graphs.** Knowledge-driven: metabolites, genes and diseases as typed nodes around her seeds, or seeds highlighted on the fixed KEGG global map. Data-driven: tens to a few hundred features (unknowns included) joined by signed partial correlations.
- **Frustrations.** Networks beyond 200-2,000 nodes are "too dense to visualize and the computer too slow to respond"; labels appear only once a node is enlarged; the interaction networks other than KEGG "are applicable to human studies only" (Tutorial I: https://api2.xialab.ca/api/download/metaboanalyst/tutorial8.pdf); the default DSPC view shows only the top edges by p-value (Tutorial II: https://api2.xialab.ca/api/download/metaboanalyst/tutorial9.pdf).

## 5. Step-by-step workflows

W20-W23 (gene list to network, cluster and annotate, enrichment map, hub ranking) are confirmed by their protocols; deltas are in Section 10. The workflows below are new or deepen existing records.

### 5.1 AP-MS hit list to an interactor figure

Sources: Cytoscape AP-MS protocol (https://cytoscape.org/cytoscape-tutorials/protocols/AP-MS-network-analysis/); Morris et al. 2014 (PMC4332878).

1. **Pre-process (core facility).** CRAPome filter; SAINT/MiST/CompPASS scoring; threshold. One row per bait-prey pair.
2. **Import (5 min).** Bait = source, prey = target, score on the edge. Duplicate preys: only the last value is kept.
3. **Known interactions among preys (5 min).** STRING at 0.999, no added interactors, confirm ambiguous matches.
4. **Merge (5 min).** Match UniProt (AP-MS) with query term (STRING): one network, two edge provenances.
5. **Style (20-30 min).** Force-directed; baits yellow; fill on a cell-line score; width on AP-MS score; AP-MS edges green, STRING gray.
6. **Enrich and chart (15 min).** STRING enrichment FDR 0.05; donut charts for top terms typed as an enhancedGraphics expression into a column.
7. **Export.** Session, PNG/SVG, NDEx, CX2/GraphML/XGMML/SIF.

The bench version stops after step 3 on the STRING website. Complexes are drawn by hand as hulls (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6762067). Sizes: 332 interactions in a SARS-CoV-2 set (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9904289); BioPlex 3.0, 118,162 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33961781/). Bait -> prey is a direction of design; BioGRID TAB 3.0 loses it.

### 5.2 Site-level phosphoproteomics: kinase activity and the kinase-substrate network (W3066), and sites on a STRING network (W242)

The usual network step for a phosphosite table is not a protein interaction network. Analysts ask which kinases changed activity, and answer it from known kinase -> site relationships: kinase-substrate enrichment analysis (KSEA) or PTM signature enrichment analysis (PTM-SEA). The graph that results is directed and two-mode: kinase -> phosphosite, each site belonging to one protein. Drawing sites on a STRING network (the Omics Visualizer route below) is the second, display-first route.

**Kinase activity inference, step by step (W3066).** Sources: KSEA protocol in the kinact toolbox, Wirbel, Cutillas and Saez-Rodriguez 2018, Methods in Molecular Biology 1711 chapter 6 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6126574; https://link.springer.com/protocol/10.1007/978-1-4939-7493-1_6); KSEA App (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5860163; R package https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/KSEAapp/html/KSEA.Complete.html); PTM-SEA (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6398202).

1. **Prepare the site table.** One row per site with log2 fold change per condition or time point and optionally a p-value; site ids are UniProt accession, residue and position (`P06239_S59`); rows that match several proteins (a semicolon in the accession) are dropped (kinact). The KSEA App wants six columns in order: Protein, Gene, Peptide, Residue.Both, p, FC; rows with NA are deleted.
2. **Choose the kinase-substrate source and version.** PhosphoSitePlus alone is the KSEA App default; NetworKIN predictions can be added above a score cutoff (examples use 5 and 3). kinact defaults to PhosphoSitePlus plus SIGNOR, filtered to human-human pairs. PTM-SEA uses PTMsigDB: 491 site-specific signatures, 220 of them human kinase signatures, each site carrying a direction (`Y315-p;u`). Resource size bounds the result: in the 2025 benchmarKIN comparison, OmniPath covers 467 kinases and PhosphoSitePlus 390 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12098709/); PhosphoSitePlus lists 10,995 kinase-substrate pairs (8,004 human) and 23,800 sites (14,663 human) (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11247916).
3. **Score each kinase.** KSEA: the mean log2 fold change of the kinase's measured substrates against the mean and standard deviation of all sites, scaled by the substrate count m, as a z-score; one-tailed p, Benjamini-Hochberg FDR. kinact also offers the mean of significant sites only (p < 0.05) and a delta count (significant up minus down sites, hypergeometric p). PTM-SEA: directional ssGSEA, enrichment of up sites minus down sites, 10,000 permutations, FDR < 0.05.
4. **Set the minimum substrate count and significance.** The KSEA App's `m.cutoff` is "the min. # of substrates a kinase must have to be included in the bar plot"; its examples use m = 5 with p = 0.01 and NetworKIN on, m = 2 with p = 0.05 and NetworKIN off. benchmarKIN scores a kinase only with "at least five measured target phosphorylation sites".
5. **Read the outputs.** KSEA App writes a bar plot of key kinases, a table of all kinase scores, and a table "listing all kinase-substrate relationships used for the calculations": that last table is the network. PTM-SEA outputs are heat maps and volcano plots of normalized enrichment scores. Neither draws the graph.
6. **Draw the kinase-substrate network (the step users take to other tools).** Keep significant kinases and their measured substrates; color kinases by score and sites by log2 fold change; read which sites drive each kinase's score and which sites are shared by several kinases.

**How the dedicated tools draw it.** PhosNetVis (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11247916) draws kinases as triangles (2D) or cones (3D) and substrates as cylinders, edges directed kinase -> substrate from `KinaseID` and `TargetID` columns; sites are not nodes but slices of the substrate's cylinder, blue to red by log2 fold change, at most 10 of the most altered sites per substrate, cylinder height by site count; 2D and 3D force-directed layouts; node size from `KinaseActivity`, `KinaseSize` or `TargetSize`. PTMNavigator (Nature Communications 2025, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11711753/) overlays data on about 3,000 KEGG and WikiPathways diagrams (1,121 human): each modified peptide is an extra node beside its protein, colored up, down or by fold change, with the residue position shown above it; peptides of one protein and direction collapse into a summary node labeled with their count and expand on double click; kinase-substrate relationships are added arrows; inferred kinase activity is a colored border (blue for score < -0.5 at FDR < 0.1); it runs KSEA, RoKAI + KSEA, PTM-SEA and KSTAR among others. PhosphoAtlas (Cancer Research 2016, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4907268) ships a Cytoscape session of 1,733 proteins, 292 kinases, 1,276 substrates and 4,758 kinase -> substrate edges (3,641 site-specific); site knowledge is drawn on the edge (color and continuity of the connector), not as a node, and kinases sit above substrates. So three tools, three site encodings: a chart slice, a satellite node, an edge style.

**Sizes.** A study scores tens of kinases: the benchmarKIN perturbation benchmark covers about 80 kinases over 230 experiments, cut to 135 experiments by the five-target rule (PMC12098709); the kinact example finds 114 measured substrate sites for CDK1 alone (PMC6126574). Measured sites run to thousands (4,624 sites on 3,036 proteins in PhosNetVis's SARS-CoV-2 example). No source gives edge counts for a typical study's drawn network.

**Coverage bias.** Kinases are known for fewer than 5% of the human phosphoproteome (Needham et al., Science Signaling 2019, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30670635/). Predictions for kinases with fewer quantified substrates perform worse (Hernandez-Armenta et al. 2017, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5870625), and benchmarks are themselves "biased towards well-studied kinases" (PMC12098709). Analysts respond by setting the minimum substrate count, adding predicted substrates (NetworKIN, the Kinase Library) or using RoKAI, which propagates over a functional network of kinase-substrate, structural and coevolution links to refine site profiles before scoring (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7895941). A drawing that shows a kinase's substrate count, and which of its known substrates were measured, makes the bias visible; no tool above does this explicitly.

**How common is each route.** OpenAlex citation counts on 2026-11-25 (https://api.openalex.org/works?filter=doi:10.1093/bioinformatics/btx415|10.1074/mcp.TIR118.000943|10.1038/s41467-021-21211-6|10.1093/bioinformatics/btx082|10.1093/nar/gku1267): KSEA App 351, PTMsigDB/PTM-SEA 373, RoKAI 103, the 2017 benchmark 91, "PhosphoSitePlus, 2014" 3,588 (the database serves far more than kinase inference). The share of phosphoproteomics papers that draw a kinase-substrate network rather than a STRING network with sites was not measured: OpenAlex refused further queries that day (HTTP 429).

**Variant: several values per protein on a STRING network (W242).** Source: JensenLab Omics Visualizer exercises (https://jensenlab.org/training/omicsvisualizer/). Import a long table, one row per phosphosite; filter adj p <= 0.01; retrieve a STRING network at 0.7; draw a donut ring per node with one slice per site colored by log ratio, and a categorical pie for cluster. Time-course variant: 1,278 genes, eight time points, one slice each. Node data is one-to-many; the answer is a per-node chart, not one color. This route answers "which interacting proteins move together", not "which kinase drove it"; the signed causal route is 5.4.

### 5.3 Overlay data on a curated directed pathway map

Sources: WikiPathways protocol (https://cytoscape.org/cytoscape-tutorials/protocols/wikipathways-app/); KEGGscape (https://keggscape.readthedocs.io/); KGML (https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/xml/docs/); Pathview (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3702256); KEGG Mapper Color (https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/webapp/color_gui.html); Reactome (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/training/online/courses/reactome-tools-for-analysing-biological-pathways/reactome-tools).

KEGG Mapper Color (5 min): paste ids with values, get the colored map back. Reactome (5 min): numeric columns become "experiments" stepped through on the diagram. Pathview in R (10 min): native KEGG PNG or a recomputed Graphviz layout. Cytoscape (30-60 min): WikiPathways as curated "pathway" or "network"; KEGG via KEGGscape (compartments lost); join expression; fill; legend.

KGML relation subtypes include activation, inhibition, expression, repression, binding, phosphorylation and ubiquitination; SBGN Activity Flow draws inhibition as a bar (https://sbgn.org/symbols). The fixed layout is the point: users want the map as drawn, with data on top.

### 5.4 Causal network from phosphoproteomics or expression (directed, signed)

Sources: CausalPath STAR Protocol (PMC8633371); CARNIVAL (PMC6848167); PathLinker (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5365231).

CausalPath, with published timings: install Java and the jar (under 1 h); format a tab file with ID (G6PD_Y401), symbols, sites, effect and a signed value (about 1 h); set `parameters.txt` (significance 0.1, 10,000 permutations, network FDR 0.1); run (about 1 h), producing SIF and JSON; view at causalpath.org (no image export) or in Newt. CARNIVAL/COSMOS find the smallest sign-consistent subnetwork of an OmniPath prior network by integer programming. PathLinker ranks edges by the k shortest receptor -> TF paths.

Unlike PPI work: the sign is the interpretation; questions are path questions; algorithms must respect direction and sign; layouts are hierarchical or fixed; conversions are lossy (BioPAX -> SIF "is lossy in general": https://www.pathwaycommons.org/pc2/formats). STRING 12.5 added a signed regulatory network (PMC11701646).

### 5.5 Robustness check across confidence cutoffs and databases

STRING's help says 0.7 "is often used as a conservative threshold for downstream computational analyses", 0.4 "may be preferable when broader coverage is desired", and higher thresholds "produce smaller networks that are more biased toward well-studied proteins" (https://string-db.org/help/scores/). A prescriptive 0.4/0.7/0.9 sweep appears only in one anonymous guide (https://neuro-psycho-group.github.io/Protein-Protein-Interaction-Toolkit/06-best-practices.html).

In practice it is manual: re-query at each cutoff, re-run cytoHubba, compare top-10 lists in a spreadsheet. Databases are largely independent curation efforts (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3011985) and network choice changes disease-gene recovery (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5920724). No tool found offers a one-step stability view. Single-cell users do the same across Leiden resolutions with clustering trees (5.14); GRN users rarely do it at all (5.9).

### 5.6 Scripted, reproducible build and publication (deepens W25)

Sources: RCy3 vignettes; Cytoscape Automation (https://genomebiology.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s13059-019-1758-4); NDEx protocols (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8544027); Reimand et al. 2019 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6607905/).

1. Start desktop Cytoscape; `cytoscapePing()`. 2. `createNetworkFromDataFrames(nodes, edges)` or a STRING query. 3. `loadTableData`; `setNodeColorMapping`; save a style. 4. Run MCL, enrichment and Analyzer by command. 5. `exportImage`, `saveSession`, `exportNetworkToNDEx`. 6. NDEx private link for reviewers, then a DOI. 7. Methods text: database, version, date, cutoff, background, parameters.

The EnrichmentMap protocol takes about 4.5 h end to end (Reimand 2019). Users want every parameter, data version and style captured as code.

### 5.7 Weighted co-expression network (WGCNA) and other matrix-derived networks

Sources: Langfelder and Horvath 2008 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2631488/); FemaleLiver tutorials, construction (https://edo98811.github.io/WGCNA_official_documentation/FemaleLiver-02-networkConstr-auto.pdf) and export (https://edo98811.github.io/WGCNA_official_documentation/FemaleLiver-06-ExportNetwork.pdf).

1. **Input.** A samples x genes matrix, often cut to the most variable genes: the paper kept 3,600 of over 23,000 probe sets; the blockwise method handles 50,000 genes in blocks of about 7,000.
2. **Soft-threshold power.** `pickSoftThreshold`; the lowest power where scale-free fit levels off near R^2 0.9 (tutorial: 6).
3. **Adjacency and TOM.** |correlation|^power; topological overlap (TOM) as similarity.
4. **Modules.** `blockwiseModules(power = 6, TOMType = "unsigned", minModuleSize = 30, mergeCutHeight = 0.25)`; modules get color names (18 in the paper).
5. **Relate to traits.** Module eigengene vs traits; module membership (kME); hubs have high kME and intramodular connectivity.
6. **Export one module.** TOM restricted to 30 top hubs, `exportNetworkToCytoscape(modTOM, weighted = TRUE, threshold = 0.02, nodeAttr = moduleColors)`. The tutorial shows the same 30 genes at TOM thresholds 0.10, 0.08, 0.06 and 0.03: edges rise sharply as the threshold drops, and the user picks the readable one by eye.

For a tool: a dense symmetric weighted matrix, not an edge list; modules as a node attribute; a weight threshold and top-k with live edge counts; readable thresholds can drop the hubs (Biostars 9514423). Same shape: GRNBoost2 adjacencies (5.9), DSPC partial correlations, microbial co-occurrence.

### 5.8 Cell-cell communication from single-cell data (CellChat, LIANA)

Sources: CellChat paper (PMC7889871); `netVisual_aggregate` (https://rdrr.io/pkg/CellChat/man/netVisual_aggregate.html); `netVisual_diffInteraction` (https://rdrr.io/github/sqjin/CellChat/man/netVisual_diffInteraction.html); tutorial code (https://openbiox.github.io/Bizard/Omics/CellChatCirclePlot.html); LIANA (PMC9184522).

1. **Create and preprocess.** `createCellChat(object = data, meta = meta, group.by = "cell_type")`; `CellChatDB.human` (2,021 interactions in 229 pathway families originally); `identifyOverExpressedGenes` (Wilcoxon, p 0.05).
2. **Infer.** `computeCommunProb(type = "triMean")` (100 permutations, p < 0.05); `filterCommunication(min.cells = 10)`; `computeCommunProbPathway`.
3. **Aggregate.** `aggregateNet` gives `net$count` and `net$weight` between each pair of groups.
4. **Draw.** `netVisual_circle(net$count, vertex.weight = groupSize, weight.scale = TRUE)`; per-sender panels keep `edge.weight.max` global so widths compare. Per pathway: `netVisual_aggregate(signaling = "CXCL", layout = "circle" | "hierarchy" | "chord" | "spatial")`, with `sources.use`, `targets.use`, `thresh` and `top`.
5. **Compare conditions.** `mergeCellChat`, `netVisual_diffInteraction(measure = "count" | "weight")`: red increased, blue decreased.

Data shape: a K x K x N array (K groups, N ligand-receptor pairs or pathways); each slice a directed weighted graph on the same 10-30 nodes. LIANA runs 7 methods and 16 resources and ranks by consensus because method and resource choice strongly change predictions (PMC9184522).

### 5.9 Gene regulatory networks from single cells (pySCENIC, SCENIC+, CellOracle and others)

Three tools, three graph shapes: TF -> target regulons, TF-enhancer-gene networks, and one network per cell type compared by centrality.

**pySCENIC regulons** (https://pyscenic.readthedocs.io/en/latest/tutorial.html; https://www.nature.com/articles/s41596-020-0336-2). (1) `pyscenic grn` (GRNBoost2) scores every (TF, target) pair, about 4 min for 10k cells; (2) per TF, targets by importance (top 50 or a percentile), sign from correlation; (3) `ctx` keeps motif-supported targets, giving regulons like SPIB(+); (4) `aucell` scores cells x regulons. Tutorials show regulon activity as heat maps and UMAPs (59 regulons in the SIB course: https://sib-swiss.github.io/single-cell-python-training/ipynb/day3-1_tf.html), not as a network.

**SCENIC+ enhancer-driven GRN (eGRN).** Source: the `scenicplus.networks` module (https://scenicplus.readthedocs.io/en/stable/_modules/scenicplus/networks.html); tutorials (https://scenicplus.readthedocs.io/en/stable/tutorials.html).

1. **Subset.** `create_nx_tables(scplus_obj, subset_eRegulons=[...], subset_regions=..., subset_genes=...)` picks the eRegulons (a TF with its enhancer regions and target genes). It returns edge tables TF2R (TF to region), R2G (region to gene) and TF2G (TF to gene), and node tables for TFs, regions and genes.
2. **Add fold changes.** `add_differential_gene_expression=True`, `add_differential_region_accessibility=True`, `differential_variable=[a cell annotation]` add `<variable>_Log2FC_<group>` columns to nodes (Wilcoxon via Scanpy, Bonferroni).
3. **Build and lay out.** `create_nx_graph(use_edge_tables=['TF2R','R2G'])` maps color, size, shape and transparency of nodes and edges to columns or fixed values. The default `concentrical_layout` puts regions evenly on a circle ordered by their first target, places each gene inward from the mean of its regions and each TF near its regions, and spring-places regions that target TFs; any other value gives Kamada-Kawai.
4. **Draw or export.** `plot_networkx` draws in matplotlib (all nodes as diamonds). `export_to_cytoscape(G, pos, out_file, pos_scaling_factor=200)` writes Cytoscape JSON with fixed positions; colors, sizes and shapes are only data columns (no style file), so the user maps them in Cytoscape by hand.

**CellOracle: one GRN per cluster, compared by centrality.** Source: the network-analysis notebook (https://morris-lab.github.io/CellOracle.documentation/tutorials/networkanalysis.html; code and outputs: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/morris-lab/CellOracle/master/docs/notebooks/04_Network_analysis/Network_analysis_with_Paul_etal_2015_data.ipynb). Data: Paul et al. 2015 mouse hematopoiesis, 2,671 cells x 1,999 genes, 24 clusters; base GRN from a mouse scATAC atlas (1,095 TF columns).

1. **Prepare.** AnnData with clusters and an embedding; the base GRN, plus optional TF-target pairs from a paper; PCA; kNN imputation (k = 2.5% of cells).
2. **Build per-cluster GRNs.** `get_links(cluster_name_for_GRN_unit="louvain_annot", alpha=10)` fits a regularized regression per gene per cluster ("~30 minutes"; 24 clusters x 1,850 genes). Each table: source, target, `coef_mean`, `coef_abs`, p, -log p; cluster Ery_0 has 74,937 edges.
3. **Optional export** of each cluster's table to CSV "using another software".
4. **Filter.** `filter_links(p=0.001, weight="coef_abs", threshold_number=2000)`: the 2,000 strongest significant edges per cluster.
5. **Degree distributions**, one plot per cluster; "the degree distribution may change depending on the filtering threshold".
6. **Scores.** `get_network_score()`: per gene per cluster, degree (all, in, out), degree centrality, betweenness, eigenvector centrality.
7. **Rank.** `plot_scores_as_rank(cluster="MEP_0", n_gene=30)`.
8. **Compare two clusters.** `plot_score_comparison_2D(value="eigenvector_centrality", cluster1="MEP_0", cluster2="GMPl_0", percentile=98)`, repeated for betweenness and degree: a scatter, genes above the 98th percentile labeled.
9. **One gene across clusters.** `plot_score_per_cluster(goi="Gata2")`. "If a gene does not have network edge in a cluster, the network scores cannot be calculated" (Cebpa in erythroid clusters): missing, not zero. Then box plots of scores and network entropy per cluster.
10. **Perturb** (https://morris-lab.github.io/CellOracle.documentation/tutorials/simulation.html). `fit_GRN_for_simulation(alpha=10)`; `simulate_shift(perturb_condition={"Gata1": 0.0}, n_propagation=3)`; `estimate_transition_prob(n_neighbors=200)`; `calculate_embedding_shift(sigma_corr=0.05)`; quiver and grid vector-field plots on the cell embedding beside a randomized-GRN control, with `scale`, `n_grid=40` and `min_mass` tuned by eye, then compared with a pseudotime gradient. The output is arrows over cells; the GRN itself is never drawn.

The paper builds 24 mouse myeloid and 38 zebrafish cell-type GRNs (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9946838).

**Other multiome GRN tools.** Pando: `get_network_graph` lays genes out by a UMAP of the GRN (or ggraph `fr`, `drl`), activating edges orange and inhibitory gray, nodes sized by centrality; `plot_tf_network(tf="PAX6")` draws a tree or circle around one TF (https://quadbio.github.io/Pando/articles/visualization.html). Dictys: dot plots and heat maps across cell types, differential regulation vs differential expression for two cell types, a subnetwork per TF, and an animated GRN along a trajectory; inference "can take weeks" without a GPU (https://github.com/pinellolab/dictys). FigR: `plotfigRNetwork` draws TF -> gene links at |regulation score| >= `score.cut` (default 1; the paper used 1.5) (https://rdrr.io/github/buenrostrolab/FigR/man/plotfigRNetwork.html). scGRNom: not researched.

**Thresholds.** The CellOracle paper filters edges "on the basis of P values and absolute values of edge strength", and "the user can define a custom value ... according to the data type, data quality and aim of the analysis" (PMC9946838); no robustness test of rankings across thresholds was found in it. Tutorial values (p 0.001 and top 2,000; FigR 1; pySCENIC top 50) become de facto defaults. No paper found that reports checking GRN centrality rankings at another threshold.

**Data shapes.** (a) Bipartite signed TF -> target. (b) Tripartite TF-region-gene with a concentric layout and fold-change columns. (c) A collection of 24-38 directed, signed, weighted graphs over one gene set, compared through per-network centrality, where a gene absent from a network has no score. (d) A GRN changing along pseudotime, shown as animation (Dictys).

### 5.11 ceRNA (lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA) and miRNA-target networks

Sources: starBase v2.0 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3964941/); the GDCRNATools vignette (https://free.nchc.org.tw/bioconductor/packages/3.9/bioc/vignettes/GDCRNATools/inst/doc/GDCRNATools.html) and its source code (https://github.com/Jialab-UCR/GDCRNATools/tree/master/R); the SPONGE vignette (https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/vignettes/SPONGE/inst/doc/SPONGE.html) and paper (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6612827); a TCGA kidney example (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8186793); Denzler et al. 2014 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4267251).

**The hypothesis the network claims.** A long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) and a messenger RNA (mRNA) compete for the same microRNAs (miRNAs). If the lncRNA soaks up a shared miRNA, less of it represses the mRNA, so the lncRNA and the mRNA rise and fall together, and each moves opposite to the miRNA. GDCRNATools states it directly: "expression of the lncRNA and mRNA in a ceRNA pair should be positively correlated." A network that only joins differentially expressed (DE) molecules through database target predictions does not test any of this.

**The procedure the main tool runs (GDCRNATools).**

1. **DE per RNA class.** `gdcDEAnalysis` (limma, edgeR or DESeq2), then `gdcDEReport`, whose defaults are |fold change| >= 2 and p < 0.01 (`fc=2, pval=0.01` in the source), split into lncRNA and protein-coding lists.
2. **Targets.** lncRNA-miRNA and miRNA-mRNA pairs from starBase by default (miRcode, miRTarBase, miRDB and TargetScan are common alternatives).
3. **Test every candidate lncRNA-mRNA pair** with `gdcCEAnalysis`, which runs three steps:
    - a hypergeometric test that the pair shares more miRNAs than chance (`hyperPValue`, not corrected for multiple testing in the code);
    - a one-sided Pearson test that the lncRNA and mRNA are positively correlated (`cor`, `corPValue`; `alternative='greater'` in the source);
    - regulation similarity (`regSim`): how alike each shared miRNA's correlation with the lncRNA and with the mRNA is (each one is computed as a one-sided test for a negative correlation). A sensitivity partial correlation (`sppc`, after Paci et al. 2014) is also returned: how much of the lncRNA-mRNA correlation the shared miRNAs explain.
      Each output row also carries the shared miRNA list, their count and the fold enrichment.
4. **Filter on several statistics at once.** The worked example keeps rows with `hyperPValue < 0.01 & corPValue < 0.01 & regSim != 0`.
5. **Export** with `gdcExportNetwork`: an edge table (`fromNode`, `toNode`, `altNode1Name`: each lncRNA or mRNA joined to each shared miRNA) and a node table (`gene`, `symbol`, `type` = `lnc`, `pc` or `mir`, `numInteractions`). The vignette says the edges are imported as the network and the nodes as a feature table in Cytoscape. The exported edges carry no statistics: the p-values and correlations stay on the pair table, and an analyst who wants them on the drawing joins them back by hand. Fold change per node has to be joined from the DE table the same way.
6. **Draw.** One shape per RNA class, red up and blue down.

**Another tool, SPONGE.** It keeps only miRNA-gene links with a negative coefficient in an elastic-net regression (default threshold -0.05), scores each gene pair by multiple sensitivity correlation over all shared miRNAs, takes p-values from simulated null models and corrects them for multiple testing; its vignette suggests a cutoff such as FDR < 0.01 (the toy code uses 0.2). It draws with visNetwork and computes node centralities, optionally weighted by -log10 adjusted p.

**What published papers do.** The TCGA kidney example (1,457 lncRNAs, 54 miRNAs, 2,307 mRNAs at |log2FC| > 2, P < 0.01; targets from miRcode and from all three of miRTarBase, miRDB and TargetScan; 107 nodes and 178 edges, diamond lncRNA, rectangle miRNA, ellipse mRNA) describes no correlation or hypergeometric test: its edges come from DE intersection alone. No source found counts what fraction of TCGA ceRNA papers report a statistical filter; papers of both kinds are easy to find.

**Why a careful analyst checks more.** Denzler et al. 2014 found miR-122 repression was released only above about 150,000 added target sites per cell and concluded that "modulation of miRNA target abundance is unlikely to cause significant effects on gene expression." Others dispute how general that is, but it means a drawn ceRNA edge is a hypothesis whose support is its statistics. The careful analyst keeps the statistics on each edge, filters on them, and checks that the signs agree across each lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA triplet.

**Data shape.** Tripartite typed nodes (lncRNA, miRNA, mRNA) with fold change and direction per node; miRNA edges carrying target source and correlation; a lncRNA-mRNA pair layer carrying hypergeometric p, correlation and its p, regulation similarity and sensitivity correlation.

### 5.12 Genetic interaction networks and layout-based annotation (adjacent)

Sources: the yeast global genetic interaction network, Costanzo et al. 2016 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5661885); TheCellMap.org, Usaj et al. 2017 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5427489); SAFE, Baryshnikova 2016 (Cell Systems, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cels.2016.04.014; protocol preprint https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/094904.full.pdf); safepy, the maintained Python implementation, its defaults file (https://raw.githubusercontent.com/baryshnikova-lab/safepy/master/safepy/safe_default.ini), its code (https://github.com/baryshnikova-lab/safepy/blob/master/safepy/safe.py) and example notebooks (https://github.com/baryshnikova-lab/safepy/tree/master/examples); the Cytoscape SAFE app (https://apps.cytoscape.org/apps/safe).

The yeast genetic interaction network is read as a profile-similarity network: genes are joined when their interaction profiles correlate at PCC >= 0.2, giving 4,909 nodes (4,418 genes) and 34,468 edges, drawn with a spring-embedded layout (PMC5427489). Genes with similar profiles sit together, so the layout carries the meaning. SAFE (spatial analysis of functional enrichment) turns the drawing into a functional map. Its steps and safepy's defaults:

1. **Lay out the network** in 2D. A network loaded from a text edge list gets a spring layout (NetworkX `spring_layout`, k = 0.2, 100 iterations, optional seed); Kamada-Kawai is the other option; a Cytoscape session keeps its own coordinates (safe_io.py). Each edge's length is its length on the drawing. The defaults file leaves `randomSeed` blank.
2. **Define each node's neighborhood.** `nodeDistanceType` is `shortpath_weighted_layout` (shortest path where each edge counts its drawn length; default), `euclidean` (straight-line distance on the drawing, used for edgeless scatter plots such as a UMAP) or `shortpath` (hop count). `neighborhoodRadius` is 0.1 with `neighborhoodRadiusType = diameter`; in code the radius is 0.1 times the x-extent of the layout, not the graph diameter in hops. On the 2016 network this gave 37.5 +/- 56.7 nodes per neighborhood. The example notebooks change it: 0.15 for a GO run, 0.06 for a scatter plot.
3. **Load attributes.** A table with nodes as rows and attributes as columns. Binary 0/1 columns (GO terms) or quantitative columns (a chemical-genomic sensitivity score per mutant). The default attribute file is a quantitative doxorubicin screen (Hoepfner et al. 2014), not GO. `background` is `attribute_file` (only nodes with a value count) or `network` (missing values set to 0); safepy warns when over half the nodes have no value.
4. **Test each neighborhood.** `enrichmentType = auto` picks the hypergeometric test when every value is 0 or 1, and otherwise a permutation test: neighborhood scores (sum by default) compared against 1,000 shuffles of node values. `annotationsign` is `both` (default), `highest` or `lowest`: for quantitative data, whether to map neighborhoods enriched for high values, low values, or both, the last as a signed score. P-values become an enrichment score, -log10 p, capped at 16. A neighborhood counts as enriched at p < 0.05 (`THRESHOLD_ENRICHMENT`). FDR correction (Benjamini-Hochberg, per node across attributes) exists but is off by default in safepy (`multiple_testing = False`), so a tool must record whether it was used.
5. **Keep region-specific attributes.** An attribute is kept ("top") only if at least 10 neighborhoods are enriched (`groupEnrichmentMinSize`) and, with `unimodalityType = connectivity`, its enriched nodes form one connected piece of the network. Attributes enriched in scattered places are dropped.
6. **Group attributes into domains.** Kept attributes are clustered by the Jaccard distance between their sets of enriched nodes (average linkage) and cut at `groupDistanceThreshold` 0.75 of the tallest merge; the GO example uses 0.65. Each node joins the domain holding most of its enriched attributes; domains that are the top choice for fewer than 10 nodes are removed. Each domain is labeled from the words of its attribute names.
7. **Draw.** One attribute at a time: nodes colored by signed enrichment on a two-color scale (safepy default blue `#82add6` for low, yellow `#facb66` for high), optionally the raw values or only significant nodes, with chosen genes labeled. All domains at once: each node colored by its domain, domain numbers and network contour shown. Outputs: tables of attribute, node and domain properties, and a PDF.

What one run produced: on the 2016 network, 569 GO biological process terms fell into 19 regions covering 1,480 genes (PMC5427489). That is a result, not a fixed number: the safepy GO example on the 2010 network gave 407 kept terms in 64 domains, trimmed by 36. The Cytoscape app (1.0.0.beta7, February 2017, Cytoscape 3.3) runs the same steps with attribute and domain tables filterable by the current selection, a red-blue high-low legend, a domain report and export of enrichment scores. Quantitative use: SAFE on chemical-genomic profiles recovered drug modes of action, linking vesicle-mediated transport to bortezomib resistance (Cell Systems abstract, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27237738/).

**Reading a gene on TheCellMap** (PMC5427489): type a systematic or common name in the search box; a red marker shows its place on the global map, whose clusters are colored by SAFE process labels (BNI1 sits in "Cell Polarity"). A gene with no partner at PCC >= 0.2 returns a link to its most similar profiles instead. "Extract Subnetwork" shows the gene's direct neighbors with a PCC slider (default 0.20). "Annotate Network" recolors by another standard (GO Slim, protein complexes, localization) or an uploaded one. The "Genetic Interaction Network" view shows direct negative (blue) and positive (yellow) interactions at P < 0.05 and |score| > 0.08, adjustable. "View as Table" and "Download" export an Excel file; a camera icon saves the view as SVG.

**Human counterpart.** DepMap co-essentiality networks (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33859415/) are usually clustered and then annotated, not mapped with SAFE: Kim et al. 2019 built a 3,483-node, 68,813-edge network from 276 CRISPR screens and annotated MCL clusters against GO, KEGG and Reactome (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6464042). No published SAFE run on a DepMap network was found. Co-essentiality carries a known mitochondrial bias (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10632734).

For a tool: SAFE needs the layout's coordinates as an input, so the layout, its seed and every SAFE parameter (distance type, radius, test, sign, background, threshold, correction, minimum size, grouping threshold) must be recorded with the map.

### 5.13 Mass-spectrometry molecular networking (GNPS), from job to figure

Sources: GNPS networking docs (https://ccms-ucsd.github.io/GNPSDocumentation/networking/); in-browser views (https://ccms-ucsd.github.io/GNPSDocumentation/networkingviews); FBMN in Cytoscape (https://ccms-ucsd.github.io/GNPSDocumentation/featurebasedmolecularnetworking-cytoscape/); NAP (https://ccms-ucsd.github.io/GNPSDocumentation/nap/); MolNetEnhancer (https://ccms-ucsd.github.io/GNPSDocumentation/molnetenhancer/); PMC8705952; PMC7885687.

1. **Convert** vendor files to mzML (ProteoWizard).
2. **Detect features (feature-based only).** MZmine or another supported tool exports a feature x file quantification table and an MGF of spectra. Classical networking splits one compound into many nodes: an EDTA test gave 13 duplicates in one family (PMC7885687).
3. **Submit** with groups G1-G6. Defaults: precursor 2.0 Da and fragment 0.5 Da tolerance; Min Pairs Cos 0.7; 6 matched peaks; Node TopK 10 (an edge is kept "only if both nodes are within each other's 'TopK' most similar nodes"); Maximum Connected Component Size 100 (larger components are split "by increasing cosine threshold"). Runs take about 10 minutes to several hours.
4. **Read in the browser.** "View Spectral Families" lists components; the user opens one: library hits blue; edge labels by cosine or delta m/z; pies per group; a typed delta m/z turns matching edges red. GNPS2 adds "Visualize Full Network w/ Singletons" (https://wang-bioinformatics-lab.github.io/GNPS2_Documentation/faq/).
5. **Propagate annotations.** NAP re-ranks candidate structures for unknowns from annotated neighbors, "limited by the connected component"; MolNetEnhancer gives each family its most frequent chemical class (`CF_class`).
6. **Figure in Cytoscape.** Import GraphML and the library table; size = `SumPeakIntensity`, pies from `_GNPSGROUP__` columns, edge width = `cosine_score`, fill by class; chemViz2 draws structures from `Smiles`. Share the job URL.

The Cytoscape half of this job is written out step by step as Workflow 9 of network-medicine-and-biomedical-knowledge-graphs.md (table join, per-job restyling, chemViz2 structures, isolating families with "First Neighbors of Selected Nodes", per-condition subnetworks, PDF and .cys export; one network had 810 sub-families: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10040239). Both are one Designloom record, W253, owned by `molecular-networking-chemist`; the medicinal chemist only opens networks a collaborator shares.

Families are capped at 100 nodes; singletons hidden by default; no source for a typical per-job node count, but one user's 55,000-node network triggered Cytoscape's "This could take a long time" prompt and was sent back to the GNPS browser (https://groups.google.com/g/molecular_networking_bug_reports/c/Gmz4qXuQGiI). For a tool: pack many small components; a component list to rank and open; structures and pies as glyphs; edges highlighted by mass difference; building parameters shown with the graph.

### 5.14 Single-cell kNN graph, Leiden clusters and PAGA

Sources: Scanpy tutorial (https://scanpy.readthedocs.io/en/stable/tutorials/basics/clustering.html); best practices, integration (https://www.sc-best-practices.org/cellular-structure/integration/) and annotation (https://www.sc-best-practices.org/cellular-structure/annotation/); Seurat integration (https://satijalab.org/seurat/articles/integration_introduction); scIB benchmark (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8748196); `scanpy.pp.neighbors` (https://scanpy.readthedocs.io/en/stable/api/generated/scanpy.pp.neighbors.html); best practices (https://www.sc-best-practices.org/cellular-structure/clustering/); Seurat PBMC 3k (https://satijalab.org/seurat/articles/pbmc3k_tutorial); PAGA (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6425583); SPRING (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6030950); clustree (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6057528).

1. **Matrix.** Cells x genes in AnnData (.h5ad) or Seurat; QC, normalize, 2,000 variable genes, PCA. With several samples, the sample is passed as `batch_key` to variable-gene selection and doublet detection (Scanpy tutorial).
2. **Integrate batches (multi-sample data, the usual case for atlases).** Color the unintegrated UMAP by sample; if samples separate, integrate. The method decides which graph exists: scVI and scANVI give a latent space and neighbors are built on it (`sc.pp.neighbors(adata, use_rep="X_scVI")`); Seurat CCA gives a corrected representation (`IntegrateLayers(..., new.reduction = "integrated.cca")`, then `FindNeighbors(reduction = "integrated.cca", dims = 1:30)`); BBKNN skips `sc.pp.neighbors` and writes the integrated kNN graph itself, enforcing "that cells are connected to cells from other batches". The Scanpy clustering tutorial saw "a minor batch effect" in two samples and did not integrate.
3. **Judge the integration on the graph.** Each method's result is a UMAP colored by batch and by cell type; best practices warns "It is tempting to select an integration based on the UMAPs, but this does not fully represent the quality of an integration", and asks for confirmation "by inspecting the distribution of marker genes". The scIB benchmark scores integrations with metrics computed on the kNN graph: kNN graph connectivity (are all cells of one label connected), graph iLISI (how many batches appear in each cell's neighborhood) and graph cLISI (how few labels do), plus kBET; scIB extended kBET and LISI so they "work on graph-based outputs" (PMC8748196, https://scib.readthedocs.io/en/latest/).
4. **Neighbors.** `sc.pp.neighbors(adata)`: `n_neighbors=15`, Euclidean in PCA space or on the integrated representation; sparse cells x cells `obsp['connectivities']`, parameters in `uns['neighbors']`. Seurat `FindNeighbors(dims = 1:10)` reweights by shared neighbors.
5. **Cluster.** Leiden or Louvain at several resolutions compared on the UMAP by eye (Scanpy tries 0.02, 0.5, 2.0; Seurat 0.4-1.2). Stability via clustering trees (a layer per resolution, edges show cells moving between clusters).
6. **Annotate cell types.** Manual (Scanpy tutorial and best practices): a dot plot of known marker genes per cluster (`sc.pl.dotplot(adata, marker_genes, groupby="leiden_res_0.50")`, each gene scaled 0 to 1), UMAPs colored by one marker each (clipped at the 99th percentile), then `rank_genes_groups` with a Wilcoxon test for markers per cluster (cluster 7 expressing NKG7 and GNLY suggests NK cells); best practices annotates clusters, not cells, because markers are sparse. Coarse labels come from a low resolution, finer ones from a higher one. Automatic: CellTypist (13,668 cells, coarse and fine immune models, majority voting and a confidence score) or label transfer from a reference with scArches; "it is very important to check the automated annotation manually". Seurat's conditions tutorial uses `FindConservedMarkers` and a `DotPlot` split by condition. Labels become a cell column and are checked again against batch: a cluster made of one sample is a batch artifact or a sample-specific state.
7. **Embed.** UMAP or ForceAtlas2; a scatter, edges not drawn; best practices advises "not interpreting distances between clusters visualized on UMAP embeddings".
8. **Abstract with PAGA.** One node per cluster, edges weighted by observed over expected connections; seeds UMAP via `init_pos='paga'`. The paper spans 1,654 to 1.3 million cells.
9. **Interactive tools.** SPRING runs a live force layout "configured for datasets up to 10 000 cells". CELLxGENE Explorer plots an embedding of a million cells (https://github.com/chanzuckerberg/single-cell-explorer); no edge view found.

Scale at atlas level: the integrated Human Lung Cell Atlas core holds 584,444 cells from 107 people, integrated with scANVI, and the extended atlas 2.4 million cells from 486 people (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10287567/); CZ CELLxGENE Discover held 169.3 million cells (93.6 million unique) in more than 1,550 datasets on 2024-10-01 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11701654). The tools that show these are embedding viewers; no edge view of an atlas graph was found.

Data shape: 10^4 to 10^6 nodes of near-uniform degree as a sparse matrix with a node table (sample, batch, condition, cluster at each resolution, cell-type label and its confidence, marker expression) and recorded parameters, including the representation the graph was built on, plus a coarse cluster graph. What the product must show beside the graph: batch mixing (cells or edges colored by batch, the batch make-up of each cluster, per-cell neighborhood batch diversity) and marker expression per cluster (a dot plot linked to the cluster map). The same vectors-to-kNN build recurs in bibliometrics-and-science-mapping.md and code-first-data-scientists-and-graph-ml.md; it belongs in data-preparation-and-import.md.

### 5.15 Residue interaction networks from protein structures and simulations (adjacent)

Sources: RING 4.0 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11223866); RING 2.0 (https://www.pubmed.gov/27198219/); RINalyzer (https://apps.cytoscape.org/apps/rinalyzer); structureViz2 (https://rbvi.ucsf.edu/cytoscape/structureViz2/); Doncheva et al. 2012 protocol (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22422314/); dynamical network tutorial (https://luthey-schulten.chemistry.illinois.edu/network/network_tutorial.pdf); Bio3D `cna` (https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/bio3d/html/cna.html).

Each amino acid (or nucleotide part) is a node with 3D coordinates; each typed contact is an edge.

**Web route (RING).** (1) Upload PDB or mmCIF, or type a PDB or AlphaFold DB id; all chains and models by default; up to 200 MB, larger ensembles run locally. (2) Adjust thresholds. RING 2.0 users report "strict" thresholds of hydrogen bond 3.5 A (donor-acceptor), van der Waals 0.5 A (between atom surfaces), ionic 4.0 A, pi-pi 6.5 A, pi-cation 5.0 A, with "closest" atom distance and "multiple" (one edge per interaction type per residue pair) as defaults (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.01607); RING 4.0 redefined most types and added pi-hydrogen, halogen and metal coordination. (3) Run: 3 to 9 s for the paper's examples. (4) Explore: a Mol* 3D view beside a network showing all types at once; a residue selected in one view is highlighted in all; filter by type, intra- or inter-chain, or frequency; color by chain, type, sequence position, secondary structure or degree; multi-model input adds per-state and probability contact maps. (5) Export JSON, SVG, tables. RING 2.0 output went to Cytoscape with RINalyzer and structureViz.

**Desktop route (Cytoscape, structureViz2, UCSF Chimera, RINalyzer).** (1) Open the structure in Chimera from Cytoscape, select residues, run "Residue Network Generation" with chosen interaction types; nodes styled by secondary structure, edges by subtype. (2) Selections stay synchronized between network and structure. (3) RINalyzer computes weighted centralities "for discovering critical residues and their long-range interaction paths" and combines aligned structures into one network to compare proteins. Last release 2014; 9,568 downloads.

**Dynamics route (VMD NetworkView; about 2 hours).** Tutorial system: the GluRS enzyme bound to tRNA(Glu). (1) One node per amino acid at its C-alpha, two per nucleotide, three for the ligand. (2) Edges where residues stay within 4.5 A for at least 75% of the trajectory, excluding same-residue and sequence-neighbor pairs. (3) Edge length d_ij = -log(|C_ij|), C_ij the correlation of the atoms' fluctuations (from Carma). (4) Draw the network inside the molecule in 3D, edge width by weight. (5) Girvan-Newman communities, "sets of residues that move in concert"; critical nodes between communities in red. (6) Suboptimal paths between two nodes (anticodon base U35 to A76) within a length offset of 20; optimal path red, others blue. One user reports a couple of hundred paths, hard to interpret (https://tcbg.illinois.edu/Research/vmd/mailing_list/vmd-l/30991.html). Bio3D does the same in R: `cna(cij, cutoff.cij=0.35)`, edge-betweenness communities, `plot.cna`.

**Population.** Citations (OpenAlex, read 2026-11-25): RING 2.0, 437; Doncheva et al. 2011 review (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tibs.2011.01.002), 274. Small next to omics users.

**For a tool.** A typed multigraph with fixed 3D node positions and per-edge ensemble frequencies, analyzed by weighted paths, betweenness and communities, drawn inside the molecule (NetworkView) or linked to a structure viewer (RING, Chimera). One node per residue, hundreds to thousands; typical edge counts and whether users prefer the in-molecule view: no source found.

### 5.16 Immune repertoire similarity network to public and disease-associated clusters

Sources: NAIR paper (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10443597/); NAIR repository (https://github.com/mlizhangx/Network-Analysis-for-Repertoire-Sequencing-); Miho et al. 2019 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6428871); ClustIRR (https://bioconductor.org/packages/ClustIRR); AIRR Rearrangement schema (https://docs.airr-community.org/en/stable/datarep/rearrangements.html).

1. **Load.** One AIRR TSV per sample plus a sample table (subject, time point, disease status). NAIR's COVID-19 case study: 108 samples from 76 subjects, 901,045 unique TCR beta clones, one clone per unique CDR3 amino acid sequence.
2. **Build one network per sample.** `buildRepSeqNetwork()` links clones whose CDR3 differs by at most one amino acid (Hamming distance; Levenshtein optional), returns an igraph object, an adjacency matrix and node and cluster tables, and plots nodes colored by sample, cluster or abundance. Clusters by igraph fast greedy; a cutoff of 2 gave fewer, larger clusters, and Leiden was an outlier among the methods compared. Cost on a laptop: 2 s to 35 min and 50-1,700 MB for 1,000-50,000 clones; Levenshtein up to 25 times longer.
3. **Find public clusters.** Keep each sample's top K largest clusters plus single clones with count above 100; take the most abundant clone of each as its representative; network the representatives; clusters with clones from different samples form a skeleton, which is expanded back to all member clones. Result: 1,594 public clusters shared by at least two samples, 170 by more than two people.
4. **Find disease-associated clusters.** Fisher's exact test (p < 0.05) on sequences shared by at least 10 samples; group the significant ones with their neighbors; build a network of all of them. Result: 135 COVID-associated clusters holding 10,416 TCRs, 30 of them seen only in COVID samples.
5. **Read and validate.** Cluster properties as heat maps with dendrograms, network properties as box plots by disease status, sequence logos per cluster, exact matches against a database of TCRs of known specificity (MIRA).
6. **ClustIRR route.** Similarities within each repertoire, one graph per repertoire, a joint graph across repertoires, Leiden or Louvain communities, then a Bayesian model of differential community occupancy between conditions.

Published figures are network plots of selected clusters, not of the whole repertoire. Miho et al. drew networks of more than 500,000 nodes in Gephi, built human networks of up to 6 million clones, and needed 625 cores for 15 minutes to compute a 1.6-million-node network. For a tool: load per-sample networks and keep the sample as a node attribute; color and filter by how many samples and subjects share a cluster; compare one cluster's membership across samples; draw 10^5-10^6 clones where every component is small.

### 5.17 Crop co-expression hub genes with release-specific ids and borrowed annotation

Sources: the plant studies in 3.10 (PMC10326901, PMC10406934, PMC12636891); agriGO v2.0 as used in PMC10326901; PlantTFDB 4.0 and PlantRegMap (PMC5210657); STRING v12.0 species list and proteome upload (PMC9825434); Ensembl Plants maize annotation page.

1. **Reference and ids (decided before any network).** Map reads to a reference genome and annotation release (wheat IWGSC RefSeq v1.0 assembly with v1.1 gene models; Saccharum spontaneum AP85-441) or assemble de novo when there is none (sugarcane `Unigene` ids). Gene ids encode the release (`TraesCS...01G` vs `...02G`; `Zm00001d` vs `Zm00001eb`); ids from older papers or tools must be converted, for example through the Ensembl Plants ID History Converter.
2. **Co-expression network (as 5.7).** Filter genes (top 75% by median absolute deviation; zero-heavy genes removed), pick the power (10, 16), run `blockwiseModules`; 20 to 81 modules of 30 to 19,380 genes.
3. **Relate modules to traits** such as drought tolerance or tiller number; pick the trait modules.
4. **Pick hubs.** Rules differ: the five highest-degree genes after keeping the top 100 edges (PMC10406934); genes with more connections among edges above weight 0.25 (PMC12636891); top degree from Cytoscape's Network Analyzer, replaced by the highest-scoring differentially expressed gene when the top genes are not differentially expressed (PMC10326901).
5. **Annotate by orthology.** Transfer GO terms across annotation releases by sequence identity (">99% identical across >90% of the sequence"), or from Arabidopsis and rice orthologs, or by BLAST and DIAMOND against UniProt, KEGG, Nr, Pfam and eggNOG; run enrichment in agriGO or PlantRegMap with the filtered gene set as background.
6. **Optional interaction layer.** For species STRING covers (rice, maize, wheat, soybean as "core"; barley, sorghum, cotton as "periphery"), query STRING directly; otherwise query the Arabidopsis orthologs and map back, or upload the proteome to STRING. Many studies skip this and draw only co-expression edges.
7. **Draw and export.** Export the module with `exportNetworkToCytoscape`; size nodes by degree; name hubs by gene id plus the Arabidopsis ortholog symbol; modules of about 2,000 genes or more stay in R because Cytoscape struggles to load them.

For a tool: the node label is often an ortholog's name, not the gene's own id, so the id, the release it belongs to and the borrowed annotation (with the share of nodes that have one) must travel together; enrichment needs plant gene sets and a recorded background; a module may have 10^4 genes.

### 5.18 Spatial neighbor graph, cell-type neighborhood enrichment and cellular neighborhoods (W2066)

Sources: `squidpy.gr.spatial_neighbors` (https://squidpy.readthedocs.io/en/stable/api/squidpy.gr.spatial_neighbors.html); `squidpy.gr.nhood_enrichment` (https://squidpy.readthedocs.io/en/stable/api/squidpy.gr.nhood_enrichment.html); Squidpy's spatial-graph guide (https://squidpy.readthedocs.io/en/stable/notebooks/examples/graph/compute_spatial_neighbors.html) and IMC tutorial (https://squidpy.readthedocs.io/en/stable/notebooks/tutorials/tutorial_imc.html); Squidpy's niche module (https://squidpy.readthedocs.io/en/latest/_modules/squidpy/gr/_niche.html); Giotto spatial networks (https://giottosuite.com/articles/networks.html) and `cellProximityEnrichment` (https://giottosuite.readthedocs.io/en/latest/subsections/md_rst/cellProximityEnrichment.html); Schurch et al. 2020 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7479520/); the histoCAT neighborhood test as reimplemented in neighbouRhood (https://github.com/BodenmillerGroup/neighbouRhood); 10x Visium HD and Xenium dataset pages.

1. **Cells with positions and labels.** A cells x genes (or cells x markers) table, each cell's centroid, and a cell-type label from clustering (5.14) or marker gating. Visium HD has no cells by default: a 6.5 x 6.5 mm capture area holds about 11 million 2 um squares, analyzed as 8 um bins of 16 squares each or as nucleus-segmented cells (https://www.10xgenomics.com/support/jp/software/loupe-browser/latest/analysis/assay-analysis/lb-hd-spatial-gene-expression). Xenium sections hold 10^5 to 10^6 cells (295,883 healthy and 531,165 tumor lung cells: https://www.10xgenomics.com/datasets/xenium-human-lung-preview-data-1-standard). The tissue image (H&E, DAPI or a multichannel stack) comes with it.
2. **Build the spatial graph, one per section.** Squidpy `sq.gr.spatial_neighbors(adata)`: `coord_type` is `grid` for Visium spots (`n_neighs=6`, `n_rings=1`) and `generic` otherwise, where the default is 6 nearest neighbors, `delaunay=True` gives a Delaunay triangulation and `radius=r` a fixed radius; `percentile` trims the longest edges. It writes `obsp['spatial_connectivities']` and `obsp['spatial_distances']`; `library_key` keeps sections apart. The function is deprecated in Squidpy 1.7 in favor of one function per graph rule. Giotto `createSpatialNetwork` defaults to Delaunay, with edges longer than the upper whisker of the neighbor-distance box plot removed; its kNN option has no distance cutoff by default. histoCAT counts cells within a few pixels of each other's boundary as neighbors. Voyager's Xenium vignette uses k = 5.
3. **Check the graph by eye.** Squidpy's guide plots one cell and its neighbors under each rule with `sq.pl.spatial_scatter(..., connectivity_key="spatial_connectivities")`; Giotto draws the whole network over the cells (`spatPlot2D(show_network = TRUE, network_color = "grey")`). The guide's radius example (`radius=0.3`) produces a graph with no edges, which only shows up in the matrix printout.
4. **Cell-type pair enrichment.** `sq.gr.nhood_enrichment(adata, cluster_key="cell type")`: counts edges between each pair of labels and compares them with 1,000 label permutations (`n_perms=1000`, seedable with `rng`), giving a z-score and a count per pair in `uns['cell type_nhood_enrichment']`; plotted as a cell-type x cell-type heat map. Giotto `cellProximityEnrichment` does the same with 1,000 label shuffles on the Delaunay network. histoCAT reports, per image, which pairs are significantly neighbored or avoided. Squidpy adds `interaction_matrix` (raw edge counts per pair), `centrality_scores` (degree, closeness and average clustering per cell type on the spatial graph) and `co_occurrence` (ratio by distance).
5. **Cellular neighborhoods.** Schurch et al. took, for each of 258,385 CODEX cells, a window of "the 10 nearest neighboring cells (including the center cell)" by Euclidean distance, counted the 28 cell types in it, and clustered the windows with "MiniBatchKMeans with k = 10"; one of the 10 clusters was mostly imaging artifacts, leaving 9 neighborhoods. Squidpy's `calculate_niche(flavor="neighborhood")` does the graph version: per-cell label fractions over neighbors within `distance` hops (default 1), z-scored, then Leiden on a kNN graph of those profiles (`n_neighbors=15`) at the chosen resolutions.
6. **Draw on the tissue.** Neighborhoods painted on cells as Voronoi tiles over the tissue and checked against the fluorescence and H&E images (Schurch et al.); a neighborhood x cell-type composition heat map; neighborhood frequency compared between patient groups or against survival.

Data shape: 10^5 to 10^6 nodes per section at fixed 2D coordinates, 5 to 10 edges each, many sections per study kept as separate graphs, a large raster image per section in the same coordinate frame (pixel size in micrometers), and a node table with cell type, neighborhood and expression. What the product must show: the graph over the image at full extent and zoomed to single cells; the cell-type pair statistics linked back to the cells and edges that produced them; the graph rule and its parameters recorded beside every statistic.

### 5.19 Metabolomics networks without a flux model: knowledge-driven (W3102) and DSPC partial correlation (W3103)

Sources: MetaboAnalyst Network Explorer Tutorial I, knowledge-driven (Chong and Xia 2018: https://api2.xialab.ca/api/download/metaboanalyst/tutorial8.pdf); Tutorial II, DSPC (Chang and Xia 2020: https://api2.xialab.ca/api/download/metaboanalyst/tutorial9.pdf); the MetaboAnalyst 5.0 network tutorial (2022: https://api2.xialab.ca/api/download/metaboanalyst/6_Network_Explorer.pdf); tutorial index (https://metaboanalyst.ca/docs/Tutorials.xhtml); MetaboAnalyst 4.0 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6030889) and 5.0 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8265181); DSPC (Basu et al. 2017: https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/btx012).

**Knowledge-driven (Tutorial I, W3102).**

1. **Upload.** Paste a "Metabolite List" (compound names, HMDB ids or KEGG compound ids) and, for multi-omics, a "Gene List" (Entrez, Ensembl, official symbols or KEGG orthologs). Fold changes are optional (5.0 tutorial).
2. **Review the mapping.** One mapping table per list, downloadable; the user fixes unmatched names before going on.
3. **Choose a network.** Five types: the KEGG global metabolic network; gene-metabolite and metabolite-metabolite networks built from "only highly confident interactions" in STITCH, mostly co-mentions in PubMed abstracts; a metabolite-disease network from HMDB; and the metabolite-gene-disease network that integrates them. All but KEGG "are applicable to human studies only".
4. **KEGG global map.** Metabolites are nodes and enzymes are edges on a fixed map; pathway analysis of the uploaded compounds is listed beside it; ticking a pathway highlights its hits, and a metabolite shared by several ticked pathways (glycine in the example) is drawn larger. Download as PNG or SVG.
5. **Seed subnetworks.** For the interaction networks, the mapped inputs ("seeds") plus their direct neighbors form first-order subnetworks, which "often produces one big subnetwork"; those with at least three nodes are listed, can each be opened, and can be downloaded as SIF for Cytoscape.
6. **Read and reduce.** Metabolites are diamonds, genes circles and diseases squares; node size follows degree and color follows betweenness. The Node Explorer sorts by degree or betweenness; selected nodes can be deleted (which re-arranges the network and can leave orphans) or extracted as a new module that joins the network drop-down. Six alignment options re-lay the nodes; selection modes cover a single node, a node and its neighbors, all highlights, or the current pathway.
7. **Enrich.** The Function Explorer runs hypergeometric enrichment (KEGG for metabolites and genes; GO or Reactome for genes) on all, up-regulated, down-regulated or highlighted nodes, and highlights a chosen pathway's members.
8. **Trace and select.** The Path Explorer finds the shortest paths between two nodes (two paths of four nodes between D-glucose and SSR in the example); Batch Selection highlights a pasted list of names.
9. **Export.** PNG, SVG or GraphML. The tutorial advises "limiting the total number of nodes to between 200 - 2000"; labels appear only when a node is enlarged by double-clicking.

I found no documented degree or betweenness threshold filter and no automatic module detection in these tutorials: reduction is by sorting, deleting and extracting, and modules are user-selected. The 5.0 tutorial mentions "network tools" to refine networks without describing them.

**Data-driven DSPC (Tutorial II, W3103).** The input is a concentration table (samples in rows or columns; compound names, HMDB or KEGG ids, or unknowns), or a Metabolomics Workbench study id.

1. **Integrity check.** Missing values are reported (0.9% in the example) and replaced by half the minimum positive value by default.
2. **Normalize.** The example uses no sample normalization, log transformation and auto-scaling, checked on before-and-after density and box plots.
3. **Compute DSPC.** Debiased sparse partial correlation, a graphical LASSO model, gives a coefficient and a p-value for every pair of features. Its "main assumption" is that the real network is sparse relative to the sample size, so it works with fewer samples than features.
4. **Filter.** Correlation filters I and II keep edges under a p-value threshold or within a coefficient range. "The default network only shows top edges based on P-value ranking (top 20% when the total number of edges is less than 1000; top 100 edges when the total number of edges is over 1000)". Subnetworks of three or more nodes are listed and downloadable as SIF.
5. **Explore.** Edge thickness shows strength; an advanced option colors positive edges red and negative blue; compounds not found in the database show as "unmapped"; enrichment runs on selected nodes. DSPC is also available from the Statistical Analysis module (5.0 paper), and the analysis ends in a PDF report.

Sizes: the tutorial datasets are plasma amino acids from 240 samples, and 151 metabolites in 1,020 KORA serum samples, where DSPC grouped phosphatidylcholines together. Basu et al. built networks of 192 metabolites from 71 mouse samples and 140 metabolites in the SWAN study, cutting edges at adjusted p < 0.05 (or p < 0.003 with FDR < 0.5 for one figure). No source gave edge counts before and after the default cut.

Data shape: the knowledge-driven network is heterogeneous (three node types, edges from three databases) and seeded; the DSPC network is complete before filtering, signed and weighted, so the threshold decides what is drawn. Neither is a curated bipartite map, which is why they are separate from W250.

### 6.2 Sizes

- Gene-list networks: 16 to 600 query genes (PMC9904289); a stringApp example has 847 nodes and 3,904 edges at 0.8, "hundreds of thousands of edges" at 0.4.
- Co-expression: 3,600 to 50,000 genes before restriction; exported modules of tens to hundreds of genes.
- Cell-cell communication: 10-30 group nodes x hundreds of layers. Regulons: tens. CellOracle: 24 per-cluster GRNs over about 1,850 genes, 74,937 raw edges in one cluster, 2,000 after filtering.
- Single-cell kNN graphs: 10^4 to 10^6 cells at about 15 edges each; PAGA graphs: tens of nodes.
- Molecular networks: per-job count not found; components capped at 100 nodes, plus singletons.
- ceRNA: about 100 nodes, 200 edges. Metabolic: thousands of reactions per model; maps of tens to hundreds.
- Metabolomics networks (5.19): knowledge-driven networks advised at 200-2,000 nodes; DSPC networks of tens to about 200 features (151 metabolites in KORA, 192 in a mouse study), with the top 20% of edges, or the top 100 above 1,000, drawn by default.
- Genetic interaction similarity: 4,909 nodes, 34,468 edges. Interactomes: BioPlex 118,162 edges; BioGRID 2.36 M. SIGNOR: 33,000 relations.
- Residue networks: one node per residue, hundreds to thousands; edge counts not found.
- Spatial cell graphs: 10^5 to 10^6 cells per section (Xenium lung 295,883 and 531,165; CODEX 258,385 over 140 regions), 5 to 10 neighbors each; a Visium HD capture area has about 11 million 2 um squares before binning (5.18).
- Immune repertoires: 1,000-50,000 clones per sample (NAIR), 901,045 across one 108-sample cohort; up to 6 million clones per human network and about 1 million edges in a mouse naive B-cell network at distance 1 (Miho et al.).

### 6.3 Identifiers

HGNC symbols, Entrez, Ensembl, UniProt, plant gene ids tied to a genome release (wheat `TraesCS4D01G207500`, maize `Zm00001eb...`, sorghum `Sobic...`, sugarcane `Sspon...`) and de novo transcript ids (`Unigene21939`, `MSTRG.102651`), STRING ids (taxon.ENSP), KEGG ids (hsa:7157), BiGG ids, miRBase ids, ChEBI/PubChem, SMILES and InChI, GNPS scan or feature ids, cell barcodes, site ids (SYMBOL_SITE), CDR3 amino acid sequences with V and J gene calls (AIRR), genomic region ids (chr10_100050979_100052296), PDB chain and residue numbers, and analyst-chosen cell-type labels. Documented problems: case-sensitive exact match on import; symbol ambiguity; Excel date conversion; many-to-one mappings creating duplicate edges; isoforms not represented (CausalPath); species mix; plant ids from different genome releases, and annotation available only through an ortholog in Arabidopsis or rice (5.17).

### 6.4 Graph properties

- STRING: undirected, probability-like confidence (not strength), seven evidence sub-scores. BioGRID/IntAct: multigraph, one row per experiment.
- AP-MS: bait -> prey plus undirected STRING edges; edge provenance. Pathways: directed, signed, typed, fixed curated layout.
- WGCNA and correlation: complete, weighted, sparsified by threshold or top-k. Cell-cell communication: directed, weighted, multi-layer on 10-30 nodes, self-loops.
- Single-cell kNN: near-uniform degree with a coarse PAGA graph. Molecular networks: mutual top-K cosine, thousands of small components, mass differences on edges.
- Regulons: bipartite signed. eGRN: tripartite TF -> region -> gene. Per-cell-type GRNs: a collection of directed signed graphs over one gene set.
- Residue networks: typed multigraph with 3D node positions and per-frame edge frequencies. ceRNA: tripartite. Metabolic: bipartite, reversible, currency hubs. OmicsNet: up to six node types (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9252810).
- Immune repertoires: edit-distance graphs on short strings, thousands of small clusters, sample and subject as node attributes; edges exist only between sequences of the same or nearly the same length.
- Spatial cell graphs: planar or near-planar (Delaunay) or near-uniform degree (kNN), node positions fixed by the image, one graph per section, a categorical label per node; statistics are per label pair, not per node.
- Time courses are node attribute vectors, not dynamic graphs (Dictys's trajectory GRN is the exception).

## 7. Analysis methods

### 7.1 What they run

- Clustering: MCL, MCODE, Louvain and Leiden at several resolutions; WGCNA hierarchical clustering with Dynamic Tree Cut; Girvan-Newman on residue networks. MCL is what the tutorials teach: stringApp's "Cluster network (MCL)" button with granularity (the MCL inflation value) 5 in the Jensen Lab disease tutorial (https://jensenlab.org/training/stringapp/diseases), while clusterMaker2 suggests about 2.0, with 1.8 to 2.5 reasonable (https://rbvi.ucsf.edu/cytoscape/clusterMaker2). clusterMaker2 also offers MCODE, Leiden, Infomap, Fast Greedy, Label Propagation and Multilevel. MCODE grows clusters from high-scoring seed nodes with Degree Cutoff 2, Node Score Cutoff 0.2, Haircut on, Fluff off, K-Core 2 and Max. Depth from Seed 100, and leaves unscored nodes unclustered (https://baderlab.org/Software/MCODE/UsersManual), so it is not a partition. graphty-element ships MCL (Inflation, default 2), Louvain, Leiden, label propagation, hierarchical, spectral and Girvan-Newman clustering; it lacks MCODE, Infomap, Walktrap, fast-greedy and Dynamic Tree Cut (Designloom capabilities community-detection and dense-region-detection).
- Hub ranking: cytoHubba's 11 methods, top-10 convention; within-module connectivity and kME; per-cluster degree, betweenness and eigenvector centrality compared across clusters (CellOracle).
- Enrichment: over-representation with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR; GSEA; ClueGO; region enrichment on a layout (SAFE); chemical class per family (MolNetEnhancer).
- Propagation: GeneMANIA (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4168749); annotation propagation within a component (NAP); in silico knockout by signal propagation through a GRN (CellOracle).
- Graph construction: kNN from PCA; mutual top-K cosine with a component cap (GNPS); coarse abstraction (PAGA); contact networks from 3D distances across trajectory frames.
- Directed and signed: k-shortest paths, ILP (CARNIVAL), causal reasoning with permutation FDR, TF activity (decoupler), Boolean simulation; suboptimal paths between two residues.
- Communication: permutation tests per ligand-receptor pair; consensus ranking (LIANA). Regulatory inference: GRNBoost2, motif pruning, regularized regression per cluster.
- Flux balance analysis and knockouts (COBRApy, Escher-FBA).

### 7.2 How they interpret

Cluster = complex or pathway, named by its top term. Hub = essential or driver gene. Module eigengene correlated with a trait = the module "drives" the trait. Thick edge between cell types = strong communication. A TF central in one cluster's GRN but not another's = a cell-type driver. A molecular family = related compounds, so a library hit names its neighbors. A residue on many suboptimal paths = an allosteric relay. Edge = "these work together" (STRING) or "A activates B" (signaling); users conflate the two, and STRING says the score "does not imply that the interaction is strong" (https://string-db.org/help/scores/).

### 7.3 Validation habits

Cutoffs come from protocol defaults more than sensitivity analysis. Background: genome by default. Bench validation of a few hits. Rarely: cutoff sweeps, database swaps, method consensus (LIANA), clustering trees, degree-preserving randomization; CellOracle shows a randomized-GRN control beside every simulation.

### 7.4 Known biases

PPI degree tracks how often a protein was studied (https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fgene.2015.00260/pdf), and study bias alone can produce power-law degree distributions (https://elifesciences.org/articles/99951); higher STRING thresholds favor well-studied proteins. Ligand-receptor resources cover pathways unevenly (LIANA). DepMap co-essentiality networks carry a mitochondrial bias (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10632734). "Hub" often means "famous". Kinase-substrate resources cover fewer than 5% of known phosphosites and favor well-studied kinases, so kinase activity scores exist only for kinases with enough measured known substrates (5.2).

## 8. Outputs and collaboration

- **Figures**: networks with legends; node donuts; pathway and flux maps; circle and chord plots; UMAP and PAGA; per-cluster centrality plots; molecular families; residue networks over a structure. Ten Simple Rules advises matrices for dense networks and avoiding unjustified 3D (PMC6762067).
- **Tables**: node tables with computed columns, module membership, hub ranks, ligand-receptor tables, per-cluster GRN edge lists, library hits.
- **Sessions**: .cys, .rds, .h5ad, CellOracle HDF5, GNPS job links, NDEx links.
- **Reproducibility reality**: cytoHubba rankings reported to change between runs; stringApp drops older STRING versions; KEGG maps change; GNPS reruns reshape families. Scripts plus NDEx are the strongest practice.
