Ten inputs, each with one meaning. Grab, release, twist, flip and stretch are physical acts that mean what they mean on a real object; poke is the only "button". A quick pinch is a poke and a held pinch is a grab, so a far grab starts about a fifth of a second after the pinch closes.

**Freeze and undo.** History is the list of freezes, shown as lines of sand in the hourglass.

- **Freeze** (press the hourglass cap, or X): commits the current arrangement as a new line and holds it still. The next change you make starts time again from that state. Saving and leaving VR freeze first, so nothing uncommitted is lost.
- **Undo** (flip the hourglass, or Y) goes back one step. If you have changed anything since the last freeze, a flip discards those changes and returns to the last freeze. If nothing has changed, a flip unfreezes the last freeze: everything it hid comes back as a dimmed preview and time runs, so you can adjust and freeze again.
- **Redo and jump** (poke the hourglass glass): a strip of every freeze unrolls, newest at the top, with undone steps shown pale; poke any line to return to it. A pale line stays until you freeze something new.

### The journey

**1. Enter**

- **What you do:** On the 2D page, press Enter VR.
- **What you see:** The 16 Florentine families float at chest height about 75 cm ahead, still settling under the default force layout. When they come to rest, the hourglass at the rack's near end fills one line of sand: the opening state is frozen as the first entry in history, so there is always something to go back to. The rack sits low on your dominant side.
- **Controls:** none in VR.

**2. Get oriented**

- **What you do:** Push the graph sideways with an open palm to spin it a half turn; grab it to stop it; grab it with both hands and pull apart to make it about twice as large; carry it a little closer.
- **What you see:** The graph turns with momentum and slows under friction. You count 16 families and 20 marriage ties; Pucci floats alone, attached to nothing.
- **Controls:** push, grab (catch and carry), stretch.

**3. Find the Medici**

- **What you do:** Grab the blank text magnet from the rack; its text field opens. Say "Medici" (or type it on the floating keyboard), and set the magnet down just outside the graph. Then poke the Medici node.
- **What you see:** The Medici node slides out of the graph toward the magnet, trailing a short streak, and stops at the magnet; its marriage ties stretch behind it. Poking it opens its readout: name, 6 marriages, its attributes, and that the text magnet is moving it. You lift the magnet back to the rack and the Medici slide home.
- **Controls:** grab, speak, release (place), poke, lift out.

**4. Who they married into**

- **What you do:** Stretch an empty bin's mouth to about a hand's width and leave it beside the graph. Grab the Medici node and draw it slowly out of the graph, then carry it over the bin and release.
- **What you see:** While you hold it, the edges behave as springs that weaken with each hop: the six families married to the Medici (Acciaiuoli, Albizzi, Barbadori, Ridolfi, Salviati, Tornabuoni) hang close beneath it, families two marriages away barely lean, the rest stay put. Released over the bin, the Medici and every family hanging within the bin's mouth drop through it. Each node springs back to its place in the graph wearing a ring in the bin's color and leaves a labeled bead in the bin; the bin reads 7. Had a second-hop family slipped in, you would grab its bead and throw it out.
- **Controls:** stretch (the bin's mouth), grab, carry, release into a bin.

**5. Who matters most (PageRank)**

- **What you do:** Grab the centrality well from the rack. Its label reads PageRank (poke the label to pick another centrality member). Set it down just below the graph.
- **What you see:** As the well nears the graph its field lines reach up into the graph and a tick marks the moment it comes into reach. On release, PageRank runs and every node is pulled toward the funnel in proportion to its score, with short trails: the Medici travel furthest, Pucci barely moves. A PageRank magnet and a PageRank lamp appear on the rack.
- **Controls:** grab, poke (optional), release (place).

**6. Read the result**

- **What you do:** Grab the well and twist it clockwise to strengthen its pull, release it slowly where it is, then poke it.
- **What you see:** A stronger pull spreads the families down the funnel in score order, highest deepest. The readout lists the ranking: Medici about 0.144, Guadagni about 0.097, Strozzi about 0.087, Albizzi about 0.078, Tornabuoni about 0.071, down to Pucci about 0.010. Poking any node now also shows its PageRank and which laws are moving it.
- **Controls:** grab, twist, release, poke.

**7. Color and size by PageRank**

- **What you do:** Grab the new PageRank lamp from the rack and set it above the graph; carry it higher until its cone covers every family; twist it to widen the size range; release.
- **What you see:** The cone's edge is visible as field lines. Nodes inside it take a color ramp and swell by PageRank; the Medici are largest and warmest. Lowering the lamp narrows the cone to fewer families; raising it again covers all. Once released, the lamp stays where it is, so your arm can come down.
- **Controls:** grab, carry (reach), twist (strength), release (place).

**8. Keep only strong families (filter)**

- **What you do:** Grab the well's rim and slide it up until the rim reads 0.070 (it steps in detents of 0.005; poke the rim to type or say an exact value), then press the hourglass cap.
- **What you see:** As the rim rises, families whose score clears it drop into the funnel; the others drift outward and dim. The rim shows "0.070 -- 5 kept" (Medici, Guadagni, Strozzi, Albizzi, Tornabuoni) and names the nearest family below it (Ridolfi, 0.069), so you see who just missed. The press flashes the scene once, time stops, the eleven dimmed families are hidden, and the hourglass gains a second line of sand. The arrangement, the lamp's style and the filter are now committed.
- **Controls:** grab the rim and slide, press the hourglass cap (freeze).

**9. Write a note**

- **What you do:** Pull a note slip off the pad; its text field opens. Say "Medici married across factions -- compare with Strozzi" (or type it), and drop the slip onto the Medici node.
- **What you see:** The slip sticks to the Medici and follows the node wherever it moves. Poking it reopens the text.
- **Controls:** grab, speak, drop onto.

**10. Undo a mistake**

- **What you do:** Reaching for the note pad, you knocked the rim up to 0.09 and pressed freeze; only the Medici and Guadagni remain. Turn the hourglass over, slide the rim back to 0.070, and press the cap again.
- **What you see:** Nothing had changed since that freeze, so the flip unfreezes it: time runs again, Strozzi, Albizzi and Tornabuoni reappear dimmed just outside the rim, and the note is still on the Medici (it was written before the mistaken freeze). Lowering the rim drops them back in. The new freeze replaces the mistaken one, whose pale line disappears from the history strip. For a mistake since the last freeze (the wrong lamp, the wrong magnet), lift the object out and the nodes relax back, or flip once to return to the last freeze.
- **Controls:** flip the hourglass (undo), grab the rim and slide, press the cap (freeze); lift out for one unfrozen object.

**11. Save**

- **What you do:** Press the lid of the specimen jar. The first time, a name field opens: say "Florentine marriages, PageRank" or type it.
- **What you see:** If anything has changed since the last freeze, the jar freezes first (one flash). The jar shows the project name and the number of freezes it holds. The saved project keeps the history, every field object as its layout, run, filter or style layer, the bin as a group, the note, and the simulation's seed.
- **Controls:** poke (the lid), speak.

### Advanced journeys

- **Compound rule (PageRank above 0.07 AND wealth above 50, OR in the Medici bin):** hang a second well under the first well's drain and drop the wealth magnet into its drain; only families that fall through both are kept (AND). A well set beside the first keeps what falls into either (OR). Flip a well to make it a hill that pushes matches out (NOT). Drop the Medici bin into the first well's funnel and its members count as kept regardless. The structure of the rule is visible as the stacking of the wells, and poking the bottom well's readout spells the rule out as text.
- **Tuning algorithm options:** poke the well and choose Options; each option grows a peg on the rim (damping, iterations, weight attribute). Twisting a peg changes the value, the algorithm reruns at each detent, and you watch the families slide as damping moves from 0.85 to 0.5. That makes sensitivity visible: a family that barely moves is robust to the option.
- **Comparing two runs:** set a PageRank well on one side of the graph and a betweenness well on the other. Each node settles between them in proportion to its two scores, a tug of war in the spirit of Dust and Magnet: families strong on both hang in the middle close to the line between the wells, families strong on one lean to its side. Freeze, then lift one well out and in again to replay the difference. For the same algorithm with two settings, set two PageRank wells with different damping pegs.
- **Layers on subsets:** drop the PageRank lamp onto the Medici bin and only the bin's members are styled. Or stand a fence through the graph and hang a lamp on one side: the fence stops its cone. Each lamp is its own style layer; lamps stack in the order you hung them, and the lamp's readout moves it up or down the order. Lifting one out removes its layer.
- **Recipes:** throw the field objects of a finished, frozen arrangement (the well with its rim setting, the lamp) into an empty bin. A bin of field objects is a recipe: it goes on the rack and stays there across projects, and dropping it onto any graph places the same objects in the same relative positions and runs them.
- **Joining tables:** stays on the 2D page. A join has no physical meaning and no useful motion; forcing it into a field object would hide what it does.

### How it scales

- **60+ algorithm catalog:** the rack holds one well per family (about eight); the drawer lists every algorithm grouped by family with a search field (dictation or keyboard), and an algorithm pulled from the drawer is a well with that member already set. Algorithms whose result is not a per-node score get a different shape of the same idea: communities become several small basins that pull each community together, a shortest path becomes a cord stretched between two nodes that the path's nodes slide onto, connected components drift apart.
- **New object types:** anything with a numeric value per node becomes a magnet and a lamp; per-edge values set the edges' spring stiffness. An object type with no quantity (a note, a project, a saved view) gets a passive object that sticks or holds but exerts no force. Every object, passive or not, gets a readout listing its verbs.
- **Plugins:** a plugin algorithm that declares a numeric per-node output gets a well in its family's drawer section automatically, with option pegs generated from its declared options. A plugin layout is a magnet-like law if it can run as forces; a one-shot layout is placed as a "settle" weight that pulls nodes toward its computed positions, so it still blends with other laws.
- **The limit:** motion stops being readable well before the simulation stops being computable. Trails and individual travel are useful up to roughly a few hundred visible nodes; beyond that the simulation runs on the GPU and the display shows density flow rather than nodes. More than three or four live laws at once produce arrangements nobody can explain; anything that is configuration rather than a quantity (data import, joins, schema edits, export settings) stays on the 2D page.

### Why it should work

- **Dust and Magnet (Yi, Melton, Stasko and Jacko, 2005)** showed that attribute magnets pulling data points by value are learnable by people with no visualization training, and that moving magnets is a natural way to compare attributes.
- **Kinetica (Rzeszotarski and Kittur, CHI 2014)** used physics on a tablet (magnets, sifters, barriers) for multivariate data and reported that people explored more of the data than with a spreadsheet.
- **BumpTop (Agarawala and Balakrishnan, CHI 2006)** showed tossing and piling with physics as a usable way to organize documents; throwing into a bin is the same act.
- **VR physics games** (Half-Life 2's gravity gun, Half-Life: Alyx's gravity gloves, Job Simulator, Boneworks) made grab, throw and catch the most practiced interactions in VR; players need no tutorial for them, and gravity gloves are the model for the far grab here.
- **Force-directed layout is already a simulation** that people watch settle; the layout package in this repository ships steppable ForceAtlas2 and Fruchterman-Reingold simulations, and webgpu-graph-algorithms runs ForceAtlas2 on the GPU, so adding forces is an extension of what already runs, not a new engine.
- **Motion is a strong perceptual signal:** the eye picks out the one node that moves among still ones faster than a color difference, so watching a result arrive should make it easier to notice than recoloring alone.
- **An explicit freeze** gives the live simulation a clear unit of history, where an always-live mix has no natural point to undo to; Superhot VR shows that people readily grasp time as something they control.

### What it would teach us

- Does watching nodes move under a law explain a result better than seeing a color appear? Do people remember who moved?
- Is the explicit freeze welcome (a feeling of control, a clean undo) or a chore people forget, leaving long stretches uncommitted?
- Do people understand the two kinds of undo step (discard changes since the last freeze; unfreeze the last freeze), or do they expect every flip to do the same thing?
- How accurate is throwing into a bin with hands versus controllers, and how much catch assistance does it need before it stops feeling physical?
- Does a rim on a funnel make thresholds easier to choose, because you see who is just below it?
- How many laws can someone stack before they can no longer predict or explain the arrangement?
- Does an inertial graph help orientation (a sense of weight and place) or cause discomfort compared with a graph that moves only while held?
- Is twist-for-strength discovered without being told, and is flip-for-reverse understood the same way on a magnet, a well, a bin and the hourglass?
- Do positions produced by forces mislead people about the data (reading closeness as similarity when it is an artifact of several laws at once)?

### Known risks

- **Superposition is hard to read:** with several laws, a node's position has several causes. Mitigation: poking a node lists the laws acting on it and their share; each object's field lines have their own hue and a label; a soft cap warns past four live laws.
- **Position is only an approximate encoding:** forces settle at equilibria that bend values. Mitigation: exact numbers are always one poke away (node readout, well ranking); freeze captures the exact state so it does not keep shifting while you read.
- **Non-determinism:** the same objects could settle differently on another run. Mitigation: the simulation takes a seed and the project stores it; freezes store positions, not just laws.
- **Throws miss:** Mitigation: bins have a catch radius that grows with throw speed, a near miss slides in, and carrying and releasing over the bin always works.
- **Releases that throw by accident:** a hand-tracked pinch release jitters. Mitigation: a release counts as a throw only above about 1 m/s; anything slower places.
- **Grabbing the wrong thing in a dense graph:** a node and the graph share space. Mitigation: the target highlights before the pinch closes (outlines on pinch on Vision Pro), a slow release of the wrong grab changes nothing, and stretching the graph spreads its nodes apart.
- **Motion sickness from a drifting graph:** Mitigation: only the graph moves, never the viewer; strong friction and a cap on spin speed; the tether returns it softly; pushing can be switched off for a held-only graph; a reduce-motion setting replaces travel with a short fade to the new positions (this gives up the motion feedback, so readouts carry more of the load).
- **Arm fatigue:** holding a lamp above the graph or twisting at chest height tires the shoulder. Mitigation: every object stays where it is released; far grab moves objects without reaching; twist ratchets by regrabbing; the rack sits at elbow height; the graph can be carried closer and lower.
- **Seated and one-handed use:** Mitigation: the rack sits in front at elbow height, not beside the hip; far grab and far poke reach everything; every two-handed verb is also a button in the object's readout.
- **Accidental pushes and twists while reaching:** Mitigation: a push needs an open flat palm moving faster than a threshold and is ignored while anything is held; twist clicks in detents and only applies while an object with a dial is held.
- **Undo for unfrozen work is coarse:** between freezes, one flip discards every change since the last freeze, not just the latest one. Mitigation: lifting out a single object undoes just that law; the discarded changes stay as a pale line in the history strip, so the flip itself can be redone. Untested risk: people may need to freeze more often than they want to get fine-grained undo.
- **No haptics on hands:** Mitigation: audible detent clicks and the field-line density stand in for rumble.
- **Dictation is uneven across browsers:** Mitigation: voice only fills text fields, and the floating keyboard always works.
- **Large graphs:** live motion of thousands of nodes is unreadable and costly. Mitigation: the GPU simulation, trails only on the nodes inside a law's reach, and density display past a few hundred visible nodes.
- **Not everything is a force:** algorithms with structural results and configuration tasks fit badly. Mitigation: basins, cords and drift for the common structural results; everything else stays on the 2D page rather than being forced into a metaphor.
